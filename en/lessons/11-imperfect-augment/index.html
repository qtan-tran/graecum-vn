Goals for this lesson: you can (1) explain the imperfect – the narrative tense that views a past action "from within", as if it were still unfolding; (2) apply the augment of both kinds: adding ἐ- before a consonant and lengthening the initial vowel (temporal augment); (3) decline the imperfect active and middle/passive in all six persons, deponents included; (4) decline εἰμί in the past (ἦν, ἦσαν…); (5) recognize and use time expressions: τότε, πρότερον, ἔτι, οὐκέτι, ἀεί and the chronographic formula ἐπ' ἄρχοντος + genitive; (6) tell the dative of time ("when?") from the accusative of extent ("how long?"): τῇ νυκτί "at night" ↔ τὴν νύκτα "all night".

1. From present to past: the same view "from within"

So far every story of ours has unfolded in the present: ὁ παῖς παιδεύεται "the child is being educated". The 1st person plural middle/passive you met in Lesson 10 (παιδευόμεθα) also views the action as in progress. A Greek tense bundles into itself the aspect – the way of looking at an action: still unfolding or already complete.

The imperfect takes exactly the "still unfolding, not yet framed" view of the present, but anchors it to a point in the past. Picture the timeline:

TenseThe viewVisualExample
Presentunfolding as it is being spoken of──●──▶ (now)ὁ παῖς παιδεύεται – the child is being educated
Imperfectunfolding at a point in the past, with no claim about the outcome──▶●──▶ (back then)ὁ παῖς ἐπαιδεύετο – the child (back then) was being educated

The imperfect does three main jobs in narration:

  1. Sets the scene for the story: "it was getting dark, the wind was blowing…" – then the new event walks in.
  2. Habit / repetition in the past: "every day he used to go to the market".
  3. Progress interrupted: "he was speaking when someone knocked at the door" – the imperfect frame waits for the event (we will meet the "event" tense in the next lesson).

Latin corner: the same need, two strategies. Latin builds its imperfect by inserting the tense sign -bā- into the stem: amā-ba-m "I was loving", monē-ba-t "he was advising". Greek instead attaches the prefix ἐ- to the front of the word and swaps the set of endings. The verb "to be" lines up on both sides like a diagram: Latin eram, erās, erat ↔ Greek ἦν, ἦς, ἦν (section 4). Both systems are children of the same Indo-European mother – each child simply chose its own path.

2. Augment: the key that unlocks the past

The augment is the syllable ἐ- attached to the front of a verb to mark the past tenses of the indicative. Meet a verb opening with ἐ- and you are almost certainly standing in the past. There are two kinds:

Type 1: the syllabic augment

A verb beginning with a consonant adds the whole syllable ἐ- (smooth breathing, no accent):

PresentPastHow to read it
παιδεύω (paideúō) I educateἐπαίδευον (epaídeuon) I was educating (and: they were)ἐ- + παιδεύ- + ον
λύω (lúō) I loosen, releaseἔλυον (élyon) I was loosening (and: they were)ἐ + λυ-; the accent pulls back onto the first syllable: ἔ-

A small detail: the augment ἐ- carries no accent of its own; the opening syllable takes the grave (ἔλυον) and the word's accent settles back as usual (ἐπαίδευον – the acute sits on the second syllable).

Type 2: the temporal augment

A verb beginning with a short vowel adds no syllable at all; instead it lengthens the initial vowel. This is the most easily missed disguise:

Initial vowelBecomesExample: present → past
αηἀκούω "I hear" → ἤκουον (ékouon) "I was hearing"
εηἐθέλω "I want" (Lesson 2) → ἤθελον (éthelon)
οωὁρίζω "I define, determine" → ὥριζον (hórizon)
αιῃαἰσθάνομαι "I perceive" (Lesson 10) → ᾐσθανόμην (ēisthanómen) – iota subscript under the η
οιῳοἴομαι "I suppose" → ᾤμην (ōimen)
ειῃmet less often; remembering the lengthening principle is enough

How to self-check the temporal augment: look at the accent + breathing mark on the first syllable. ἤκουον carries the rough breathing on η – because the opening syllable was originally ἀ- with rough breathing; the augment lengthens the vowel but preserves the type of breathing mark. If you meet a word opening with η/ω/ῃ/ῳ and the dictionary lookup fails: try running the rule in reverse (η→α or ε, ω→ο, ᾐ→αι…) and look again – section 6 drills exactly this skill.

Three special cases

1. Verbs beginning with ῥ: after the augment, ρ doubles and the rough breathing changes to a smooth breathing: ῥάπτω (rháptō, rough breathing) "I sew" → ἐρράπτον (errápton) "I was sewing". Meet a doubled ρ after ἐ- wearing a smooth breathing: that is an augment, not a misprint.

2. The grandfather of all exceptions: ἔχω "I have". The imperfect is not ἐ-εχον but εἶχον (eîkhon) "I was having (and: they were)". The augment is ει-, because the verb's root anciently began with ϝ (digamma, Lesson 8): ϝεχ- → ἐ + ϝε- yields ει-. This verb is far too common to dodge: learn εἶχον by heart as a special item, and remember the promise made back in Lesson 1 (ἔχει "he has") – today we pay the debt.

3. Verbs beginning with εὐ-: they usually lengthen to ηὐ- (like an ordinary temporal augment, except that υ is already long and stays put): εὑρίσκω heurískō "I find" → past ηὕρισκον hḗriskon. You will meet this very cluster ηὐ- again in εὗρον "found" – the aorist of the same verb (Lesson 13). A few words keep εὐ- without augment – the dictionary states each one.

Epic poetry: Homer often omits the augment – the same past appears with ἐ- in one passage and without it in another, as the verse requires. So when reading the Iliad/Odyssey and you meet a past verb "bare" of its ἐ-: your eyes are not failing you, it is poetic practice. (Herodotos, the author of today's Reading I, also occasionally keeps that old habit.)

The augment in compound verbs

A compound verb = prepositional prefix + simple verb. Because the prefix is glued onto the verb as a chunk of meaning, the augment slots in between the prefix and the stem, not at the front of the word. Four rules for handling the prefix:

  • Position: προκρίνω "I prefer" → προέκρινεν (προ- + ἐ + κριν-; exactly the form in today's Reading II); εἰσάγω "I lead in" → εἰσῆγον; προστάττω "I command" → προσέταττον.
  • A prefix ending in a vowel loses its final vowel (elision, Lesson 3) before the augment, like the two examples εἰσ-/προσ- just given – except περι- and προ-, which never elide: περιβάλλω "I throw around" → περιέβαλλον; and προ- fuses with the augment (προ + ἐ- → προὐ-, crasis – precisely the προὔτεινεν of Reading I).
  • A prefix ending in a consonant undoes its assimilation (it returns to its original shape): συλλαμβάνω "I seize" (συν- + λαμβάνω, ν assimilated to λ) → past συνελάμβανον – the ν "comes back to life" before the augment.
  • The prefix ἐκ- → ἐξ- before the augment (exactly the rule ἐκ → ἐξ before a vowel, Lesson 2): ἐκβάλλω "I throw out" → ἐξέβαλλον.

Careful – many compound verbs do not wear their compound on their sleeve: the English meaning looks like a simple verb, but the prefix is still there and the augment still slots into the middle: ἀποκρίνομαι "answer" (deponent, Lesson 10) → ἀπεκρινόμην; ἀποθνήσκω "die" → ἀπέθνησκον. Recognize them by their prefix (ἀπο-, ἐπ-, συν-…), not by their meaning. The meaning system of the prefixes will get a lesson of its own.

3. The imperfect active: six persons, the παιδεύω model

The formula: augment + present stem + thematic vowel + secondary endings. The "secondary" set is the set of endings specialized for the past tenses of the indicative:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
1 Sg.ἐπαίδευονepaídeuonI was educating
2 Sg.ἐπαίδευεςepaídeuesyou were educating
3 Sg.ἐπαίδευε(ν)epaídeue(n)he/she was educating

Short model λύω: ἔλυον, ἔλυες, ἔλυε(ν), ἐλύομεν, ἐλύετε, ἔλυον(ν). The mark (ν) is the movable ν (Lesson 10): it appears before a vowel, e.g. ἐπαίδευον ἐν τῇ πόλει… but ἐπαίδευε ὁ παῖς "the child was educating" needs none.

Trap no. 1 of the whole lesson: 1 Sg. = 3 Pl. ἐπαίδευον is both "I was educating" and "they were educating" – the consequence of the -ον ending doing double duty. Three lifebuoys:

  1. An explicit subject pronoun: ἐγὼ ἐπαίδευον = I; οἱ πολῖται ἐπαίδευον = the citizens (plural).
  2. A plural article + noun, not a pronoun: οἱ παῖδες ἐπαίδευον "the children were educating".
  3. Narrative context: the subject has already been introduced, the verb merely carries on.

The imperfect exists only in the indicative mood – there is no imperfect imperative and no imperfect infinitive. This is no omission: the imperfect is a purely narrative tense, while commands always "live in the present" (or use the aorist system, Lesson 13) and the other non-personal moods belong to Lessons 16–17. The practical consequence: do not hunt for past-tense command forms in the dictionary – you will not find them; and an infinitive -ειν / -εσθαι met in a text certainly belongs to the present.

4. The imperfect middle/passive & deponents + the past of εἰμί (ἦν)

Middle/passive

Same formula: augment + present stem + the secondary middle/passive endings. Compare with Lessons 5/10:

Present m/p (Lesson 5)Imperfect m/pπαιδεύω in the imperfect m/p
1 Sg.-ομαι-όμηνἐπαιδευόμην epaideuómen
2 Sg.-ῃ-ουἐπαιδεύου epaideúou
3 Sg.-εται-ετοἐπαιδεύετο epaideúeto
1 Pl.-όμεθα-όμεθαἐπαιδευόμεθα epaideuómetha
2 Pl.-εσθε-εσθεἐπαιδεύεσθε epaideúesthe
3 Pl.-ονται-οντοἐπαιδεύοντο epaideúonto

The 2 Sg. changes "face" completely: the present contracts to -ῃ (βούλῃ, Lesson 5), but the imperfect contracts in another direction: -εσο → -ου. Compare the two "twins": the deponent βούλομαι, present βούλῃ "you want" ↔ past ἐβούλου "you were wanting". Confusing -ῃ/-ου is confusing the tenses: check with the augment – with an augment it is past, so the ending must be -ου.

Deponents: the augment enters them as normal

βούλομαι "want" (Lesson 5) in the imperfect, all six persons – learn this paradigm together with the table above, because Reading I uses ἐβούλετο in its very opening sentence:

ἐβουλόμην (eboulómen), ἐβούλου (eboúlou), ἐβούλετο (eboúleto), ἐβουλόμεθα (eboulómetha), ἐβούλεσθε (eboúlesthe), ἐβούλοντο (eboúlonto).

A pair that combines two skills of this lesson: αἰσθάνομαι (deponent, Lesson 10) + temporal augment → ᾐσθανόμην "I was perceiving" (ᾐ = alpha + iota under the η – the same submerged-iota family you met in the terminology of Lesson 2). And the passive: πέμπω (Lesson 4) in the past m/p gives ἐπεμπόμην "I was being sent" (1 Sg.) and ἐπέμπετο "he was being sent" (3 Sg.) – augment + present m/p stem, endings exactly as in the table above.

Three points worth gazing at: (1) 1 Sg. = 3 Sg. – the same "double-duty" trap as ἐπαίδευον (1 = 3 Pl.); context and pronouns untie it; (2) poetry has a variant 2 Sg. ἦσθα – meet it in Homer without panic; (3) two real historical examples to anchor the forms: ὁ Κροῖσος ἦν βασιλεὺς τῶν Λυδῶν "Croesus was king of the Lydians", ὁ Κῦρος ἦν βασιλεὺς τῶν Περσῶν "Cyrus was king of the Persians" (ὁ Λυδός "a Lydian", ὁ Πέρσης "a Persian", βασιλεύς "king" – the proper-name set of today's two readings).

5. Time expressions: τότε, ἔτι, οὐκέτι… and the chronographic formula ἐπ' ἄρχοντος

Narrating the past needs temporal addresses. Nearly half of today's vocabulary is timeline words. Learn them as opposing pairs:

GreekTransliterationMeaningWhere it appears in today's readings
τότεtótethen, at that timethe anchor that opens a past story
πρότερονpróteronbefore, formerlyεἶπον μὲν καὶ πρότερόν τοι – Herodotos 1,207
πάλαιpálailong agostresses the distance in time
ἔτιétistill, yetthe action had not yet stopped at the past point
οὐκέτιoukétino longer= οὐκ + ἔτι; the state had already ended
ἀείaeíalways, foreverHerodotos writes αἰεί (Ionic): αἰεὶ τοὺς αὐτοὺς εὐτυχέειν

Position: time adverbs usually stand at the very front of the sentence, right after τότε/δὲ if there is one – that is the "index board" of a narrative sentence. τότε ὁ Κῦρος ἐβούλετο… = "at that time Cyrus was wanting…". In English translation, keeping them at the front of the sentence is the most natural choice.

Time nouns: the dative of time & the accusative of extent

Adverbs tell time with words; Greek also tells time with the case of the noun itself, without any preposition. The two main usages answer two different questions – learn them as one pair (a third one, the genitive of time, sits at the bottom of the table):

To translate a dative of time, add a fitting preposition (at / in / on – Greek writes no preposition); to translate an accusative of extent, use "for…" or "all…". The four time nouns of the table: ἡ ἡμέρα hē hēméra (day), ὁ χρόνος ho chrónos (time), ὁ ἐνιαυτός ho eniautós (year), ἡ νύξ, νυκτός hē nýx, nyktós (night, third declension with the stem νυκτ-); plus two adjectives μακρός makrós (long) and πρῶτος prṓtos (first); the numerals πέντε, δύο from Lesson 8. Full vocabulary in section 9.

The bottom row is a third case that likewise needs no preposition: the genitive of time, answering within what time? – καὶ ἡμέρας καὶ νυκτός kaì hēméras kaì nyktós "by day and by night", χειμῶνος cheimṓnos "in winter". It is rarer than the two cases above, mostly nested in "parallel" contrasts (day and night alike) and in the chronographic formula just below; a full map of the genitive's functions waits in Supplementary Lesson 09b.

Careful – changing the case changes the question:

GreekQuestionEnglish
τῇ νυκτί (dative)when?at night (when?)
τὴν νύκτα (accusative)how long?all night (how long?)

Likewise: τῇ ἡμέρᾳ "on that day" contrasts with τὴν ἡμέραν "all day long". Self-check: ask when? or how long? – whichever makes the meaning flow is the case to choose.

A 3-step recognition pattern: (1) a noun in the dative or accusative that follows no preposition and is not governed by the verb; (2) ask when? / how long? – one of the two makes the meaning flow; (3) the noun is a time word (day, night, year…) → it is one of the two usages. With the imperfect, the accusative of extent fits especially well: ἐπαίδευον ἡμέρας τρεῖς epaídeyon hēméras treîs – "for three days I kept on teaching" (τρεῖς from Lesson 8, ἐπαίδευον from section 3).

Careful – only time nouns may be read this way: any other dative is usually the dative of means (Lesson 4): τῷ λόγῳ tō lógō – "by word", for λόγος is no time word! And if a phrase already has its preposition written out (ἐν τῷδε τῷ ἐνιαυτῷ – "in this year", ὅδε from Lesson 6) then simply translate according to the preposition: it is not one of the two new usages.

Quick self-check

(1) τῇ νυκτί – when? or how long? Translate. (2) ἡμέρας πέντε – ? Translate. (3) "for two years" – which case? Write the phrase. (4) τῷ πρώτῳ χρόνῳ – ? Translate.

(1) When? (a point in time) – "at night". (2) How long? – "five days"; the numeral πέντε standing after its noun is still correct. (3) The accusative of extent: δύο ἐνιαυτούς (or ἐνιαυτοὺς δύο). (4) When? – "at first".

Latin corner: "when?" Latin assigns to the ablative: eōdem diē "on that same day" ↔ ταύτῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ; and "how long?" both families answer with the accusative: decem annōs "for ten years" ↔ ἐνιαυτοὺς δέκα. If you already know Latin, you only need to swap Greek dative ↔ Latin ablative for the point-in-time question.

The chronographic formula: ἐπ' ἄρχοντος + genitive

The Greeks did not say "in the year 445 BC". Athens dated its years by the name of the head of the office, the archon (ὁ ἄρχων): "under the archonship of X". The formula is stamped thousands of times across the histories:

ἐπ' ἄρχοντος + name (genitive) = "under the archonship of…"

A real example from Diodorus (used continuously throughout his Library of History): ἐπ' ἄρχοντος γὰρ Ἀθήνησι Λυσιμαχίδου… "under the archonship of Lysimachides at Athens…". Notice two details already learned: ἐπ' is ἐπί elided before a vowel (elision, Lesson 3); Λυσιμαχίδου is a genitive – the "preposition + genitive" formula is a fixed template (learned back in the earliest lessons).
How the two readings relate: Herodotos tells his Persian-Lydian story (6th century BC) without this formula (an Athenian chronographic habit), while Diodorus – a chronicler in the true sense – opens each dated section with it.

Section takeaway: one timeline, three cases. Dative = when? (point in time); accusative = how long? (extent); genitive = within what time? (see the last row of the table) plus its fixed role in the chronographic formula ἐπ' ἄρχοντος you just met. Meet a time noun, try these three questions before reaching for the dictionary.

Latin corner: Latin has a completely parallel formula: years are named after the two consuls in office, X et Y consulibus (ablative absolute: "with X and Y as consuls"). Both civilizations "named" the year after a magistracy with a fixed case formula, instead of a modern year number.

6. Reading strategy: "peeling off" the augment to use the dictionary

The Greek dictionary lists verbs in the 1st person present. But in past-tense text, verbs wear the augment – looking up ἐπαίδευε comes back empty-handed. The four-step "peeling" skill:

  1. Look at the first syllable: does the word open with ἐ- (smooth breathing, no accent)? → peel the ἐ- off.
  2. Does it open with an unusually long η/ω/ῃ/ῳ/ᾐ/ᾠ? → reverse the temporal augment: η→α/ε, ω→ο, ᾐ→αι, ᾠ→οι.
  3. Turn the ending back to the present (if needed): -ον/-ες/-ε/-ομεν/-ετε/-ον is the past set – once peeled, the stem usually shows its dictionary face.
  4. Look it up, then reassemble the meaning: present stem + "was …-ing (in the past)".
Form you meetHow to peel itDictionary target
ἐπαιδεύετοpeel off ἐ-; the ending -ετο = 3 Sg. m/p pastπαιδεύω – "he was being educated"
ἤκουονη → ἀ (keep the rough breathing!)ἀκούω – "I was hearing (and: they were)"
ᾐσθανόμηνᾐ → αι, the iota subscript surfacesαἰσθάνομαι – deponent, "I was perceiving"
εἶχονει- is this word's special augmentἔχω – "I was having (and: they were)"

Compound verbs while "peeling the augment" (full rules in section 2): if you see the augment "stranded mid-word" – like προέκρινεν (προ- + ἐ + κριν-, the exact form in Reading II) – it is still an augment: peel as usual, then look up προκρίνω. The meaning system of the prefixes will get a lesson of its own.

7. Reading I: The wheel of fortune (Herodotos 1,207)

Context: Herodotos, "the father of history", recounts: Cyrus, king of the Persians, wanted to swallow the land of the Massagetae (οἱ Μασσαγέται). Among his advisers stood Croesus – the king of Lydia who had lost his own kingdom to this very Cyrus (that story is at 1,86–91; today we read his famous advice). Note the pun history plays: the defeated is advising the victor on the theme of "the wheel of fortune".

Sentence 1

ὁ Κῦρος ἐβούλετο τὴν ἀρχὴν τῶν Μασσαγετῶν λαμβάνειν.

Dissection: (1) ἐβούλετο = augment ἐ- + the deponent βούλομαι "want" (Lesson 5), 3 Sg.: "he was wanting" – an imperfect to open with: the frame of the whole story. (2) τὴν ἀρχὴν accusative (object) – "rule, empire"; τῶν Μασσαγετῶν a genitive plural after the noun. (3) λαμβάνειν the present active infinitive (-ειν, Lesson 2) "to take, seize" – after βούλομαι the infinitive stands bare (the object + infinitive pattern you know well).
Translation: "Cyrus was wanting to seize the rule of the Massagetae."

Sentence 2

ὁ Κροῖσος ὁ Λυδὸς ἔλεγεν πρὸς τὸν Κῦρον·

Dissection: (1) ἔλεγεν = augment + λέγω (Lesson 4), 3 Sg. imperfect: "he was saying" – exactly the "scene-setting" meaning: it opens the speech. (2) The repeated-article noun ὁ Κροῖσος ὁ Λυδὸς "Croesus, the Lydian" – a pattern met in earlier readings. (3) πρὸς + accusative "to someone" (familiar). The colon introduces direct speech.
Translation: "Croesus the Lydian was saying to Cyrus:"

Sentence 3 (advice, present tense kept as in the original)

ἄνθρωπος εἶ, καὶ ἀνθρώπων ἄρχεις.

Dissection: (1) εἶ "you are" – present of εἰμί (Lesson 6). (2) ἄρχεις 2 Sg. present of the new verb ἄρχω "to rule" + genitive: ἀνθρώπων "of (mortal) men".
Translation: "You are a (mortal) man, and you rule (mortal) men."
Point to ponder: the speech keeps the present although the narrative frame is past – a universal truth (gnomic present) needs no augment. Greek splits the labor cleanly: the imperfect tells the story, the present tells the truth.

Sentence 4 (the heart of the passage)

ὁ κύκλος τῶν ἀνθρωπηΐων πρηγμάτων περιφέρεται καὶ οὐκ ἐᾷ τοὺς αὐτοὺς αἰεὶ εὐτυχέειν.

Dissection: (1) ὁ κύκλος "wheel, circle" (a word still alive today: cyclone, cycle); τῶν ἀνθρωπηΐων πρηγμάτων genitive – "of human affairs". (2) περιφέρεται "turns around" (περί + φέρω). (3) ἐᾷ 3 Sg. of ἐάω "to allow"; τοὺς αὐτοὺς accusative "those same men". (4) αἰεὶ = ἀεί "always"; εὐτυχέειν infinitive "to be fortunate" (root εὐ- like the εὖ of Lesson 9!).
Translation: "The wheel of human affairs turns around, and it does not allow those same men to be fortunate forever."

Sentence 5

ὁ Κῦρος δὲ οὐκ ἐπείθετο.

Dissection: ἐπείθετο = augment + πείθομαι (Lesson 2: πείθω "persuade"; m/p "obey"), 3 Sg. imperfect: "he was not obeying". δὲ a light contrast. A very Greek way to end: the silence does the narrating.
Translation: "Cyrus, however, was not obeying." – (The history continues: Cyrus crossed the river and attacked, and fate answered with its very own wheel: 1,214.)

Original text (Herodot 1,207): εἰ δ' ἔγνωκας ὅτι ἄνθρωπος καὶ σὺ εἶς καὶ ἑτέρων τοιῶνδε ἄρχεις, ἐκεῖνο πρῶτον μάθε, ὡς κύκλος τῶν ἀνθρωπηΐων ἐστὶ πρηγμάτων, περιφερόμενος δὲ οὐκ ἐᾷ αἰεὶ τοὺς αὐτοὺς εὐτυχέειν. – "If you know that you too are a mortal, ruling over others of just such a kind, then learn this first: there is a circle of human affairs, and as it revolves it does not allow the same men to be fortunate always."
The Ionic dialect: πρηγμάτων (Attic: πραγμάτων), εὐτυχέειν (Attic: εὐτυχεῖν), αἰεί (Attic: ἀεί). Herodotos writes in the Ionic dialect – you have just read the West's first historiography.

8. Reading II: Charondas and the art of letters (Diodoros 12,13)

Context: Diodoros of Sicily (1st century BC) wrote the Bibliotheca Historica – the "Library of History" – a synthesis of the whole ancient world. Section 12,13 belongs to the chapter on Charondas (ὁ Χαρώνδας), the lawgiver of Thurii (αἱ Θούριοι, an Athenian colony in what is now southern Italy): a magistrate linked to a public education policy remarkably early for the histories.

Sentence 1

ὁ Χαρώνδας ἦν νομοθέτης ἐν ταῖς Θουρίοις.

Dissection: (1) ἦν – 3 Sg. imperfect of εἰμί (section 4): "he was". (2) νομοθέτης "lawgiver" = νόμος (Lesson 8) + τιθέναι "to place" – the one "who places laws". (3) ἐν ταῖς Θουρίοις – a plural place name + plural article in the dative: exactly the ἐν ταῖς Ἀθήναις pattern dissected in Lesson 10 (a plural noun as a "collection of villages").
Translation: "Charondas was a lawgiver in Thurii."

Sentence 2

ἐνομοθέτει πάντας τοὺς πολίτας γράμματα μανθάνειν.

Dissection: (1) ἐνομοθέτει 3 Sg. imperfect "he was enacting" – from ἐνομοθετέω "to enact into law"; the ending -ει (instead of -ε) marks a contract verb in -έω – Lesson 12 will teach the contraction; today you only need "augment + -ει = 3 Sg. past". (2) πάντας τοὺς πολίτας accusative plural. (3) γράμματα accusative – "letters, writing" (plural of τὸ γράμμα "letter of the alphabet"); μανθάνειν infinitive "to learn" – an irregular verb (learn its face for now, the exercises do not ask you to decline it).
Translation: "He was enacting that all citizens must learn their letters."

Sentence 3

ἡ πόλις ἐχορήγει τοὺς μισθοὺς τοῖς διδασκάλοις.

Dissection: (1) ἐχορήγει 3 Sg. imperfect of χορηγέω "to furnish, pay for" (also a contract verb in -έω, ending -ει) – "the city was paying". (2) τοὺς μισθούς "wages" (ὁ μισθός, a new word of this passage alone) – accusative. (3) τοῖς διδασκάλοις dative plural "for the teachers" – dative = "for whom" (familiar from earlier lessons).
Translation: "The city was paying the wages of the teachers." – free public education, drawn in the imperfect: a policy in steady operation, not a one-off act.

Sentence 4

ὁ νομοθέτης τὰ γράμματα προέκρινεν.

Dissection: (1) προέκρινεν 3 Sg. imperfect of προκρίνω "to prefer" – a compound verb, so the augment ἐ- slots in between the prefix προ- and the stem κριν- (section 6!). (2) τὰ γράμματα accusative "the art of letters".
Translation: "The lawgiver was preferring the art of letters (to every other subject)." – Diodoros goes on: in his view, "even more than power".

Sentence 5 (the reason, present kept)

διὰ τῶν γραμμάτων οἱ νεκροὶ ἔτι ζῶσιν ἐν τῇ μνήμῃ τῶν ζώντων.

Dissection: (1) διὰ + genitive "through, thanks to" (met in earlier lessons). (2) οἱ νεκροὶ "the dead" ↔ οἱ ζῶντες "the living" – a contrasting pair; ζῶσιν 3 Pl. present of ζάω "to live" (infinitive ζῆν – met again in sentence 6). (3) ἔτι "still" + ἐν τῇ μνήμῃ dative "in the memory".
Translation: "Thanks to letters, the dead still live in the memory of the living." – gnomic present: a truth carries no augment.

Sentence 6 (the closing aphorism)

τὸ μὲν ζῆν ἡ φύσις αἰτία, τὸ δὲ εὖ ζῆν ἡ τῶν γραμμάτων παιδεία.

Dissection: (1) τὸ ζῆν = article + infinitive, substantivized: "living" (the Lesson 9 pattern: the article turns anything into a noun). (2) μὲν… δὲ contrasts the two halves. (3) ἡ φύσις αἰτία "nature is the cause" – αἰτία "cause" (the root of the word "aetiology", borrowed via Latin); (4) εὖ ζῆν "to live well" – the irregular adverb εὖ from Lesson 9 stands before the infinitive as an adverb of manner; ἡ τῶν γραμμάτων παιδεία "the education of letters" – a genitive between nouns.
Translation: "Living is nature's doing; living well is the doing of the education that letters give."

Original text (Diodor 12,13): ἐνομοθέτησε γὰρ τῶν πολιτῶν τοὺς ἅπαντας μανθάνειν γράμματα, χορηγούσης τῆς πόλεως τοὺς μισθοὺς τοῖς διδασκάλοις… διὸ καὶ τοῦ μὲν ζῆν τὴν φύσιν αἰτίαν ὑποληπτέον, τοῦ δὲ καλῶς ζῆν τὴν ἐκ τῶν γραμμάτων συγκειμένην παιδείαν. – "For he enacted that all the citizens learn their letters, the city paying the wages of the teachers… Hence: of living, nature is to be judged the cause; of living well, the education composed of letters." (The original uses the aorist ἐνομοθέτησε – the "event" tense of the next lesson; the passage we read has been turned into the imperfect to match today's lesson.)
Style: Diodoros writes post-classical Koine, long sentences bundling many subordinate clauses; today's reading keeps his grammatical skeleton and trims the clauses.

11. Exercises

Answers appear only when printing (Ctrl+P → Save as PDF) – finish everything first.

Exercise 1 – Decline the imperfect paradigm

Build the full six-person past indicative paradigm of παιδεύω (active) and ἀποκρίνομαι (deponent, Lesson 10), with transliteration for the 2nd person singular.

Answers 1

παιδεύω active: ἐπαίδευον, ἐπαίδευες (epaídeues), ἐπαίδευε(ν), ἐπαιδεύομεν, ἐπαιδεύετε, ἐπαίδευον(ν) – 1 Sg. = 3 Pl. as learned. ἀποκρίνομαι deponent: ἀπεκρινόμην, ἀπεκρίνου (apékrinou), ἀπεκρίνετο, ἀπεκρινόμεθα, ἀπεκρίνεσθε, ἀπεκρίνοντο – endings exactly as in the m/p table; the augment ἐ- slots between the prefix ἀπο- and the stem.

Exercise 2 – Add the augment

Given ten present-tense verbs, write the 1st person singular imperfect (active; with a deponent the middle/passive system is of course used):

ἀκούω, ἐλπίζω, ὁρίζω, λύω, πείθω, ἐθέλω, ῥάπτω, αἰσθάνομαι, οἴομαι, ἔχω

Answers 2

ἤκουον (α→η, keep the rough breathing); ἤλπιζον (ε→η); ὥριζον (ο→ω); ἔλυον (the syllable ἐ-); ἔπειθον (ἐ- + πείθ-, the accent falls on the augment syllable itself); ἤθελον (ε→η); ἐρράπτον (ἐ- + doubled ρ, smooth breathing); ᾐσθανόμην (deponent: m/p endings, αι→ᾐ with iota subscript); ᾤμην (deponent, οι→ᾠ); εἶχον (the special augment ει- – the big trap of the lesson).

Exercise 3 – Peel the augment, use the dictionary

Each of the following forms occurs in a text. Write the dictionary form (1st person present) + the probable meaning:

ἤκουον, ἐλύετο, ἐβούλετο, ἐπαιδεύομεν, ᾐσθανόμην, ἦσαν, προέκρινεν, εἶχον, ἐρράπτον, ἤθελον

Answers 3

ἀκούω hear; λύω loosen/release (m/p 3 Sg. "was being loosened"); βούλομαι want (deponent); παιδεύω educate (1 Pl. "we were educating"); αἰσθάνομαι perceive (deponent, 1 Sg.); εἰμί be (3 Pl. "they were"); προκρίνω prefer (compound verb – augment in the middle); ἔχω have (the augment ει-); ῥάπτω sew (doubled ρ); ἐθέλω want.

Exercise 4 – Present or past?

Fill in the correct form of the verb in brackets, choosing present or imperfect according to the time keyword:

  1. τότε ὁ Κῦρος ______ (βούλομαι) τὴν ἀρχὴν λαμβάνειν.
  2. νῦν οἱ πολῖται ______ (μανθάνω) γράμματα.
  3. πρότερον οἱ ἄνθρωποι οὐκ ______ (εἰμί) ἐν ταῖς Θουρίοις.
  4. ὁ Χαρώνδας ______ (ἐνομοθετέω) νέους νόμους· ἔτι δὲ οἱ νόμοι ______ (ζάω "to live"; hint: 3rd person plural).

Answers 4

(1) ἐβούλετο – τότε + past narration = imperfect. (2) μανθάνουσι(ν) – νῦν "now" = present, 3 Pl. (3) ἦσαν – πρότερον + οὐκ = the imperfect of εἰμί, 3 Pl. (4) ἐνομοθέτει – past narration (contract verb -έω, ending -ει); ἔτι δὲ οἱ νόμοι ζῶσιν "and the laws still live" – ἔτι does not decline, it is an adverb riding with a present.

Exercise 5 – Translate into Greek

Translate (use the vocabulary of the two readings; imperfect indicative):

  1. "Cyrus was king of the Persians."
  2. "Croesus said to Cyrus: you too are a man."
  3. "At that time the city was still paying the wages of the teachers."

Answers 5

(1) ὁ Κῦρος ἦν βασιλεὺς τῶν Περσῶν – ἦν 3 Sg. (1 = 3 Sg.); genitive after the noun. (2) ὁ Κροῖσος ἔλεγεν πρὸς τὸν Κῦρον· ἄνθρωπος εἶ – imperfect frame + present inside the speech; the original text uses εἶς ("you too"). (3) τότε ἡ πόλις ἔτι ἐχορήγει τοὺς μισθοὺς τοῖς διδασκάλοις – τότε and ἔτι both stand at the front; imperfect because the policy was in operation.

Exercise 6 – Cumulative passage (review of Lessons 4–11)

τότε ὁ Κῦρος ἐβούλετο τὴν ἀρχὴν τῶν Μασσαγετῶν λαμβάνειν. ὁ Κροῖσος δὲ ἔλεγεν· "ἄνθρωπος εἶ. ὁ κύκλος τῶν ἀνθρωπηΐων πρηγμάτων περιφέρεται." ὁ δὲ Κῦρος οὐκ ἐπείθετο, καὶ διὰ τοῦ ποταμοῦ ἐπορεύετο.

(Gloss for the passage: ὁ ποταμός "river"; πορεύομαι deponent "to march, go".)

(a) Identify all imperfect verbs in the passage and give person + number. (b) Why does the sentence inside the speech use the present? (c) Find one time adverb and one prepositional phrase of route in the passage. (d) ἐπορεύετο (πορεύομαι "to march, go") – peel the augment and give person, number, tense.

Answers 6

(a) ἐβούλετο (deponent βούλομαι, 3 Sg.), ἔλεγεν (λέγω, 3 Sg.), ἐπείθετο (deponent πείθομαι, 3 Sg.), ἐπορεύετο (deponent πορεύομαι, 3 Sg.) – the narrative simply advances in the imperfect. (b) The speech is a universal truth (gnomic present) – it carries no augment, as analyzed in Reading I. (c) Time adverb: τότε (sentence-initial); prepositional phrase of route: διὰ τοῦ ποταμοῦ (διὰ + genitive: "through the river"). (d) ἐπορεύετο = ἐ- + πορεύομαι: 3 Sg. imperfect m/p of the deponent "to march" – "he was marching".
