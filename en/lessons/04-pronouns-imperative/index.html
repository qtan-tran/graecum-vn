1. Personal pronouns: two sets of forms – emphatic & enclitic

Personal pronouns stand in for a known person or thing when there is no need to name it with a noun. Greek has the 1st and 2nd persons (the 3rd person comes later – Greek has no personal pronoun "he/she/it" the way English does; that role is played by the demonstrative pronouns, Lesson 6).

Compare French: Le chien poursuit le chat. Elle est pourtant plus rapide que lui – "elle/lui" stand in for "le chat/le chien". Greek pronouns do exactly that job, but with a feature French only hints at: each case has two sets of forms.

1.1 The paradigm: 1st and 2nd person

The characteristic feature of Greek: in the 1st and 2nd person, each case has two forms: the emphatic (full) form and the enclitic form – the "leaning" form, which has no accent of its own and leans on the word before it.

1.2 The rule for choosing which form

  • The nominative is always the full form and is used only when the person needs emphasis: ἐγὼ λέγω egṑ légō "I (myself) am the one speaking" (contrast), unlike plain λέγω, which needs no pronoun – the verb ending already gives the person.
  • The other emphatic forms (ἐμοῦ, ἐμοί, ἐμέ…) are likewise used only for emphasis, e.g. for contrast ("me, not you"), or, very commonly: after prepositions.
  • Otherwise: use the enclitic form wherever one exists. The 1st and 2nd person plural have no enclitic forms, so the full form is always used there.

Common mistake: writing ὁ φίλος ἐμοῦ when there is no emphatic purpose – the default is ὁ φίλος μου ho phílos mou (my friend). Self-test: does the sentence contrast anyone with anyone? Does the pronoun follow a preposition? Both answers "no" → use the enclitic (μου, μοι, με, σου, σοι, σε).

Accent note: because the enclitic "leans" on the previous word, that word may have to take an extra acute: ὁ ἄνθρωπός σου ho ánthrōpós sou (your human being) – ἄνθρωπος takes an extra acute on its final syllable. But ὁ φίλος μου stays unchanged (its accent is already in place). You do not need the detailed rule yet – just train your eye to recognize it in texts.

Pattern to reuse: the pair "emphatic / leaning" is exactly the French pair moi, toi (emphatic) / me, te (attached): ἐμοῦ / μου ≈ de moi / de me. Memory trick for the emphatic forms: take the enclitic and put ἐ- in front (μου → ἐμοῦ, μοι → ἐμοί, με → ἐμέ); σύ works the same way (σου → σοῦ…). The plural pair ἡμεῖς / ὑμεῖς differs by a single letter – exactly like French nous / vous, and it is the classic copying error: η = "we", υ = "you (pl.)".

Historical connection: ἐγώ shares its root with Latin ego, English I, German ich (Indo-European root *egh₂); σύ belongs with Latin tu, French tu. The "n" dropped out in the Western European languages, while Greek kept it – so the Greek form looks "longer" but is actually the most archaic one.

Self-test pattern (three questions): when you meet a genitive, ask what it depends on:

  • It depends on a verb (that verb demands it) → genitive object → translate it as the verb's object.
  • It depends on a preposition → translate it together with the preposition as one unit.
  • It modifies a head noun standing before it → genitive attribute → only now ask further: "of whom?" or "among which?".

These three questions handle almost every genitive you will meet; only the "elegant translation" step needs extra finesse.

2.2 Genitive object

Some verbs demand their object in the genitive. Vocabulary and governing case form one unit: when you learn a verb, always learn "which case it takes".

Common mistake: the Greek case often does not match the translation! English says "accuse someone" with a plain object, but κατηγορέω demands the genitive: οἱ ἄνθρωποι τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγοροῦσιν. That is why vocabulary notebooks record it as κατηγορέω + Gen. – the italics underline the "unexpected case". Translating in the reverse direction is where errors happen: do not "cling" to your native language's structure.

GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἱ ἄνθρωποι τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγοροῦσιν.hoi ánthrōpoi toû philosophou katēgoroûsin.The people accuse the philosopher.

Morphology note: κατηγορέω is a contract verb: stem κατηγορε- + ending -ουσι(ν) contract into κατηγοροῦσιν – the root of the international word "category/catēgoría" (from "accuse" → "assign to a class").

2.3 Possessive genitive

  • It is an attribute (remember Lesson 1: attributive position); it expresses belonging, possession.
  • Check question: whose?
  • Translation: English uses 's / of.
GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἱ τῶν φιλοσόφων λόγοιhoi tôn philosophōn lógoithe words of the philosophers

Check: whose? – of the philosophers ✓ → possessive.

2.4 Partitive genitive

  • It is an attribute; it expresses a part – whole relation.
  • Check question: a part of what?
  • Translation: English "of / among".
GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἱ σοφοὶ ἀνθρώπωνhoi sophoì anthrṓpōnthe wise among men

Check: the wise are a part of what? – of men ✓ → partitive. Tell-tale sign: after quantity words like οὐδείς "no one", πάντες "all", πολλοί "many"… the genitive is almost always partitive.

2.5 Genitive pronouns: two special ways to translate

Genitive pronouns very often carry the functions 2.3–2.4, and then deserve their own translation style:

FunctionTranslationExampleTransliterationEnglish
Possessivelike a possessive pronoun (my, our…)ὁ φίλος ἡμῶνho phílos hēmônour friend
Partitive"among"οὐδεὶς ἡμῶνoudeìs hēmônnone of us

Self-test: does ἡμῶν follow a head noun and answer whose? → translate "our"; does it answer among whom? (usually after a quantity word like οὐδείς) → translate "of us / among us". The same word ἡμῶν, two different "jobs" – the function decides the translation, not the form.

Pattern to remember: the 2 Pl. imperative = the 2 Pl. indicative – the forms are identical: νομίζετε is both "you (pl) believe" and "believe (you all)!", and only context (and the exclamation mark) tells them apart. 4. Negation: οὐ or μή?

Greek has two negation words, and the choice depends on the communicative function of the sentence, not on its "position":

  • οὐ, οὐκ, οὐχ ou, ouk, ouch – negates a fact, a statement: used in declarative sentences (indicative mood).
  • μή mḗ – negates a prohibition, a request, a wish: used with the imperative and (usually) with the infinitive.
GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἱ ἄνθρωποι οὐ κατηγοροῦσιν.hoi ánthrōpoi ou katēgoroûsin.The people do not accuse.
μὴ νομίζετε!mḕ nomízete!Do not believe!
μὴ πέμπετε τῷ βασιλεῖ ἄγγελον!mḕ pémpete tôi basileî ággelon!Do not send the king a messenger!

Self-test when translating into Greek: stating a fact → οὐ; prohibiting / requesting / wishing / negating an infinitive → μή. The three variants οὐ / οὐκ / οὐχ depend on the following sound: οὐκ before a vowel (as learned in Lesson 2: οὐκ αἰσθητόν), οὐχ before a vowel with rough breathing – you will meet these again in texts; just get used to reading them.

Latin corner: Latin splits negation along the same lines: declarative sentences use nōn (nōn timet "he is not afraid"), while prohibitive / wishing sentences use nē + subjunctive – the classic pattern in Latin grammar is nē timuerīs "do not be afraid". The pair οὐ/μή ≈ nōn/nē: one Indo-European source, two sister languages dividing "saying no to a fact" from "saying no to the will".

5.2 a) Indirect object

When a verb has a "double object" (send something to someone): the recipient = dative, the thing = accusative – this map matches English "send someone something".

GreekTransliterationEnglish
τῷ βασιλεῖ ἄγγελον πέμπουσιν.tôi basileî ággelon pémpousin.They send the king a messenger.

Check: send to whom? – the king (dative) ✓; send what? – a messenger (accusative) ✓.

5.3 b) Dative of possession

εἶναι can stand with a subject and a dative "of the owner"; the natural translation is then belong to / have / own:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
Τῷ Διὶ ὁ οὐρανὸς καὶ ἡ γῆ εἰσίν.Tôi Dií ho ouranòs kaì hē gê eisín.The sky and the earth belong to Zeus.

Translation trap: if you choose the rendering "Zeus has the sky and the earth", you must swap the roles: the dative of possession becomes the subject of your English sentence, and the original subject becomes the object. This kind of "sentence rebuilding" is a favorite exam task: always ask who is the owner? before building your target sentence.

5.4 c) Other dative objects: learned verb by verb

Verbs that demand the dative do not necessarily match the English translation – once again "vocabulary + case = one unit" (like κατηγορέω + [Gen.] in section 2.2):

GreekTransliterationEnglishNote
τοῖς θεοῖς πιστεύει.toîs theoîs pisteúei.He trusts the gods.English uses a plain object; Greek: + Dat.
τῷ ὄχλῳ χαλεπαίνουσιν.tôi óchlōi chalepaínousin.They are angry at the crowd.English uses the preposition at; Greek: + Dat.

5.5 The dative after prepositions: σύν, ἐν – and "dual-case prepositions"

Each preposition governs a fixed case – learning a preposition = learning (preposition + case + meaning) as one three-column unit. Today: σύν + [Dat.] sýn "(together) with" (the root of English syn-: synergy, synthesis); ἐν + [Dat.] has been familiar since Lesson 1.

Dual-case prepositions – same preposition, different case → different meaning:

  • μετά + [Acc.] "after (in time/counting)" – but μετά + [Gen.] "(together) with, among"
  • πρός + [Acc.] "toward, against" – but πρός + [Dat.] "at, in addition to"

Self-test: a familiar preposition with a "strange case" → its meaning has certainly changed! Conversely, when a translation looks "paradoxical" → check the case of the following noun.

5.6 c) The adverbial dative – means (dative of means)

First, re-anchor a basic concept: an adverbial is a sentence element expressing a circumstance (place, time, manner, means…) of the event; it is not demanded by the verb – cut it away and the sentence still "works".

The "cutting experiment" (English): in the sentence "Yesterday I gave my charming friend at school the teacher's book." – cut "yesterday", "at school" (adverbials), "charming", "the teacher's" (attributes) → "I gave my friend the book": still grammatical, still complete. But cut the subject, the predicate or the objects (give whom what) and the sentence "breaks".

Cannot be cutCan usually be cut
subject · predicate · objects demanded by the verbattributes (modifying nouns) · adverbials (modifying the event)

Do not confuse: an adverb is a word class (Lesson 3: ἀγαθῶς…), while an adverbial is a sentence function – an adverb performs the adverbial function, but an adverbial can also be performed by a prepositional phrase or a noun (which is what happens today!). The same "word class vs sentence function" distinction as in Lesson 1: a noun can be subject or object.

Dative of means: an adverbial dative expressing the instrument / means – check question: with what?

GreekTransliterationEnglish
τοῖς λίθοις βάλλομεν.toîs líthois bálomen.We throw with stones.

Check: throw with what? – with stones ✓; the verb βάλλω does not "demand" this dative (cut it away and the throwing still happens) → adverbial of means, not an object.

Self-test pattern for datives (3 questions, in order): (1) Does it follow a preposition? → translate it together with the preposition as one unit. (2) Does the verb demand it (can you answer "to/for whom"?) → dative object. (3) Does it answer "with what?" and depend on no preposition → dative of means (adverbial). The dative of time and the other kinds follow later.

6. The accusative-with-infinitive (AcI): first steps

6.1 From the "that"-clause to the AcI

Consider: λέγεις, ὅτι οἱ Ἀθηναῖοι τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγοροῦσιν. Légeis, hóti hoi Athēnaîoi toû philosophou katēgoroûsin. – "You say that the Athenians accuse the philosopher."

Greek can also "package" the same content in a completely different way – compare the two architectures:

Structure A ("that"-clause)Structure B (AcI)
Greekλέγεις, ὅτι οἱ Ἀθηναῖοι τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγοροῦσιν.λέγεις τοὺς Ἀθηναίους τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγορεῖν.
TransliterationLégeis, hóti hoi Athēnaîoi toû philosophou katēgoroûsin.Légeis toùs Athēnaíous toû philosophou katēgoreîn.
Structureframe predicate + the conjunction ὅτι + nominative subject + genitive object + finite verbframe predicate, no conjunction + subject becomes accusative + genitive object + infinitive
Translationidentical: "You say that the Athenians accuse the philosopher."

AcI = accusativus cum infinitivo "accusative with infinitive": the subject of the embedded event becomes an accusative (accusative-subject), the predicate becomes an infinitive (infinitive-predicate), and there is no conjunction. The AcI is not an independent clause but is "embedded" in the frame sentence; when translating, you rebuild it as a "that"-clause.

6.2 Anatomy of an AcI

ComponentGreekTransliterationRoleRole in the "that"-clause when translating
Trigger verbλέγειςlégeispredicate of the frame sentence"you say"
Accusative-subjectτοὺς Ἀθηναίουςtoùs Athēnaíous"subject" of the AcI"that the Athenians"
Genitive objectτοῦ φιλοσόφουtoû philosophouobject of the infinitive"accuse the philosopher"
Infinitive-predicateκατηγορεῖνkatēgoreîn"predicate" of the AcIpredicate of the "that"-clause

The term infinitive-predicate helps you (1) distinguish it from subject/object infinitives (Lesson 2) – because not every infinitive belongs to an AcI, and (2) remember that when translating, this infinitive must become the predicate of the "that"-clause.

6.3 Seven steps for translating an AcI

  1. Identify the trigger verb (the frame sentence – see section 6.4).
  2. Identify the accusative-subject and the infinitive-predicate (a "floating" accusative right before the infinitive).
  3. Translate the trigger verb (e.g. λέγομεν → "we say").
  4. Insert the word "that".
  5. Translate the accusative-subject as the subject of the "that"-clause.
  6. Translate the infinitive-predicate as the predicate of the "that"-clause (inflect it by the tense of the frame sentence).
  7. Translate any remaining objects and adverbials of the infinitive (if present).

6.4 Which verbs trigger an AcI?

An AcI always needs a trigger verb – not every verb can do it. Three familiar groups at level I:

  1. Verbs of thought & will: νομίζω (believe), ἐλπίζω (hope), ἐθέλω (want).
  2. Impersonal verbs: δεῖ (it is necessary).
  3. Verbs of speech: λέγω (say).

Unlike Latin! After verbs of perception ("see, hear…") Greek does not use an AcI; Latin does (video eum currere "I see him running"). Greek expresses perception with participles – Lesson 19. For now: when you meet an accusative + infinitive, first look for a trigger verb of groups 1–3; if there is none → suspect a different construction.

6.5 Special case: εἶναι with a "double accusative"

If the infinitive-predicate is εἶναι and the original sentence has two nominatives (subject + predicative noun), then when embedded as an AcI both become accusatives:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἱ ἄνθρωποι λέγουσι τὸν φίλον σοφὸν εἶναι.hoi ánthrōpoi lègousin tòn phílon sophòn eînai.People say that the friend is wise.

The underlying structure: ὁ φίλος σοφὸς ἐστίν. Ho phílos sophòs estín. "The friend is wise" – two nominatives. In the AcI: τὸν φίλον σοφὸν εἶναι – two accusatives. Pattern: "the whole embedded clause rotates one full turn": nominative → accusative, finite verb → infinitive.

Related pattern: this construction is the "treasurer" of European academic prose: Latin brought it into written style (puto te sapientem esse "I think you wise" ≈ νομίζω σε σοφὸν εἶναι), and modern English still keeps its trace in formal English: "I believe the gods to be wise" – accusative + infinitive, exactly! When you meet an English sentence of that kind, you have already translated it into a Greek AcI.

English (close translation)
Do not delay to philosophize, my friend; for we must philosophize. Do not believe the gods to be evil; for the gods are blessed. Trust the gods. Death is nothing to us.

Quick analysis (check with the tools of this lesson):

  • μὴ μέλλε φιλοσοφεῖν: negated imperative → μὴ (section 4); 2 Sg. imperative μέλλ-ε (section 3); φιλοσοφεῖν is an "object" infinitive of μέλλω (Lesson 2).
  • δεῖ γὰρ ἡμᾶς φιλοσοφεῖν: γάρ postpositive – second slot (Lesson 1); δεῖ is an AcI trigger of group 2: accusative-subject ἡμᾶς + infinitive-predicate φιλοσοφεῖν (section 6) – "it is necessary [that] we…".
  • μὴ νόμιζε τοὺς θεοὺς κακοὺς εἶναι: AcI with double accusative (section 6.5): τοὺς θεοὺς + κακοὺς + εἶναι; 2 Sg. imperative νόμιζε with its receding accent.
  • τοῖς θεοῖς πίστευε: dative object (section 5.4 – πιστεύω + [Dat.]) + 2 Sg. imperative.
  • ὁ θάνατος οὐδὲν ἡμῖν ἐστίν: ἡμῖν is a dative of the pronoun (a relative of the dative of possession from Lesson 1) = "to us"; οὐδὲν a neuter singular predicative noun; ἐστίν oxytone at the sentence end → acute.

Reading comprehension questions: (1) Find all the imperatives in the text – which person/number, and why do their negations use μή rather than οὐ? (2) δεῖ γὰρ ἡμᾶς φιλοσοφεῖν is an AcI triggered by which verb – identify the accusative-subject and the infinitive-predicate, then translate it as a "that"-clause. (3) Which case is ἡμῖν in the last sentence, and how does the English translation handle it? Compared with Τῷ Διὶ ὁ οὐρανὸς καὶ ἡ γῆ εἰσίν (section 5.3): in what respect do the two sentences use the dative alike, and in what respect differently?

Answers to the reading questions (print to check)

1. Three 2 Sg. imperatives: μὴ μέλλε, μὴ νόμιζε (negated) and πίστευε (not negated) – the two negated imperatives both take μὴ because they negate a request (a command), not a fact. 2. Trigger: δεῖ (impersonal verb); accusative-subject = ἡμᾶς, infinitive-predicate = φιλοσοφεῖν → "it is necessary [that] we philosophize". 3. ἡμῖν is the dative of the pronoun "we"; the translation renders it as "to us". It resembles the Zeus sentence in that the dative stands next to εἶναι and is not the verb's object; it differs in function: Τῷ Διὶ is a dative of possession ("belongs to Zeus"), while ἡμῖν is a dative of reference/interest ("to us") – two siblings in the same "dative + εἶναι" family, which you will meet many more times.

9. Summary table of easily confused points

Confusable pair / groupCore differenceSelf-check
ἐμοῦ, ἐμοί, ἐμέ vs μου, μοι, μεemphatic = contrast / after prepositions; enclitic = defaultIs there a "not…, but…" contrast? Is there a preposition before? No → enclitic.
ἡμεῖς, ἡμῶν, ἡμῖν, ἡμᾶς vs ὑμεῖς, ὑμῶν, ὑμῖν, ὑμᾶςη = "we", υ = "you (pl.)" (like French nous/vous)Underline the differing letter when reviewing; read aloud: ἡ- = we, ὑ- = you (pl.)
Possessive vs partitive attribute"whose?" → of…; "a part of what?" → among…After quantity words (οὐδείς, πολλοί, πάντες…) almost always partitive.
Genitive object vs genitive attributethe object is demanded by the verb; the attribute modifies a head nounAsk: does this genitive depend on the verb or on the noun?
οὐ vs μήοὐ negates facts (indicative); μή negates commands / requests / wishes / infinitivesLook at the mood: indicative → οὐ; imperative (and infinitive) → μή.
μετά + [Acc.] vs μετά + [Gen.]; πρός + [Acc.] vs πρός + [Dat.]same preposition, different case → different meaningAlways learn (preposition + case + meaning) as one unit; a "paradoxical" translation → re-check the case.
Dative object vs dative of meansthe object is demanded by the verb ("to whom?"); the means is an adverbial ("with what?") – not demandedTry cutting the dative: the sentence still "works" → means; the verb "breaks" → object.
Dative of possession vs dative objectwith εἶναι: the dative = the owner → "belong to / have"; with other verbs: recipient / targetThe main verb is εἶναι → always think of the dative of possession (Lesson 1).
Adverb vs adverbialadverb = a word class (Lesson 3); adverbial = a sentence function (performed by adverbs, prepositional phrases or nouns)Two questions: "which word class does this word belong to?" and "what does it do in the sentence?"
Infinitive in an AcI vs other infinitivesthe AcI's infinitive-predicate must become the predicate of the "that"-clause; not every infinitive belongs to an AcILook for a trigger verb + an accusative right before the infinitive. No trigger → not an AcI.
ὅτι-clause vs AcIὅτι keeps nominative + finite verb; the AcI turns them into accusative + infinitive, without a conjunctionA "floating" accusative + infinitive after a saying/thinking verb → rebuild the "that"-clause in 7 steps.
2 Pl. imperative vs 2 Pl. indicativeidentical forms (νομίζετε)Context + exclamation mark; only the 2 Sg. imperative (νόμιζε) has the distinctive -ε ending.

10. Exercises

Answers appear only when printing (Ctrl+P → Save as PDF) – work through them first, then print to check.

Exercise 1 – Personal pronouns

Fill in the correct pronoun form (choosing emphatic / enclitic where needed):

  1. Our friend: ὁ φίλος ___
  2. No one of you: οὐδεὶς ___
  3. They send us a messenger: ___ ἄγγελον πέμπουσιν.
  4. They accuse you – no emphasis: ___ κατηγοροῦσιν.

Answer 1

  1. ἡμῶν
  2. ὑμῶν
  3. ἡμῖν
  4. σε
    5. (the enclitic of the genitive, because κατηγορέω demands the Gen. – the lesson's trap: English "accuse you" looks like an accusative, but Greek needs the enclitic genitive σε, not the accusative σέ)

Exercise 2 – The function of the genitive

Determine: genitive object, possessive attribute or partitive attribute?

  1. οἱ τῶν Ἀθηναίων λόγοι
  2. οἱ σοφοὶ τῶν ἀνθρώπων
  3. οἱ ἄνθρωποι τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγοροῦσιν.

Answer 2

  1. Possessive attribute (the words of the Athenians – ask "whose?").
  2. Partitive attribute (the wise among men – ask "a part of what?").
  3. Genitive object (demanded by κατηγορέω: accuse whom).

Exercise 3 – Present active imperatives

Form the 2 Sg. and 2 Pl. imperatives of the verbs:

  1. πέμπω
  2. νομίζω
  3. βάλλω
  4. πιστεύω

Answer 3

  1. πέμπε! / πέμπετε!
  2. νόμιζε! / νομίζετε!
  3. βάλλε! / βάλλετε!
  4. πίστευε! / πιστεύετε!

The 2 Pl. always equals the indicative; the 2 Sg. = stem + -ε with a receding accent.

Exercise 4 – οὐ or μή?

  1. οἱ ἄνθρωποι ___ πιστεύουσιν. (The people do not believe.)
  2. ___ νομίζετε! (Do not believe!)
  3. ___ βάλλετε λίθους! (Do not throw stones!)
  4. ὁ φίλος ___ σοφὸς ἐστίν. (The friend is not wise.)
  5. οὐδεὶς ___ πέμπει ἄγγελον. (No one sends a messenger.)

Answer 4

  1. οὐ (indicative – negating a fact)
  2. μὴ (negated imperative)
  3. μὴ (negated imperative)
  4. οὐ (indicative)
  5. οὐ (indicative; note that οὐδείς already contains οὐ)

Exercise 5 – The function of the dative

Determine: indirect object · dative of possession · other dative object · dative after preposition · dative of means:

  1. τοῖς θεοῖς πιστεύομεν.
  2. τοῖς λίθοις βάλλομεν.
  3. τῷ βασιλεῖ ἄγγελον πέμπουσιν.
  4. ἐν τῇ γῇ ἐσμέν.
  5. Τῷ Διὶ ὁ οὐρανὸς καὶ ἡ γῆ εἰσίν.

Answer 5

  1. Other dative object (trust whom – πιστεύω + [Dat.]; English uses a plain object).
  2. Dative of means (adverbial: "with what?" – with stones; cut it away and the throwing still happens).
  3. Indirect object (the recipient; accompanied by the accusative object ἄγγελον).
  4. Dative after preposition (ἐν + [Dat.] – Lesson 1; ἐσμέν = "we are", 1 Pl. of εἶναι).
  5. Dative of possession (with εἶναι → "belong to / have").

Exercise 6 – AcI

  1. Translate and identify the accusative-subject + infinitive-predicate: λέγομεν τοὺς ἀνθρώπους σοφοὺς εἶναι.
  2. Convert the following ὅτι-clause into an AcI: λέγεις, ὅτι οἱ Ἀθηναῖοι τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγοροῦσιν.
  3. Translate into Greek (AcI): "We believe the gods to be wise."

Answer 6

  1. Accusative-subject = τοὺς ἀνθρώπους, infinitive-predicate = εἶναι (with σοφοὺς – the double accusative of section 6.5). – We say that the people are wise.
  2. λέγεις τοὺς Ἀθηναίους τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγορεῖν.
  3. νομίζομεν τοὺς θεοὺς σοφοὺς εἶναι.
    4. (νομίζω is a group 1 trigger verb; "the gods" → accusative, "wise" → accusative, "are" → infinitive εἶναι.)

Exercise 7 – Putting it all together (imperative + negation + dative + accusative)

Translate the following sentence, then "dissect" each component with the tools of this lesson:

μὴ πέμπετε τῷ βασιλεῖ ἄγγελον!

Answer 7

Do not send the king a messenger!

  • μὴ + πέμπετε: negated 2 Pl. imperative (imperative form = indicative 2 Pl.).
  • τῷ βασιλεῖ: indirect object – the recipient (dative).
  • ἄγγελον: object – the thing sent (accusative).
