Goals for this lesson: you can (1) conjugate the relative pronoun ὅς, ἥ, ὅ in full across gender–number–case and tell it apart from the article by its accent; (2) apply the double agreement rule: gender & number follow the antecedent, case follows the pronoun's own job inside the subordinate clause; (3) read complex sentences containing relative clauses with the "tongs" technique and recognize case attraction; (4) use the possessive pronouns ἐμός, σός in the right position and say "his/her" with the genitive of αὐτός; (5) keep the three uses of αὐτός apart (intensive – "the same" – 3rd-person pronoun) and distinguish ἑαυτοῦ "himself" from αὐτοῦ "his"; (6) read two original passages: Plato, Meno 72d–73c and Apollodorus 2,5,9.

1. The relative pronoun ὅς, ἥ, ὅ: the full paradigm

Since Lesson 6 you have known demonstrative pronouns, which "point a finger" at a thing. Today you meet the linking kind of pronoun: the relative pronoun does not point – it joins a subordinate clause onto a noun, like the cord from which a lantern hangs. English uses who, which, that (the source language of this course, Vietnamese, uses "mà / ai / cái / những điều mà…").

ὅς, ἥ, ὅ (hós, hé, hó) – "(the one) who/which…". The full paradigm:

SINGULARPLURAL
masc.fem.neut.masc.fem.neut.
Nominativeὅςἥὅοἵαἵἅ
Genitiveοὗἥςοὗὧνὧνὧν
Dativeᾧᾗᾧοἷςαἷςοἷς
Accusativeὅνἥνὅοὕςἅςἅ

Transliteration row by row: hós, hé, hó; hoû, hês, hoû; hô(i), hêi, hô(i); hón, hḗn, hó; hoí, haí, há; hôn; hoîs, haîs, hoîs; hoús, hás, há.

Three morphological observations:

  • Looks familiar? The whole singular is identical to the article – but with two small differences, both easy to check: it always carries an accent (ὅς, not the plain white τὸ like ὁ) and it always has the rough breathing (ὅς, ἥ, ὅ; the article only has the smooth breathing). The plural contrasts the same way: οἵ (relative) vs οἱ (article), αἵ vs αἱ.
  • The neuter = a double form: nominative and accusative are one and the same (ὅ singular, ἅ plural) – exactly the rule "the neuter does not distinguish nominative from accusative" that you have met in every paradigm table since Lesson 1.
  • Plural of all three genders: the genitive has only one form ὧν, the dative only οἷς / αἷς – masculine and feminine are distinguished, while the neuter "travels with" the masculine, exactly the pattern of ἐκεῖνος (Lesson 6).

Pattern to carry over: the fastest mnemonic is "article + accent + rough breathing": ὁ → ὅς, τοῦ → οὗ, τῷ → ᾧ, τόν → ὅν… add the accent and the rough breathing and you have the relative pronoun (only the plural dative must be memorized as the set οἷς, αἷς, because the article is τοῖς, ταῖς with a τ). Self-check when you meet a strange word: accent + rough breathing, no initial τ → almost certainly a relative, not an article.

Latin corner: Latin has a "twin brother" of ὅς, ἥ, ὅ: quī, quae, quod – put the two tables side by side and you will see them mirroring each other pair for pair: cuius "of whom" ↔ οὗ, cui "to whom" ↔ ᾧ, quem ↔ ὅν, quam ↔ ἥν, quod ↔ ὅ. Both languages inherited the same Indo-European relative (*kw-): that is why both Latin quī and Greek ὅς are related to the question words "who/what" (Latin quis, Greek τίς – Lesson 2/5). Anyone who knows the Latin table will learn the Greek one in a single sitting.

2. Relative clauses: the double agreement rule (gender–number / case)

A relative clause is a subordinate clause attached to a noun of the main clause – that noun is called the antecedent. The whole subordinate clause does the work of an adjective: ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς λέγει = "the man who-is-speaking" = "the man who is speaking".

The learner's number-one question: which form of ὅς do I choose? The answer is the double agreement rule – the relative pronoun listens to two different masters:

  • Gender and number: from the antecedent – it "inherits" gender (masculine/feminine/neuter) and number (singular/plural) of the antecedent, like an adjective.
  • Case: from its own job inside the subordinate clause – is it the subject? the object? after a preposition? "of whom"? The case is decided entirely by its function inside the subordinate clause, not by the case of the antecedent.
GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς λέγει.ho ánthrōpos hós légei.the man who speaks. (nominative – subject of the subordinate clause)
ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃν βλέπω.ho ánthrōpos hón blépō.the man whom I see. (accusative – object of βλέπω)
ὁ ἄνθρωπος ᾧ πιστεύομεν.ho ánthrōpos hô(i) pisteúomen.the man whom we trust. (dative – πιστεύω + dative, Lesson 4)
ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὗ τὸν φίλον παιδεύομεν.ho ánthrōpos hoû tòn phílon paideúomen.the man whose friend we educate. (genitive – "whose")
τὸ ἔργον ὃ τελειοῖ.to érgon hó teleiói.the task which he completes. (neuter – nominative and accusative are one form)
αἱ Ἀμαζόνες αἳ μάχονται.hai Amazones hái mákhontai.the Amazons who fight. (feminine plural – nominative)

Read rows 3 and 6 closely: ᾧ and αἳ stand in a different case from their antecedents (ὁ nominative; τῶν Ἀμαζόνων genitive) – because the case follows the job. This is exactly the point where the relative pronoun and an ordinary adjective part ways: an adjective agrees in all three (gender, number, case); the relative borrows only two (number, gender) and decides the third for itself.

A 3-step pattern – works for every relative clause: (1) Find the antecedent – the subordinate clause "grows" out of it. (2) Copy gender + number from that noun. (3) Ask what job the pronoun has inside the subordinate clause: subject → nominative; object → accusative; after a preposition → the preposition's case; "of whom" → genitive. To translate: gender–number tells you "which one", case tells you "what is being done with it". Self-check: if the pronoun's case coincides with the antecedent's case "by coincidence" and no role explains it – redo step 3.

Achtung – "whose" drags a noun along: in row 4 the genitive οὗ is followed by its own noun (τὸν φίλον) – because "the man whose friend…" means that what is really being discussed is his friend. The safe model is English "whose": "the man whose friend we educate". Classic error: seeing οὗ and translating "where/in which" (that is a different adverb – see the confusables table at the end of the lesson).

3. Reading long sentences: the "tongs" technique – paying a debt previewed in Lessons 13–14

Relative clauses in real Greek prose almost always have the word order "opened by the pronoun, closed by the verb": ὃν βλέπω, αἳ μάχονται, ᾧ πιστεύομεν – the relative pronoun stands at the front of the subordinate clause, the verb locks it at the end. Picture a pair of tongs: the upper jaw (the pronoun) and the lower jaw (the verb) grip everything in between. When reading a long sentence, the technique is:

  1. Push aside augments and endings – find the relative pronoun first (accent + rough breathing, section 1).
  2. The upper jaw = the pronoun. Fly your eye to the end of the clause and find the lower jaw = the verb.
  3. Read the content held between them, then attach the subordinate clause to its antecedent.

Two "debts" previewed in earlier lessons can be repaid today:

Previously previewed passageDecoded today
Lesson 13 (Klearchos speaks): παρ' οὓς ἡμεῖς τὴν φιλίαν συνθέμενοι κατεθέμεθαοὕς = accusative plural masculine – because it follows παρά + accusative (Lesson 4: "beside, toward") inside the subordinate clause: "the gods beside-whom we deposited our friendship" → "with the gods beside whom we deposited our friendship". Gender–number (masculine, plural) follows the gods; the case (accusative) is decided by παρά.
Lesson 14 (the market of knowledge): ἃ πωλοῦσινἅ = accusative plural neuter – the object of πωλοῦσιν "they sell": "the things that they sell". The antecedent is understood (the wares): a relative clause without an explicit antecedent still translates perfectly well as "what…".

Reading pattern: the relative clause is the "nesting box" of Greek prose: the big box (main clause) contains a smaller box (relative clause), and the smaller box can contain a grandchild box (AcI, prepositional phrase…). Do not read word by word in a straight line – find the box borders: every relative pronoun opens a new box; every closing verb shuts it. After 3–4 nested boxes you will see that a long sentence of Plato is nothing but… a few tidy boxes.

4. Case attraction: when the relative pronoun "leaves" its rightful place

A familiar face in Attic prose (especially Plato – right in today's Reading I): the antecedent stands in the genitive or dative, while by the rule of section 2 the relative pronoun "should" be in the accusative (because it is only an object inside the subordinate clause). Literary Greek often "pulls" the pronoun into the case of the antecedent:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
τοῦ ποταμοῦ ὃν βλέπεις τὸ ὄνομα οὐ γιγνώσκεις.toû potamoû hón blépeis tò ónoma ou gignṓskeis.by the rule: you do not know the name of the river that you see.
τοῦ ποταμοῦ οὗ βλέπεις τὸ ὄνομα οὐ γιγνώσκεις.toû potamoû hoû blépeis tò ónoma ou gignṓskeis.with attraction: ὃν → οὗ – same meaning.

The usual condition: inside the subordinate clause, apart from the pronoun, there is no other accusative – the accusative is "vacant", so it gets pulled toward the antecedent. The phenomenon is called case attraction: the case of the pronoun is attracted into the case of the antecedent.

One example you have already met in Lesson 14: ὧν ἄγουσιν ἀγωγίμων "(the wares) that they carry along, (of the kind that is) for sale" – ὧν "should" be ἅ (accusative plural neuter, object of ἄγουσιν "they carry"), but it is attracted into the genitive of the (understood) antecedent before it.

Achtung – know it when reading, do not imitate it (yet) in writing: attraction is an available option, not a compulsory law: τοῦ ποταμοῦ ὃν βλέπεις is completely correct. So (1) when reading: if you meet οὗ / ᾧ and the subordinate clause "lacks the verb this case would need" – try decoding it as attraction; (2) when doing Greek composition at this level: always use the case required by the rule of section 2, do not attract on your own. The classic error in the other direction: attracting wrongly when the antecedent is nominative – ὁ λόγος οὗ ἀκούομεν is WRONG (if the antecedent is nominative, the accusative ὅν has nothing to "grab onto"– it must be ὁ λόγος ὃν ἀκούομεν).

Latin corner: Latin does not attract – its relative pronoun keeps its case absolutely: fluminis, quod vides, nomen nescis "you do not know the name of the river that you see" – quod stays accusative (neuter) even though the antecedent is genitive. The reverse comparison helps your memory: Greek "smears" the case toward the antecedent, Latin is absolutely rigid. This is one of the few points where Latin is simpler than Greek – make the most of it when you compare.

5. Possessive pronouns: ἐμός, σός, ἡμέτερος, ὑμέτερος – and the Greek way of saying "his"

Continuing the personal pronouns of Lesson 4, today we add the possessive pronoun – in reality a possessive adjective, declined like an a/o adjective of the three-ending type (Lesson 1):

PossessiveTransliterationMeaningAttributive-position example
ἐμός, ἐμή, ἐμόνemós, emḗ, emónmyὁ ἐμὸς πατήρ / ὁ πατὴρ ὁ ἐμός
σός, σή, σόνsós, sḗ, sónyour (sg.)ὁ σὸς λόγος / ὁ λόγος ὁ σός
ἡμέτερος, -α, -ονhēméterosourἡ ἡμετέρα πόλις
ὑμέτερος, -α, -ονhyméterosyour (pl.)τὰ ὑμέτερα ἔργα

Three important points about usage:

  • Position: like every adjective, a possessive pronoun must stand in attributive position (Lessons 1/6: the article directly before it) – ὁ ἐμὸς πατήρ or ὁ πατὴρ ὁ ἐμός. In predicative position, ἐμός is no longer the attributive "my": it works as a predicate "(that man) is mine": ὁ Σωκράτης ἐμὸς πατήρ ἐστιν "Socrates is my father" – in contrast with the attributive ὁ ἐμὸς πατήρ "my father". Classic error: putting ἐμός after article + noun but not slotting it between them (attributive) and letting it trail loosely at the end (predicative) – the meaning turns 180 degrees.
  • The everyday rival: classical Greek mostly says "my" with the genitive of the personal pronoun (Lesson 4): ὁ πατήρ μου, ἡ μήτηρ σου – shorter and more neutral. Use ἐμός when you want to stress "MY own", or in literature and poetry.
  • The 3rd person has no possessive adjective! Classical Greek has no "his/her" adjective at all. To say "his/her father", Greek borrows the genitive of αὐτός (section 6): ὁ πατὴρ αὐτοῦ "his father", ὁ πατὴρ αὐτῆς "her father". Latin has eius – the same pronoun solution.

Pattern – "three ways to say 'of'": (1) ὁ ἐμὸς πατήρ – possessive adjective, stressed "MY"; (2) ὁ πατήρ μου – genitive of the pronoun, the everyday way; (3) "of himself" (section 7): ὁ ἑαυτοῦ πατήρ "his own father (pointedly)". One axis, three points, all translated "of…" but with different shades: neutral – stressed – reflexive. Self-check when reading: when you see a genitive pronoun after a noun, ask "stressed? referring back to the subject?" – if neither, a plain "of…" is the right translation.

Latin corner: the Latin set is meus, tuus, noster, vester – corresponding pair for pair with ἐμός, σός, ἡμέτερος, ὑμέτερος (the commonest is meus ↔ ἐμός; both carry a light emphatic shade). And as in Greek, the Latin 3rd person usually goes the pronoun way: pater eius ↔ ὁ πατὴρ αὐτοῦ. The memorable difference: Latin also has suus "his own" as an adjective; Greek has none and must borrow the reflexive genitive ἑαυτοῦ placed after the noun (section 7).

6. αὐτός: one word, three uses – position decides

Since "his" has to go through αὐτός, you need to know how this word works. αὐτός, αὐτή, αὐτό (autós, autḗ, autó) declines exactly like ἐκεῖνος (Lesson 6 – pronominal declension; the neuter singular αὐτό has no ν). But its "profession" changes with position – the very position you learned in Lessons 1 and 6:

UseIn which position?ExampleEnglishLatin
1. Intensive "himself"predicative (before the article or after the noun)αὐτὸς ὁ βασιλεὺς λέγει. / ὁ βασιλεὺς αὐτόςthe king himself speaksipse
2. "The same" (identical)attributive (article directly before it)ὁ αὐτὸς βασιλεύςthe same king (not another king)idem
3. 3rd-person pronoun (unemphatic)standing alone in the oblique cases – genitive / dative / accusative (without article + noun)βλέπω αὐτόν.I see himis / eum

One set of forms, three meanings – and the recognition rule is precisely the attributive / predicative position of Lesson 6: αὐτὸς ὁ βασιλεύς (predicative) = "the king himself"; ὁ αὐτὸς βασιλεύς (attributive) = "the same king". Just put your finger on the article: article directly before αὐτός → use 2; none → use 1. Use 3 needs no discussion: αὐτόν stands bare, with no article anywhere near it – nothing to emphasize even if it wanted to – it is simply "him/her" (the everyday 3rd-person personal pronoun that Lesson 4 was missing) – and only in the oblique cases; for the nominative exception see Achtung (3) just below.

Achtung – three classic errors: (1) Translating αὐτὸς ὁ βασιλεύς as "the same king" or ὁ αὐτὸς βασιλεύς as "the king himself" – mixing up the positions turns the meaning 180 degrees. (2) More important: αὐτός is never the reflexive "he…-self"! "He hides himself" cannot be κρύπτει αὐτόν (that is "he hides some other man") – you must use ἑαυτόν (section 7). English "himself" covers both meanings ("he sees himself" = reflexive; "he himself sees" = intensive): Greek keeps them strictly apart. (3) αὐτὸς ἔρχεται is not "he is coming"! In the nominative, a solo αὐτός is always the intensive "himself", pointing at the subject already sitting inside the verb ending: "he himself is coming". To say "he is coming", just let the verb ending do the work: ἔρχεται. The 3rd-person pronoun only appears in the oblique cases (genitive / dative / accusative).

Pattern to carry over: the trio "one word – position – profession": (a) preceded directly by the article → "the same"; (b) followed by article + noun but not directly preceded by the article → "himself, the very"; (c) standing alone in an oblique case → 3rd-person "he/she/it"; alone in the nominative → still the intensive "himself". The genitive of use 3 is precisely the "his/her" of section 5. Quick test with three words: τὸν αὐτὸν λόγον – article directly before → "the same word"; αὐτὸς ὁ Σωκράτης – none → "Socrates himself"; βλέπω αὐτόν – alone → "I see him". Answer these three riddles and you own the section.

7. The reflexive pronoun: ἑαυτοῦ and the system of "self"

The reflexive pronoun answers "who receives the action of the very subject?" – the image of a mirror: the action leaves the subject, swings round and shines back onto the subject itself. The paradigm:

PersonGenitiveDativeAccusative
1 Sg.ἐμαυτοῦἐμαυτῷἐμαυτόν
2 Sg.σεαυτοῦσεαυτῷσεαυτόν
3 Sg. (masc./neut.)ἑαυτοῦἑαυτῷἑαυτόν
3 Sg. (fem.)ἑαυτῆςἑαυτῇἑαυτήν

Two morphological observations:

  • The 3rd person = ἑ- (the weak form of the 3rd-person pronoun) + αὐτοῦ; the 1st/2nd = personal pronoun (ἐμ-, σε-) + αὐτοῦ. So memorize the recipe "pronoun + αὐτοῦ" and you can build the whole family.
  • There is no nominative! The reflexive is never the subject – because the subject is precisely the "mirror" it shines back onto. The plural is rare at Level I; prose usually says it in a roundabout way: ἡμᾶς αὐτούς "we ourselves" (pronoun + intensive αὐτός).
GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ Ἡρακλῆς ἑαυτὸν κρύπτει.ho Hēraklês heautòn krýptei.Heracles hides himself.
ὁ Σωκράτης ἑαυτὸν ἐρωτᾷ.ho Sōkrátēs heautòn erôtaî.Socrates asks himself.
βλέπω αὐτόν.blépō autón.I see him (someone else).
ἡ Ἱππολύτη τὴν ἑαυτῆς ζώνην φέρει.hē Hippolýtē tḕn heautês zónēn phérei.Hippolyte wears her own belt.

Row 4 opens a second use: a reflexive genitive after a noun = "of his/her own": ὁ ἑαυτοῦ πατήρ "his own father (for emphasis: not anybody else's father)", in contrast with ὁ αὐτοῦ πατήρ "his father (some man's)". This is exactly where Latin uses suus: pater suus ↔ ὁ ἑαυτοῦ πατήρ.

Achtung – the near-identical-typography trap: the form αὑτοῦ (rough breathing + accent standing bare) is the contracted variant of ἑαυτοῦ "of himself" – while αὐτοῦ (smooth breathing) is "his". They differ by exactly one rough breathing, which is often "swallowed" in a blurry print! Check with semantics: if the genitive refers back to the subject → reflexive (rough breathing); if it just means "of some third party" → αὐτοῦ. You will meet both in today's readings.

Latin corner: Latin packs the reflexive into one word for every person: mē, tē, sē (and suī, sibi in genitive/dative) – "myself/yourself/himself" are all sē. Greek splits them: ἐμαυτόν, σεαυτόν, ἑαυτόν. The point worth buying: the reflexive only points back at the subject – the "mirror" rule is identical in both languages (and identical in German sich – a direct descendant of Latin sē!).

Pattern to carry over: when you meet a pronoun in a text, ask the three career questions: (1) Who does it stand in for? (personal; 3rd person = αὐτός). (2) What does it join? (relative – always opens a subordinate clause). (3) Where does it point? (reflexive → the subject; possessive → someone's; demonstrative → near/far/just-mentioned). As soon as one of the three questions is answered, that word "finds its job" – and the rest of the complex sentence lights up by itself.

  • τὸ σχῆμα ἐστὶν ὃ τὸ χρῶμα ἀεὶ ἀκολουθεῖ: ὅ neuter nominative – the subject of ἀκολουθεῖ inside the subordinate clause (section 2: case follows the job!).   • τὸν αὐτὸν λόγον λέγεις: αὐτός use 2 "the same" – the article directly before it (τὸν αὐτόν), section 6. γελᾷ: a contract verb -άω (Lesson 12).

Meno protests that this is the defining style of the master debaters; Socrates then offers a second definition, "shape is the limit of a solid" (πέρας στερεοῦ) – and even defines color after Empedocles as "a stream flowing off the shapes". The whole stretch Meno 72d–73c is the classic textbook example of defining a concept before debating whether it can be taught.

General grammar points to remember: the passage is a museum of Lesson 15: a relative pronoun obeying the double agreement rule (ἣν in sentence 2: feminine singular after the antecedent, accusative because it is the object of ζητεῖς inside the subordinate clause); reflexives in all three persons (σεαυτόν sentence 3, ἐμαυτὸν sentence 8, ἑαυτὸν sentence 9), each form pointing back at the subject of the verb it travels with; αὐτός in sentence 7 stands in attributive position and therefore carries the meaning "the same" (διδακτὸν αὐτὸν λόγον); the neuter relative pronoun in the nominative (ὅ) that opens each definition in sentence 6 is the classic face of the construction.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. Reflexives: σεαυτόν (C3, 2nd person), ἐμαυτὸν (C8, 1st person), ἑαυτὸν (C9, 3rd person). Relative pronouns: ἣν (C2), ὅ twice (C6, neuter nominative, subject of ἀκολουθεῖ); note that C6 is a single sentence holding two symmetrical relative clauses. αὐτὸν in C7 stands after διδακτὸν inside the article phrase, so it is "the same": "the very same word".

Step 2, Meaning and structure. The speaking turns: Meno in C1, C5, C7 and C9; Socrates in C2 through C4, C6 and C8. The two definitions in C6 are fully symmetrical: only σχῆμα and χρῶμα are swapped, while the mold "that which... always follows" stays put – which is exactly why Meno pokes back with "one and the same word". Double agreement for ἣν in C2: feminine singular borrowed from ἀρετήν, accusative decided by its job as object of ζητεῖς.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. The circular definition is only a formula turned on itself; it never touches the essence. Socrates splits the big question into an easier one (shape and color first, virtue afterwards) and questions himself before questioning others. Today, a debate that has not agreed on the definition of its terms merely shuffles words; but demanding definitions too harshly can itself be a trick to stall the debate.

Quick analysis (checked with the tools of this lesson):

The subordinate clause obeys the "tongs" exactly: opened by ἣν, closed by the verb (section 3).
  • ἡ βασίλεια τῶν Ἀμαζόνων, αἳ … μάχονται: the antecedent (Ἀμαζόνων) stands in the genitive, yet αἳ remains nominative – because the case is decided by the job (subject of μάχονται) (section 2). This is the most natural example of the relative pronoun's "two masters".
  • ἡ Ἱππολύτη αὐτῷ τὴν ζώνην διδόναι βούλεται: αὐτῷ = the 3rd-person dative pronoun "for him" (use 3, section 6); βούλεται deponent (Lesson 5) + object infinitive (Lesson 2). On διδόναι see the info box below.
  • ἡ Ἥρα ἑαυτὴν Ἀμαζόνα ποιεῖ: the 3rd-person feminine reflexive (ἑαυτήν) pointing back at the subject ἡ Ἥρα – "turns herself" (section 7); Ἀμαζόνα is the singular accusative of ἡ Ἀμαζών (-ον stem, Lesson 7) as the second predicate accusative (double accusative – Lesson 2).

Two major constructions of the past week meet in a single word.
  • ἡ ζώνη … τοῦ Ἄρεως ἐστίν: the possessive predicate τοῦ Ἄρεως stands after ἐστίν (Lesson 1: the genitive of "belonging to"); Ἄρεως s-stem (Lesson 8). In myth the belt is a trophy of the war god – this sentence packs the cultural information too.
  • ἡ Ἥρα, ἣ ἑαυτὴν Ἀμαζόνα ἐποίει, … μισεῖ: ἐποίει unaugmented imperfect (contract verb, Lesson 12) paints the background – "Hera, who had turned herself into an Amazon, still hates…": the background/foreground aspect of the imperfect (Lesson 11) plus a relative clause introducing the character.

In the original: Apollodorus tells it so: the belt belongs to Ares; Hera takes the shape of an Amazon and wanders among the warriors crying out that the queen is being carried off; Heracles, believing Hippolyte has betrayed him, kills her, takes the belt, routs the Amazons, then departs. General grammar points to remember: the passage strings together six relative clauses in every situation: attached to the object of an imperative (C1, ἣν, object of φέρει); attached to a genitive noun (C2: αἳ nominative because it is the subject of μάχονται); nesting an infinitive phrase (C7: ἣν accusative because it is at once the object taken up again, προδότην accusative as the logical subject of the infinitive phrase); the object of the verb (C8, C9); introducing a character's background (C10: ἣ + the reflexive ἑαυτὴν). The unaugmented verb ἐποίει (C10) paints the background for the drawn-out action μισεῖ.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. Six relative pronouns: ἣν (C1, accusative), αἳ (C2, nominative), ἣν (C7, accusative), ἣν (C8, accusative), ὅ (C9, accusative), ἣ (C10, nominative). Hera's two reflexive forms: ἑαυτὴν (C5) and ἑαυτὴν (C10), 3rd person feminine. αὐτῷ in C4 and C9 is the 3rd-person dative pronoun standing on its own ("for him"), the plain personal-pronoun use, without emphasis.

Step 2, Meaning and structure. All six tongs are opened by the relative pronoun and closed by the verb of the subordinate clause (φέρει, μάχονται, οἴεται, λαμβάνει, ἔδωκεν, ἐποίει). Sentence 2: Ἀμαζόνων is genitive and expresses only belonging, while αἳ keeps the nominative because it does the job of the subject: gender and number borrowed from the antecedent, case according to the job. Sentence 7: the believe-that construction (οἴεται + the infinitive εἶναι) demands two accusatives: ἣν, the object taken up again, and προδότην as the logical subject – a double accusative.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. The rumor's chain of transformations: one lie told by Hera (C5), an armed crowd with nobody checking (C6), an action that cannot be undone (C7). Sentence 10 tells us that the one who set the story in motion hates Heracles forever and pays no price. Today, a rumor needs three things to become "truth": a source that looks credible, a crowd ready to act, and no one stopping to check; the text packs all three into three consecutive sentences.

  2. ᾧ
  3. ἅ
  4. οὕς
  5. αἷς

Translation: (1) "of the woman (who)" – whose; (2) "to/for the thing (which)" – dative; (3) "the things (which)" – those which; (4) "the men (whom)" – whom (pl.); (5) "to/for the women (whom)" – to whom (f. pl.).

Exercise 2 – Double agreement: choose the correct form

Choose the correct form of the relative pronoun for each sentence and explain the case:

  1. ὁ λόγος ___ ἀκούομεν. (the word that we hear)
  2. αἱ Ἀμαζόνες ___ τὰ ὅπλα φέρουσιν. (the Amazons who carry the weapons)
  3. τὸ σχῆμα ___ τὸ χρῶμα ἀκολουθεῖ. (the shape that the color follows)
  4. ἡ ἀρετή, ___ σὺ ζητεῖς. (the virtue that you seek)
  5. ὁ πατήρ, ___ τὸν φίλον παιδεύομεν. (the father whose friend we educate)
  6. τὸ ἔργον ___ τελειοῖς. (the task that you complete)
  7. αἱ ἀποκρίσεις ___ εὑρίσκομεν. (the answers that we find)
  8. ὁ βασιλεύς, ___ οἱ ἄνθρωποι πείθονται. (the king whom the people obey)

Answers 2

  1. ὃν
  2. αἳ
  3. ὅ
  4. ἥν
  5. οὗ
  6. ὅ
  7. ἅς
  8. ᾧ

Reasoning: (1) accusative – object of ἀκούω + accusative (the thing heard, Lesson 12); (2) nominative – subject of φέρουσιν; (3) nominative – subject of ἀκολουθεῖ inside the subordinate clause (τὸ χρῶμα is the object); (4) accusative – object of ζητεῖς; (5) genitive – "whose" (the noun τὸν φίλον comes along); (6) neuter accusative (nominative and accusative are one form) – object of τελειοῖς; (7) feminine plural accusative – object of εὑρίσκομεν; (8) dative – πείθομαι + dative "obey/trust someone" (Lesson 2: πείθω/πείθομαι).

Exercise 3 – Translating relative clauses

  1. ὁ Μένων ζητεῖ τὸ σχῆμα, ὃ τὸ χρῶμα ἀεὶ ἀκολουθεῖ.
  2. ἡ Ἱππολύτη ἐστὶν ἡ βασίλεια τῶν Ἀμαζόνων, αἳ μετὰ τῶν ἵππων μάχονται.
  3. τοῦ ποταμοῦ οὗ βλέπεις τὸ ὄνομα οὐ γιγνώσκεις.
  4. ὁ Ἡρακλῆς τελειοῖ τὸ ἔργον, ὃ ὁ Εὐρυσθεὺς αὐτῷ ἔδωκεν.
  5. ἡ Ἥρα, ἣ ἑαυτὴν Ἀμαζόνα ἐποίει, τὸν Ἡρακλέα ἀεὶ μισεῖ.

Answers 3

  1. Meno seeks the shape that the color always follows. (ὅ nominative inside the subordinate clause.)
  2. Hippolyte is the queen of the Amazons – the women who fight on horseback. (αἳ nominative; a genitive antecedent does not drag the case along – section 2.)
  3. You do not know the name of the river that you see. (This sentence has attraction: οὗ "should" be ὅν; the genitive antecedent ποταμοῦ has "pulled" the pronoun toward itself – section 4.)
  4. Heracles completes the task that Eurystheus gave him. (ὅ neuter accusative – object of ἔδωκεν inside the subordinate clause; αὐτῷ the 3rd-person dative pronoun.)
  5. Hera – she who had turned herself into an Amazon – hates Heracles forever. (ἣ nominative matching Ἥρα; ἑαυτὴν the reflexive pointing back at the subject; ἐποίει the imperfect painting the background.)

Exercise 4 – αὐτός or ἑαυτοῦ? "The very" or "the same"?

Fill in the correct form (αὐτός / ἑαυτοῦ / their forms) and translate:

  1. ὁ Ἡρακλῆς ___ κρύπτει. (Heracles hides himself)
  2. ὁ Σωκράτης ___ ἐρωτᾷ. (Socrates asks himself)
  3. ὁ Μένων τὸν ___ λόγον λέγει. (Meno says the same word)
  4. ___ ὁ βασιλεὺς λέγει. (The king himself speaks)
  5. ἡ Ἱππολύτη τὴν ___ ζώνην φέρει. (Hippolyte wears her own belt)
  6. ἡ γυνὴ τὰ τέκνα αὐτῆς / ἑαυτῆς παιδεύει. (the woman educates her children / her own children) – how do the two translations differ?

Answers 4

  1. ἑαυτόν
  2. ἑαυτόν
  3. αὐτόν
  4. αὐτὸς
  5. ἑαυτῆς
  6. αὐτῆς: her children (children of some woman – possibly mothered by somebody else); ἑαυτῆς: her very own children (born/brought up by the subject herself) – the reflexive shining back at the subject. Latin: eius vs suus.

Notes: (1)–(2): the action swings back onto the subject → reflexive; (3) the article directly before αὐτόν → "the same"; (4) predicative position, no article before it → the intensive "the very"; (5) a reflexive genitive after a noun = "of her own".

Exercise 5 – Find and fix the error

Each of the following sentences contains exactly one pronoun/relative error; find it and fix it:

  1. ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἥν βλέπω διδάσκει τὸν φίλον.
  2. τὸ σχῆμα ὃς ἀκολουθεῖ τὸ χρῶμα καλόν ἐστιν.
  3. αἱ Ἀμαζόνες ὃς μάχονται ἵππους ἔχουσιν.
  4. ὁ λόγος οὗ ἀκούομεν ἀληθής ἐστιν.
  5. ἐμὸς ὁ πατὴρ ἀεὶ ἐμαυτὸν ἐρωτᾷ.

Answers 5

  1. Wrong gender: ἥν → ὅν (the antecedent ὁ ἄνθρωπος is masculine; the accusative is right because it is the object of βλέπω).
  2. Wrong gender: ὃς → ὅ (the antecedent τὸ σχῆμα is neuter; the nominative is right because it is the subject of ἀκολουθεῖ).
  3. Wrong gender–number: ὃς → αἳ (the antecedent αἱ Ἀμαζόνες is feminine plural; the nominative is right).
  4. Wrong case (attraction applied wrongly): οὗ → ὅν – with a nominative antecedent ὁ λόγος there is nothing to attract; the accusative follows the role (object of ἀκούω).
  5. Wrong position: ἐμὸς ὁ πατήρ → ὁ ἐμὸς πατήρ – the possessive adjective must stand in attributive position (article directly before it); ἐμαυτὸν is in its right place as the 1st-person reflexive.

Exercise 6 – Translate into Greek

  1. "Meno, you are seeking a virtue that you do not yet know."
  2. "Socrates always asks himself and finds the true answers."
  3. "Heracles kills the queen whom he believes to be a traitor."

Answers 6

  1. ὦ Μένων, ἀρετὴν ζητεῖς, ἣν οὔπω γιγνώσκεις.
  2. ὁ Σωκράτης ἀεὶ ἑαυτὸν ἐρωτᾷ καὶ τὰς ἀληθεῖς ἀποκρίσεις εὑρίσκει.
  3. ὁ Ἡρακλῆς τὴν βασίλειαν ἀποκτείνει, ἣν προδότην οἴεται εἶναι.

Notes: (1) ὦ + vocative (Lesson 2); the relative clause slots in after the object; οὔπω "not yet"; (2) the 3rd-person reflexive ἑαυτόν points back at the subject Σωκράτης; ἀληθεῖς accusative plural of the two-ending adjective (Lesson 9); (3) an AcI nested inside a relative clause: ἣν = the logical subject of εἶναι → accusative; προδότην the predicate accusative – exactly the structure analyzed in Reading II.
