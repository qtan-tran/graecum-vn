Goals for this lesson: you can (1) explain the future and decline the active future in all six persons with the endings containing -σ- (sigmatic future), the consonant + σ rules included; (2) decline the middle/passive future and the deponents (βουλήσομαι…), and decline εἰμί in the future (ἔσομαι, ἔσται); (3) recognize the liquid future of verbs with stems in λ/μ/ν/ρ (μενῶ); (4) decline the contract verbs of the three families ποιέω, τιμάω, δηλόω in the present and imperfect; (5) build the "uncontracted" future of contract verbs (ποιήσω) and decode the ἐπολέμει, ἐνομοθέτει forms you met in Lesson 11; (6) read two original passages: Xenophon, Anabasis 1,9 and Plutarch, Lykurgos 14.

1. The future: the tense of what is "about to come"

After the present (Lesson 6) and the imperfect (Lesson 11), you now open the third cell on the timeline: the future. The future's view is still "in progress" (like the present and the imperfect), but it is anchored to a point after the moment of speaking:

TenseThe viewVisualExample
Presentunfolding as it is being spoken of──●──▶ (now)ὁ παῖς παιδεύεται – the child is being educated
Imperfectunfolding at a point in the past──▶●──▶ (back then, Lesson 11)ὁ παῖς ἐπαιδεύετο – the child (back then) was being educated
Futurewill be unfolding, seen from now toward what comes──▶▶● (to come)ὁ παῖς παιδεύσεται – the child will be educated

The Greek future does three main jobs:

  1. Prediction, forecast: "tomorrow the sky will brighten".
  2. Promise: "I shall give you ten thousand gold coins" – the sentence you are about to read in Reading I.
  3. Soft instruction: "you shall do X" – the polite command of the ancient world.

The three-handed clock: which time keyword goes with which tense?

In Lesson 11 you saw τότε as the anchor of the past. Today a new key joins it:

KeywordMeaningTense it usually rides withExample
τότεthen, at that timethe past (Lesson 11), but it also fits the future if the story looks aheadτότε ἐπολέμει "at that time he was waging war"
νῦνnowthe presentνῦν γυμνάζονται "now they are training"
αὔριον ★tomorrowthe futureαὔριον ἄξει "tomorrow he will lead the army"

Latin corner: every language invents its own "material" for the future cell. Latin has two sets: conjugations 1–2 insert -b- (amā-b-ō "I shall love", monē-b-is "you will advise"), conjugations 3–4 insert -ā-/-ē- (leg-ā-m, aud-ē-s). Greek uses one single material for every verb: the letter σ. Meet a σ wedged between stem and ending, with no augment, and you are in the future. Two systems, one idea, two wrappings.

2. The active future: the endings with -σ- (sigmatic future)

The formula of the active future is wonderfully compact:

present stem + σ + present active endings

PersonEndingπαιδεύω "educate"TransliterationEnglish
1 Sg.-σωπαιδεύσωpaideúsōI shall educate
2 Sg.-σειςπαιδεύσειςpaideúseisyou will educate
3 Sg.-σειπαιδεύσειpaideúseihe/she will educate
1 Pl.-σομενπαιδεύσομενpaideúsomenwe shall educate
2 Pl.-σετεπαιδεύσετεpaideúseteyou (pl.) will educate
3 Pl.-σουσι(ν)παιδεύσουσι(ν)paideúsousi(n)they will educate

Two pieces of good news, one that needs care:

  • No augment. The augment is the marker of the past (Lesson 11); the future never takes one. So παιδεύσω and the ἐπαιδευσ--forms can never collide.
  • Six endings, six distinct shapes. For the first time you need no "form-clash" trap: in the present -εις/-ει look nearly alike, in the imperfect -ον (1 Sg.) collides with -ον (3 Pl.), but in the future the 2 Sg. -σεις, with its visible σ, is clearly distinct from the 3 Sg. -σει.
  • But: the stem of the verb may "change shape" when it meets σ – that is the subject of the next section.

The consonant + σ rules: three paths of transformation

The sound-combination rules you first met with elision (Lesson 3) now come into full play. A stem ending in a consonant lets that consonant "fuse" with σ:

Stem ends in+ σ becomesExamples
labials π, β, φψγράφω "write" → γράψω (grápsō) "will write"; πέμπω "send" → πέμψω "will send"
palatals κ, γ, χξἄγω "lead" → ἄξω (áksō) "will lead"
dentals τ, δ, θσ (the dental drops out, σ takes its place)πείθω "persuade" → πείσω (peísō) "will persuade"

Spot the pattern: these three paths are not future-only – you will meet them again, exactly the same, wherever σ is attached in the aorist (Lesson 13) and in neuter nouns of the type -μα, -ματος (Lesson 15). Learn them once, use them in three places.

Achtung – an exception to learn by face: ἔχω → ἕξω. The verb "have" changed face once in the past (εἶχον, Lesson 11); now it changes face a second time in the future: ἕξω (héksō) "I shall have" – a rough breathing appears over the initial vowel! So when "peeling the future" (section 7) you meet a form beginning with ἑ- and rough breathing, do not keep hunting through the dictionary under ἐ-.

How to learn this: learn each "present + future" pair as a single flashcard: γράφω – γράψω, πείθω – πείσω, ἄγω – ἄξω. When you later learn the aorist, the card grows into a triplet: γράφω – γράψω – ἔγραψα.

Deponents keep behaving exactly as deponents (Lessons 5, 10): a present in the m/p means a future in the m/p too, and the meaning stays active:

Achtung – the "voice-flip" group in the future: present active, future m/p. A small group of verbs, all very common: their present looks completely active, but their future carries m/p endings. Three faces to learn today: λαμβάνω "take" → λήψομαι "will take"; τίκτω "give birth to" → τέξομαι "will give birth" (you meet it right in Reading II: τέξονται); ἀκούω "hear" → ἀκούσομαι "will hear". There is a fourth face of ἀκούω to remember: it takes a genitive for the person/source heard, or an accusative for the thing heard – both at once also happens: ἀκούσεσθε ἐμοῦ τὴν ἀλήθειαν akoúsesthe emoû tēn alḗtheian – "you will hear from me the truth" (ἐμοῦ = genitive, τὴν ἀλήθειαν = accusative). Self-check: if you see the endings -σομαι/-σεται while the dictionary lists the verb as active, do not rush to "correct" it into *τέξει – look up the verb's "future" entry; dictionaries state the m/p ending for this group.

εἰμί in the future: ἔσομαι and the ἔσται trap

The verb "to be" (present in Lesson 6, past in Lesson 11) now collects its last piece, the future:

Note the 3rd singular: ἔσται (éstai) – the one place where the future's σ "survives" right before -ται instead of dropping. The meaning of ἔσται spans both "will be" and "there will be, it will happen": πόλεμος ἔσται "there will be war".

Latin corner: the two line up person for person: Latin erō, eris, erit ↔ Greek ἔσομαι, ἔσῃ, ἔσται. The etymology is the same: Indo-European root es- + future marker -s-; Greek preserves the s (ἐσ-σ-ται → ἔσται), Latin "rusts" the s into r (esō → erō). One genome, two expressions.

4. The liquid future: μένω → μενῶ

Verbs whose stems end in λ, μ, ν, ρ (the "liquid" consonants) cannot comfortably carry a σ: these sounds make the σ "melt away", and to compensate, the vowel before it is lengthened – the phenomenon of compensatory lengthening you already met when peeling the temporal augment in Lesson 11 (ᾐσθανόμην).

PresentLiquid futureWhat happens
μένω (ménō) "remain"μενῶ (menô) "will remain"μεν + ε + σ + ω: the σ drops out before ν, the ε lengthens in compensation: ε + ω → ω
κρίνω "judge"κρῑνῶ (krīnô)the σ drops out before ν; the iota of κρι- sinks to an iota subscript (a subscript iota, already met with the augment ᾐ- ᾠ- in Lesson 11)

The full paradigm of μένω:

PersonFutureTransliterationEnglish
1 Sg.μενῶmenôI shall remain
2 Sg.μενεῖςmeneîsyou will remain
3 Sg.μενεῖmeneîhe/she will remain
1 Pl.μενοῦμενmenoûmenwe shall remain
2 Pl.μενεῖτεmeneîteyou (pl.) will remain
3 Pl.μενοῦσι(ν)menoûsi(n)they will remain

Look closely: this paradigm looks exactly like the present of a contract verb of the -έω family (which you meet right in section 5). That is no coincidence: the liquid future is precisely a kind of "contraction" – the σ drops and the lengthened vowel then fuses into one. How to tell the two apart lies in which verb it is: if the dictionary lists μένω, κρίνω (a stem with a liquid consonant), then μενῶ is a future; if the dictionary lists ποιέω, then ποιῶ is a contracted present. The comparison table in section 11 gathers both.

5. Contract verbs: the three families ποιέω / τιμάω / δηλόω

Until now all our verbs had stems ending in a consonant. But a very large group – and one of the most frequently used – has stems ending in a vowel: ε, α, ο. When the ending begins with a vowel (like -ω, -εις, -ομεν), the two vowels share one syllable and "contract" into a single vowel: hence the name contract verb.

The dictionary always lists the uncontracted form so you know the root: ποιέω "make, do", τιμάω "honour", δηλόω "make clear". These three verbs represent three large families; here is the present of all three:

3 Sg.ποιεῖ (poieî)τιμᾷ (timâi)δηλοῖ (dêloî)
1 Pl.ποιοῦμεν (poioûmen)τιμῶμεν (timômen)δηλοῦμεν (dêloûmen)

An extremely quick recognition pattern: the contracted vowel always carries the circumflex and stands exactly in the position of the original vowel: ποιῶ, τιμᾷ, δηλοῖ, ποιοῦμεν. With ordinary verbs (like παιδεύω), by contrast, the accent follows the recessive rule you have known since Lesson 2. Meet a circumflex "riding on" the long stem vowel, and you are almost certainly holding a contract verb.

You met a contract form long ago without naming it: Lesson 5 had εἰρήνην ποιεῖται "establishes peace" – that is precisely the middle of ποιέω (its full paradigm will come in Lesson 20). Today the debt is paid: ποιεῖται = ποιέ-ε-ται, the m/p ending contracted after a vowel into -εῖται.

6. The imperfect and future of contract verbs (decoding the ἐπολέμει of Lesson 11)

Imperfect: augment + the uncontracted stem + secondary endings

The rule in one line: take the uncontracted stem (ποιε-, τιμα-, δηλο- in their original shape), attach the augment ἐ- at the front (Lesson 11), then add the secondary endings:

PersonποιέωτιμάωδηλόωSecondary ending
1 Sg.ἐποίουν (epoíoun)ἐτίμων (etímon)ἐδήλουν (edêloun)-ον
2 Sg.ἐποίειςἐτίμαςἐδήλους-ες
3 Sg.ἐποίειἐτίμαἐδήλου-ε(ν)
1 Pl.ἐποιοῦμενἐτιμῶμενἐδηλοῦμεν-ομεν
2 Pl.ἐποιεῖτεἐτιμᾶτεἐδηλοῦτε-ετε
3 Pl.ἐποίουν(ν)ἐτίμων(ν)ἐδήλουν(ν)-ον(ν)

Note two details:

  • In the singular, the α of τιμάω "retreats" to ἐτίμα, ἐτίμας, ἐτίμων (no contraction), but in the plural it contracts as usual (ἐτιμῶμεν, ἐτιμᾶτε). Let the table above be your standard.
  • 1 Sg. and 3 Pl. continue to coincide (ἐποίουν) – exactly the imperfect active rule of Lesson 11.

Paying a debt from Lesson 11. In the Diodoros reading you met ἐνομοθέτει "he was enacting laws" and ἐχορήγει "he was financing" – today you can read them like print: augment ἐ- + uncontracted stem -νομοθετε- / -χορηγε- + the 3 Sg. ending -ει of a contract verb of the -έω family. And in today's Reading I, the star form ἐπολέμει "he was waging war" follows exactly that same formula, from πολεμέω "wage war". The original text was never "beyond" you – it was only waiting for you to reach it.

The future: NO contraction – ποιήσω, τιμήσω, δηλώσω stand still

A delightful surprise: in the future, contract verbs drop the "contracting" game – because the future ending begins with σ (a consonant), there are no longer two vowels crowding each other. The stem appears with its long vowel ready-made (ποιη-, τιμη-, δηλω-), plus σ and the endings you learned in section 2:

Personποιέωτιμάωδηλόω
1 Sg.ποιήσω (poiḗsō)τιμήσωδηλώσω
2 Sg.ποιήσειςτιμήσειςδηλώσεις
3 Sg.ποιήσειτιμήσειδηλώσει
1 Pl.ποιήσομεντιμήσομενδηλώσομεν
2 Pl.ποιήσετετιμήσετεδηλώσετε
3 Pl.ποιήσουσι(ν)τιμήσουσι(ν)δηλώσουσι(ν)

Achtung – the accent stands still, it does not retreat. You are used to the recessive rule (παιδεύσομεν with the accent on the third syllable from the end). But in the future of contract verbs, the accent stands still on the long stem vowel throughout the paradigm: ποιήσω, ποιήσεις, ποιήσει, ποιήσομεν, ποιήσετε, ποιήσουσι(ν). This is the special rule of the "contract future" – and it is good news: one paradigm, one accent position, no shifting. Exercise 6 below contains ἀκολουθήσουσι "they will follow" from ἀκολουθέω – the accent stays on the ή no matter how long the word grows.

Latin corner: Latin contracts too, but it contracts once and then "stamps" the result into the vocabulary: coāgō "I gather together" contracts to cōgō; nihil contracts to nīl. The Romans store the result in the dictionary; the Greeks contract "live" every time they conjugate. Two philosophies handling the same sound phenomenon.

7. Reading strategy: spotting -σ- and "peeling the future"

Meet a Greek verb, and the diagnostic question set now has three branches:

  1. Is there an augment (ἐ- or a lengthened initial vowel)? → past; peel the augment as in Lesson 11.
  2. No augment, but a σ wedged between stem and ending? → future; peel the σ to get back to the present stem.
  3. No augment, no σ, but it looks "contracted" (circumflex over a long vowel)? → present of a contract verb; or a liquid future if the dictionary gives a stem in λ/μ/ν/ρ. Ask: which verb is this?

The "peel the future" table – dissecting each form back to its dictionary form:

Form metPeelDictionary formRead as
παιδεύσειremove -σει → stem + σπαιδεύω3 Sg. "he will educate"
γράψουσι(ν)ψ → φ + σγράφω3 Pl. "they will write"
μενοῦμενου (compensatory) → ε + σ lost before νμένω1 Pl. "we shall remain"
ἄξειξ → γ + σἄγω3 Sg. "he will lead"
βουλήσονταιremove -σονταιβούλομαι (deponent)3 Pl. "they will want"

Achtung – σ does not hold a monopoly on the future. A σ does not guarantee a future: the aorist (Lesson 13) is also built with σ for most verbs. The difference lies in the augment: λύσω "will loosen" (future, no augment) ↔ ἔλυσα "loosened" (aorist, with augment). You will drill this pair thoroughly in Lesson 13; today just engrave the principle: the augment is the border of the past.

8. Reading I: Klearchos and the ten thousand gold coins (Xenophon, Anabasis 1,9)

Context. The Anabasis (the name means "the march up country") by Xenophon (about 430–354 BC), an Athenian, recounts the expedition of Cyrus the Younger: in 401 BC this Persian prince secretly hired a Greek army to contest the throne with his brother, King Artaxerxes II. Note: this Cyrus belongs to the same Achaemenid house as the Cyrus the Great of the Lesson 11 reading, and carries that king's name: the Greeks called him "Cyrus the Younger" to distinguish him from "Cyrus the Great". Book 1 lists where each contingent was recruited; passage 1,9 introduces the second army, commanded by Klearchos the Spartan. The reading below keeps nearly the full original wording, shortening only a few structures not yet learned (noted where they occur).

1 ὁ Κλέαρχος Λακεδαιμόνιος φυγὰς ἦν. 2 ὁ Κῦρος ἠγάσθη αὐτὸν καὶ δίδωσιν αὐτῷ μυρίους δαρεικούς. 3 ὁ δὲ τὸ χρυσίον εἶχεν καὶ στράτευμα συνέλεγεν· ἐπολέμει γὰρ τοῖς Θρᾳξὶ τοῖς ὑπὲρ Ἑλλήσποντον. 4 αἱ δὲ πόλεις αὐτῷ χρήματα συνεβάλλοντο εἰς τὴν τροφὴν τῶν στρατιωτῶν. 5 τὸ δὲ στράτευμα οὕτω ἐτρέφετο.

ho Kléarkhos Lakedaimónios phygàs ên. ho kýros ēgásthē autòn kaì dídōsin autōi myríous dareikoús. ho dè tò chrysíon êichen kaì stráteyma synélegen· epólemei gàr toîs Thrāxì toîs hypér Hellēsponton. hai dè póleis autōi chrḗmata synebállonto eis tḕn trophḕn tôn stratiōtôn. tò dè stráteyma hoútō etrépheto.

  1. Step 1, Formal observation: underline every verb and note its tense: present, imperfect, or a form to "learn by face" (no need to name it yet)? Sentences 2 and 3 each contain one special verb: a present standing inside a past story, and an -ει ending of a contract verb – circle both and guess why the author uses them.
  2. Step 2, Meaning and structure: build a "who does what to whom" table with four rows: Klearchos, Cyrus, the cities, the army – note in each row the case and number of the recipient (if there is one). Sentence 4: who contributes, what, to whom, for what? Which adverb does sentence 5 use to underline that mechanism?
  3. In your view, what does Klearchos gain by hiding the information, and where in today's life does an "unlabeled payment" of that kind sit (scholarships, sponsorship, advertising)?

English (free translation)

Sentence-by-sentence dissection

Sentence 1

ὁ Κλέαρχος Λακεδαιμόνιος φυγὰς ἦν – "Klearchos the Spartan was an exile". This opening sentence is word-for-word the original – you have just read genuine Xenophon. ἦν is the imperfect of εἰμί (Lesson 11); φυγάς, -άδος (gloss: exile) "one banished, one forced to leave his homeland" – a masculine noun of the third declension; Λακεδαιμόνιος (gloss: Spartan) "Spartan" stands in apposition to ὁ Κλέαρχος.

Sentence 2

ὁ Κῦρος ἠγάσθη αὐτὸν καὶ δίδωσιν αὐτῷ μυρίους δαρεικούς – "Cyrus admired him and (the story continues:) gives him ten thousand dareiks". Three grammar gifts:

  • ἠγάσθη (from ἄγαμαι, a deponent; gloss: admire) "wondered at, admired" – today just read it as "admired": the form belongs to the aorist, the "tense of the event", which you will learn in Lesson 13.
  • δίδωσιν (from δίδωμι; gloss: give) "give" – a present! Xenophon is narrating the past but presses the "present" button to make the scene vivid: that is the historic present; when translating, settle it back into the past: "and he gave him…". The verb δίδωμι belongs to the "mi-verb" class, dissected only in Lesson 20 – for now learn its face.
  • μυρίους δαρεικούς – two accusative plurals side by side: μυρίοι (gloss: ten thousand) "ten thousand" (a bigger number than χίλιοι "thousand" of Lesson 8); δαρεικός (gloss: Persian gold coin) "dareik", the standard Persian gold coin. A sum large enough to keep a whole army in the field for years.

Sentence 3

ὁ δὲ τὸ χρυσίον εἶχεν καὶ στράτευμα συνέλεγεν· ἐπολέμει γὰρ τοῖς Θρᾳξὶ τοῖς ὑπὲρ Ἑλλήσποντον – "He kept the gold and was gathering an army; for he was waging war against the Thracians (the ones) beyond the Hellespont".

  • εἶχεν – imperfect "was having" of ἔχω, exactly the shortened form learned in Lesson 11; τὸ χρυσίον (gloss: gold, money) "gold (money)".
  • συνέλεγεν – imperfect of συνλέγω "gather": συν- + the root λέγ-. "Gather, pick" is an old meaning of λέγω that survives only in compounds – you have met its relatives: προκρίνω from Diodoros (Lesson 11), and ἐκλεκτός will appear later. The original uses the aorist συνέλεξεν ("having gathered"); our narrated past reads it as an imperfect.
  • ἐπολέμει – the grammar star of the passage: imperfect of the contract verb πολεμέω (gloss: wage war) "wage war" – augment ἐ- + uncontracted stem πολεμε- + the 3 Sg. ending -ει, exactly the table of section 6. Meet it again in the wild: this form floods Greek historiography.
  • τοῖς Θρᾳξί – dative plural of ὁ Θρᾷξ, -κος (gloss: Thracian) "a Thracian", a people of the north; ὁ Ἑλλήσποντος (gloss: Hellespont) "the Hellespont" (today's Dardanelles strait).
  • τοῖς ὑπὲρ Ἑλλήσποντον – article + prepositional phrase: "those (men) beyond the Hellespont". ὑπέρ + accusative (gloss: beyond) "beyond, on the far side of": a new preposition, close kin of ὑπό (Lesson 10) – just as περί stands beside παρά. The original says it in full, τοῖς οἰκοῦσι "those dwelling" – a form not yet learned, so we leave it implicit.

Sentence 4

αἱ δὲ πόλεις αὐτῷ χρήματα συνεβάλλοντο εἰς τὴν τροφὴν τῶν στρατιωτῶν – "And the cities were contributing money to him for the upkeep of the soldiers". συνεβάλλοντο is the imperfect middle/passive 3 Pl. of συμβάλλω "contribute, throw in" (Lesson 11 supplied the endings -όμην, -ετο, -οντο; the root βαλλ- "place, throw"): the ending -οντο is in its slot. εἰς + accusative "into" (familiar from the early lessons); ἡ τροφή "upkeep, sustenance", kin of τρέφω "feed" in sentence 5; τῶν στρατιωτῶν – genitive plural of ὁ στρατιώτης "soldier", declined exactly like ὁ πολίτης you already know.

Sentence 5

τὸ δὲ στράτευμα οὕτω ἐτρέφετο – "And the army was being maintained in this way". ἐτρέφετο – imperfect m/p 3 Sg. of τρέφω (gloss: feed, nourish) "feed": "was being fed". οὕτω (gloss: in this way) "in this way" – an adverb that rings the whole mechanism just narrated.

The original, closing sentence: τοῦτο δ' αὖ οὕτω τρεφόμενον ἐλάνθανεν αὐτῷ τὸ στράτευμα – "and so, being fed in this way, the army escaped his notice". The verb ἐλάνθανεν (from λανθάνω; gloss: escape notice) "escaped notice, stayed hidden": the soldiers were fed without knowing they were an army for Cyrus's expedition – the expedition's secret lay precisely in that "not knowing". Chapters 1,10–1,11 go on to introduce the other armies; together they are "the Ten Thousand" – the army that passes into history after the battle of Cunaxa and the famous march home.

General grammar points to remember: the passage shows Lesson 12 at work with exactly two faces of tense: the imperfect paints the narrative background (εἶχεν, συνέλεγεν, συνεβάλλοντο, ἐτρέφετο), while the vivid accents are entrusted to the historic present (δίδωσιν) and to the contract verb (ἐπολέμει, with its -ει ending exactly as the section-6 table has it). The central figure is referred to by the third-person pronoun in two cases at once: αὐτόν (accusative, as object) and αὐτῷ (dative, the recipient): Cyrus gives to him, the cities contribute to him, the army is fed in that way – a dative–accusative chain running around one man.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. The verbs: C1 ἦν (imperfect of εἰμί, Lesson 11); C2 ἠγάσθη (an aorist form to learn by face) and δίδωσιν (present, historic present); C3 εἶχεν, συνέλεγεν (imperfect) and ἐπολέμει (imperfect of the contract verb πολεμέω, the star of the passage); C4 συνεβάλλοντο (imperfect m/p plural); C5 ἐτρέφετο (imperfect m/p singular). Two special spots: δίδωσιν presses the "present" button in the middle of a past story to make the scene vivid; ἐπολέμει illustrates the contract-ending table of section 6.

Step 2, Meaning and structure. The four-row table: (1) Klearchos is an exile, keeps the gold and gathers an army; (2) Cyrus admires him (the deponent ἠγάσθη) and gives him ten thousand dareiks (recipient αὐτῷ, dative singular); (3) the cities contribute money to him (αὐτῷ, dative) toward the upkeep of the soldiers (εἰς τὴν τροφήν, accusative); (4) the army is fed (ἐτρέφετο, m/p). Sentence 4: the cities are the subject, χρήματα (accusative) is what is contributed, to αὐτῷ, into εἰς τὴν τροφήν τῶν στρατιωτῶν. Sentence 5 closes the circle with the adverb οὕτω "in this way", ringing back the whole mechanism just narrated.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. Klearchos takes ten thousand dareiks and keeps the army fed without a named payer – perhaps so that Cyrus can hide the scale of the expedition from his brother the king, and so that Klearchos himself holds the troops. A payment with no payer named keeps its power today: unlabeled sponsorship, conditional scholarships, advertising paid through third parties – the recipient stays "fed without knowing by whom".

9. Reading II: "Only we give birth to men" (Plutarch, Lykurgos 14)

Context. Plutarch (about AD 46–119), from Chaeronea, wrote the Parallel Lives (Vitae parallelae), a work standing with one foot in Greek and one in Roman literature. The Lykurgos chapter of that collection – the legendary lawgiver of Sparta, whom you met in Lesson 8 – discusses in section 14 the physical training of Spartan girls: they ran, wrestled and threw the discus in public. The passage closing the section contains one of the most famous one-liners in Greek literature, from the mouth of Queen Gorgo, wife of King Leonidas.

1 ὁ Λυκοῦργος ἐνόμιζε τὴν παιδείαν μέγα ἔργον τοῦ νομοθέτου εἶναι. 2 αἱ οὖν Λάκαιναι δρόμοις καὶ πάλαις τὰ σώματα ἐγύμναζον. 3 ὁ γὰρ Λυκοῦργος ἐνόμιζε· αἱ παρθένοι ἰσχυραὶ ἔσονται καὶ τέκνα ἀγαθὰ τέξονται. 4 μία δὲ ξένη ἔλεγεν πρὸς Γοργῶ· "Μόναι τῶν ἀνδρῶν ἄρχετε ὑμεῖς αἱ Λάκαιναι." 5 ἡ δὲ Γοργὼ ἔφη· "Μόναι γάρ τίκτομεν ἄνδρας."

ho Lykoûrgos enómize tḕn paideían méga érgon toû nomothétou eînai. hai oûn Lákainai drómois kaì pálais tà sṓmata egýmnazon. ho gàr Lykoûrgos enómize· hai parthénoi ischyraì ésontai kaì tékna agathà téxontai. mía dè xénē élegen pròs Gorgọ̄i·"Mónai tôn andrôn árchete hyméis hai Lákainai." hē dè Gorgò ḗphē·"Mónai gár, tíktemen ándras."

Three reading steps: work through them in order before scrolling down to the translation; the answers appear only in print, for checking. The text is numbered by sentence: C4 means sentence 4.

  1. Step 1, Formal observation: underline every verb and sort them into two groups: the "storytelling" group (imperfect) and the "looking forward" group (future). In the dialogue (sentences 4 and 5), which tense do the verbs return to? Circle the two futures in sentence 3 and check section 3 to see that one of them belongs to the "voice-flip" group you have already learned.
  2. Step 2, Meaning and structure: build a "who does what" table with four rows: Lykurgos, the Spartan maidens, the foreign woman, Gorgo. Sentence 1 has an object + infinitive structure (learned in Lesson 4): who considers what to be what? Sentence 4 has a "frame embracing the verb" – find the two phrases at the two ends and the verb in the middle.
  3. Step 3, Discussion and connection: with which verb does the outsider praise the Spartan women for "ruling the men", and which verb does Gorgo swap in? One changed word turns a mocking compliment into pride – in your view, does the power of the reply lie in grammar or in culture? Lykurgos's physical-training programme for maidens, seen from today: progress, or another kind of imposition?

Translation:

English (free translation)
Lykurgos considered education the great work of the lawgiver.
So the Spartan women were training their bodies by running and wrestling.
For Lykurgos held that: the maidens will be strong and will bear fine children.
A foreign woman said to Gorgo: "You alone, the Spartan women, rule over the men."

Sentence-by-sentence dissection

Sentence 1

ὁ Λυκοῦργος ἐνόμιζε τὴν παιδείαν μέγα ἔργον τοῦ νομοθέτου εἶναι – "Lykurgos considered education the great work of the lawgiver". ἐνόμιζε – imperfect of νομίζω (Lesson 4), opening an accusative + infinitive structure (AcI): object τὴν παιδείαν + complement μέγα ἔργον + εἶναι – exactly the AcI practice sentence you met in Lesson 4. ἡ παιδεία and ὁ νομοθέτης were both learned in Lesson 11. The original uses superlatives: μέγιστον καὶ κάλλιστον "greatest and finest" – the superlative is not yet learned, so our simplified version reduces it to μέγα "great"; the full original will be handled when you reach Lesson 22 (comparative and superlative).

Sentence 2

αἱ οὖν Λάκαιναι δρόμοις καὶ πάλαις τὰ σώματα ἐγύμναζον – "So the Spartan women were training their bodies by running and wrestling". ἐγύμναζον – imperfect 3 Pl. of γυμνάζω "train (the body)" (future: γυμνάσω). δρόμοις καὶ πάλαις – the dative of means (Lesson 4): "by runs, by wrestling-bouts". τὰ σώματα – accusative plural of τὸ σῶμα "body" (Lesson 7). οὖν "so" (Lesson 5). The original: τὰ σώματα τῶν παρθένων… διεπόνησεν "he subjected the bodies of the maidens to toil" – an aorist, with Lykurgos himself as subject; our retelling makes the Spartan women the subject so that the whole sentence sits safely in the imperfect.

Sentence 3

ὁ γὰρ Λυκοῦργος ἐνόμιζε· αἱ παρθένοι ἰσχυραὶ ἔσονται καὶ τέκνα ἀγαθὰ τέξονται – "For Lykurgos held that: the maidens will be strong and will bear fine children". Two futures straight from the tables of section 3: ἔσονται (3 Pl. of εἰμί) and τέξονται (3 Pl. of τίκτω – precisely the "voice-flip" group from the achtung in section 3!). ἰσχυραί – nominative plural feminine of the adjective ἰσχυρός "strong" (two endings declined like ἀγαθός, Lesson 9); τέκνα ἀγαθά – accusative "fine children". The original wraps this in a purpose clause ("so that the maidens…"), a structure not yet learned – the retelling recasts it as confident narration.

Sentence 4

μία δὲ ξένη ἔλεγεν πρὸς Γοργῶ – "A foreign woman said to Gorgo". μία – "one" (feminine), from the paradigm εἷς, μία, ἕν learned in Lesson 8; ἡ ξένη – feminine of ξένος "foreigner, guest" (Lesson 4); ἔλεγεν πρὸς + accusative – the familiar frame of Lesson 11. Her remark: Μόναι τῶν ἀνδρῶν ἄρχετε ὑμεῖς αἱ Λάκαιναι – "You alone, the Spartan women, rule over the men". Peel it: μόναι – feminine plural of μόνος "alone, only"; τῶν ἀνδρῶν – genitive plural of ὁ ἀνήρ, ἀνδρός "man" (third declension, learn to recognize it); ἄρχετε – 2 Pl. present of ἄρχω + genitive "rule" (Lesson 11); ὑμεῖς αἱ Λάκαιναι – pronoun + noun in apposition: "you, the Spartan women". A "frame A occupying both ends" pattern – one worth remembering: τῶν ἀνδρῶν at the front, αἱ Λάκαιναι at the close, embracing the verb between them.

Sentence 5

ἡ δὲ Γοργὼ ἔφη· "Μόναι γάρ τίκτομεν ἄνδρας" – "Gorgo replied: "For only we give birth to men"". ἔφη (from φημί; gloss: say) "(she) said" – a past form to learn by face, from the most-used verb of saying in Greek; Plutarch slots it right inside the remark (Μόναι γάρ, ἔφη, τίκτομεν ἄνδρας). τίκτομεν – 1 Pl. present of τίκτω "give birth to". The wordplay at its peak: the outsider praises Sparta "you rule (ἄρχετε) men", Gorgo swaps one word – "we give birth to (τίκτομεν) men" – and the reply becomes an aphorism. γάρ "for" (Lesson 5) turns the compliment into a proud definition.

The original, verbatim: εἰπούσης γάρ τινος, ὡς ἔοικε, ξένης πρὸς αὐτὴν ὡς "Μόναι τῶν ἀνδρῶν ἄρχετε ὑμεῖς αἱ Λάκαιναι," "Μόναι γάρ," ἔφη, "τίκτομεν ἄνδρας." – the two remarks are kept exactly as in our simplified version; the outer shell (εἰπούσης… is a genitive absolute clause, not yet learned) adds: "a certain foreign woman, as it seems, said this to her, and (she) replied so". The historical Gorgo was the wife of Leonidas, the Spartan king famous at Thermopylae; Herodotos also records several anecdotes about her quick wit from childhood. A reply this compressed later came to be called "laconic" – after the speech-style of the Lacedaemonians.

General grammar points to remember: the passage strings together all three tense layers of Lessons 11–12: the imperfect narrates (ἐνόμιζε, ἐγύμναζον, ἔλεγεν, ἔφη), the future looks ahead to the coming generation (ἔσονται, τέξονται– τέξονται belongs to the voice-flip group: active present but middle future), and the vivid present lives inside the dialogue (ἄρχετε, τίκτομεν). Sentence 1 displays the consider-to-be structure (ἐνόμιζε τὴν παιδείαν μέγα ἔργον ... εἶναι), and the two remarks form a matched pair of verbs – a valuable lesson: change one verb and you change the whole argument.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. The storytelling group (imperfect): ἐνόμιζε (C1, C3), ἐγύμναζον (C2), ἔλεγεν; while ἄρχετε in the dialogue (C4) and τίκτομεν (C5) are the directly vivid present of speech. Looking forward: ἔσονται (C3, future of εἰμί) and τέξονται (C3, future of τίκτω– exactly the "voice-flip" group of the achtung in section 3: active present but middle future). The two lines of dialogue keep the present because speech is not tied to the narrative time.

Step 2, Meaning and structure. The four-row table: (1) Lykurgos considers education the great work of the lawgiver (object τὴν παιδείαν + complement μέγα ἔργον + infinitive εἶναι); (2) the Spartan maidens train their bodies by running and wrestling (dative of means δρόμοις καὶ πάλαις); (3) the foreign woman says to Gorgo that they alone rule the men; (4) Gorgo answers that only they give birth to men. The "frame embracing the verb" in sentence 4: τῶν ἀνδρῶν at the front (the genitive governed by ἄρχω), the verb ἄρχετε in the middle, αἱ Λάκαιναι at the close.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. The outsider uses ἄρχετε (rule), Gorgo swaps in τίκτομεν (give birth to): the same μόναι, the same ἀνδρῶν/ἄνδρας, but power is redefined from "holding power" to "producing those who hold it" – the force lies both in the grammar (one verb swapped inside a kept frame) and in the culture (the laconic reply). The physical-training programme for maidens, seen from today, has two faces: it gave women health and a public presence rare in Greece, but it still served the state's goal (bearing fine children), not individual freedom.

(c) What does the final sentence say is about to happen to the gold money, and what does that signal about Klearchos's role? (d) Peel ἀκολουθήσουσι back to its dictionary form and translate.

Answers 6

(a) ἄξει (ἄγω, 3 Sg. "will lead"), ἀκολουθήσουσι (ἀκολουθέω, 3 Pl.), ἕξει (ἔχω, 3 Sg., with the rough breathing of the exception). (b) Both are past: ἐπολέμει = augment ἐ- + uncontracted stem of a contract verb + the 3 Sg. secondary ending; συνεβάλλοντο = augment + the secondary m/p ending -οντο. Neither has a σ, neither is a future – matching the τότε that opens the passage. (c) The gold will no longer be in Klearchos's hands but in Cyrus's: the cities pay for the upkeep, but Cyrus himself is about to lead the army in person – the commander's role is drifting from Klearchos to Cyrus. (d) ἀκολουθήσουσι = ἀκολουθέω (+ dative) 3 Pl. future "they will follow (Cyrus)"; the accent stands still on ή however long the word – exactly the contract-future rule.
