What you will be able to do after this lesson: (1) conjugate and translate present active verbs in all six persons using the "stem + ending → person + number" routine; (2) recognize the vocative, including the exception ὦ ἄνθρωπε, and translate sentences containing direct address; (3) tell apart an infinitive used as subject (after δεῖ) and as object (after ἐθέλω…); (4) translate the two kinds of double accusative – "teach someone something" and "consider someone something"; (5) choose correctly between οὐ / οὐκ / οὐχ; (6) handle the two strategies for avoiding the vowel clash (hiatus): movable nu and elision, including the exception μετά → μεθ'.

How to use this lesson: This lesson extends Lesson 1 exactly where Lesson 1 "framed things off": the 3rd person becomes all six persons, the infinitive takes on two roles (subject/object), and you gain tools that make spoken Greek flow more smoothly (movable nu, elision). Vocabulary marked with ★ is compulsory memorization.

1. The present active verb: all six persons

1.1 Review: what does a verb form tell us?

Lesson 1 (section 3) showed that every Greek verb form encodes five feature bundles: person, number, tense, mood, voice. Lesson 1 used only the 3rd person; this lesson gives you all six persons of the first bundle:

FeatureThis lesson
Moodindicative – presents the action as a statement ("he worships")
Tensepresent (other tenses: later)
Voiceactive (passive, middle: later)
Person + numberall 6: 1st/2nd/3rd person × singular/plural

1.2 The full paradigm (model verb θεραπεύω)

PersonActive endingGreekTransliterationEnglish
1st sg.-ωθεραπεύωtherapeúōI worship
2nd sg.-ειςθεραπεύειςtherapeúeisyou (sg) worship
3rd sg.-ειθεραπεύειtherapeúeihe/she/it worships
1st pl.-ομενθεραπεύομενtherapeúomenwe worship
2nd pl.-ετεθεραπεύετεtherapeúeteyou (pl) worship
3rd pl.-ουσι(ν)θεραπεύουσι(ν)therapeúousi(n)they worship
Infinitive-εινθεραπεύεινtherapeúeinto worship

The routine for translating a verb form – it runs in your head every time you meet a new form:

StepIn your headOn paper
1. Split stem + endingθεραπεύ-ομεν (therapeú-omen) → stem "worship" + ending 1st person plural
2. Build the English sentence1st person plural = "we"We worship.

Pattern to reuse: the ending announces person + number, the stem announces the meaning: -ω = I, -εις = you, -ει = he/she, -ομεν = we, -ετε = you (pl), -ουσι(ν) = they. Because the person is already built into the ending, Greek does not need a subject pronoun (Lesson 1, section 7): the single word θεραπεύομεν is the whole sentence "We worship." Self-test: cover the verb ending – if you cannot state person + number, you are reading "lazily"; split stem/ending before translating.

Latin corner: Latin tags the person in exactly the same way: amo, amas, amat, amāmus, amātis, amant ↔ θεραπεύω, θεραπεύεις, θεραπεύει, θεραπεύομεν, θεραπεύετε, θεραπεύουσι(ν). The matching ending pairs: -m / -s / -t / -mus / -tis / -nt ↔ -ω / -εις / -ει / -ομεν / -ετε / -ουσι(ν). Anyone who knows amo by heart just has to "switch keyboards".

Vocabulary note: θεραπεύω (therapeúō) means "to worship (a god)" in the early lessons, but the dictionary also lists "to heal, to tend". The original sense is "to serve, to attend to": serving the gods → tending the sick. That root meaning still lives in modern English: therapy is a direct descendant of θεραπεύω. One verb, several meanings – choose by context.

Watch out: (1) Do not confuse -εις (you) with -ει (he/she): they differ by one iota. (2) The ending -ουσι(ν) is 3rd person plural; the 1st person plural is -ομεν – the two "ο" endings are easily mixed up when reading fast. (3) The (ν) in brackets is the movable nu – see Lesson 1 section 6 and section 9 below.

2. The vocative: addressing someone

The vocative is the case of direct address: you use it when directly calling the person (or thing) you are talking to. Three properties to engrave:

  • Its form usually matches the nominative – it does not always have its own ending.
  • It never carries an article.
  • It does no job inside the sentence (it is neither subject nor object): it stands detached from the rest, separated by a comma. For emphasis, put the particle ὦ (ō) in front – the equivalent of English "O" in "O friends!".
GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὦ φίλοιō phíloiO friends!

2.1 The important exception: masculine o-nouns in -ος

CaseSingularTransliterationPluralTransliteration
Nominativeὁ ἄνθρωποςho ánthrōposοἱ ἄνθρωποιhoi ánthrōpoi
Genitiveτοῦ ἀνθρώπουtoû anthrṓpouτῶν ἀνθρώπωνtôn anthrṓpōn
Dativeτῷ ἀνθρώπῳtōi anthrṓpōiτοῖς ἀνθρώποιςtoîs anthrṓpois
Accusativeτὸν ἄνθρωπονtòn ánthrōponτοὺς ἀνθρώπουςtoùs anthrṓpous
Vocativeὦ ἄνθρωπεō ánthrōpeὦ ἄνθρωποιō ánthrōpoi
  • Singular: a masculine noun in -ος changes its vocative to -ε: ὦ ἄνθρωπε – not ὦ ἄνθρωπος!
  • Plural: vocative = nominative: ὦ ἄνθρωποι. (The rule "usually matches the nominative" wins in the plural.)

Latin corner: Latin does exactly the same in the o-declension: dominus → domine! (as in the liturgical Domine). Both languages preserve the address ending -e from their common Indo-European ancestor. And ὦ is simply the "O…!" of direct address: "O my friend!"

Watch out: the vocative is not the subject! In ὦ φίλοι, τοὺς θεοὺς θεραπεύετε (ō phíloi, toùs theoùs therapeúete), the subject of θεραπεύετε is already inside the ending -ετε (= "you all"); ὦ φίλοι is only the address, detached by a comma. Self-test: try dropping the address phrase – if the remaining sentence is still complete in meaning and grammar, you were looking at a vocative.

3. The infinitive II: subject or object?

Lesson 1 (section 9) met the infinitive in one frame: after δεῖ. This lesson distinguishes two roles of the infinitive – this is a difference of sentence function, not of form (the form is always -ειν).

3.1 The infinitive as subject

GreekTransliterationEnglish
τοὺς θεοὺς θεραπεύειν δεῖ.toùs theoùs therapeúein deî.It is necessary to worship the gods. | One must worship the gods.

Check question: "What is necessary?" → precisely the act of "worshipping the gods" – the infinitive plays the role of subject of δεῖ.

3.2 The infinitive as object

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ φιλόσοφος τοὺς ἀνθρώπους πείθειν ἐθέλει.ho philósophos toùs anthrṓpous peíthein ethélei.The philosopher wants to persuade the humans.

Check question: "What does he want?" → the act of "persuading the humans" – the infinitive plays the role of object of ἐθέλω.

An object infinitive follows verbs whose meanings are: want, say, think, demand, can, understand, teach, learn – ἐθέλω (to want), λέγω (to say) (lesson 4), νομίζω (to think)…

Watch out: these two types are not the AcI (accusative + infinitive after "trigger" verbs – Lesson 4 (not yet)). From now on you must distinguish three kinds of infinitive: (1) as subject (after δεῖ), (2) as object (after ἐθέλω…), (3) the predicative infinitive inside an AcI (lesson 4). Test with the question: is there a "floating" accusative after the infinitive serving as its logical subject? If not → type (1) or (2).

Pattern to reuse: English has both frames too: "It is necessary to worship" (the infinitive belongs to a provisional "it" – subject role) and "he wants to persuade" (object infinitive). Each time you meet δεῖ + infinitive, think the frame "It is necessary to…" first; with ἐθέλω + infinitive, think "wants to…".

4. The double accusative

Some Greek verbs stand with two accusatives at once – divided into two types. This is the first time you see one case carrying two roles in the same sentence: do not panic – the two roles are told apart by word order and the article.

4.1 Type a: accusative of person + accusative of content

Frame: "teach someone something" – representative verb: διδάσκω (didáskō).

GreekTransliterationEnglish
πολλὰ(content) διδάσκει με(person) ὁ βίος.pollà didáskei me ho bíos.Life teaches me many things.

με (me) is the accusative of the pronoun "I" (ἐγώ) – pronouns are treated in full later (not yet); for this lesson just remember: με = "me" (as an object).

4.2 Type b: object + predicative noun in the accusative

Frame: "consider someone something" – representative verb: νομίζω (nomízō). Compare εἶναι + predicative noun (Lesson 1, section 8): here the predicative noun works the same way, but it steps down into the accusative to agree with the object.

GreekTransliterationEnglish
τοὺς ἵππους(object) φίλους(predicative) νομίζομεν.toùs híppous phílous nomízomen.We consider the horses (to be) friends.

The formal rule: the object comes with an article, the predicative noun has no article (exactly like εἶναι + predicative noun in Lesson 1).

Latin corner: Latin has both types: vita multa me docet (life teaches me many things – type a) and puto Socratem sapientem (I consider Socrates wise – type b, "object + predicative"). The English frame "consider A (to be) B" is type b as well.

Watch out: (1) The verb's meaning changes with the "frame" around it: νομίζω with one object = "to believe, think"; with a double accusative = "to consider (as)". (2) When translating you usually must add a connecting word: "consider… to be". Self-test: in a sentence with two accusative phrases, check the articles – the phrase without an article that still "stands freely" is the predicative noun; rearrange into the "object + predicative" frame, then pick the verb meaning ("consider as") to match.

5. Negation: οὐ / οὐκ / οὐχ

"Not" in Greek has three forms – chosen by the sound that follows (a process of sound assimilation). This is the first time you see Greek "pre-packaging" the pronunciation for the reader, in the same spirit as the movable nu (section 9):

FormTransliterationStands beforeExampleTransliteration
οὐoua consonantοὐ πείθειou peíthei
οὐκouka vowel (no rough breathing)οὐκ ἐθέλειouk ethélei
οὐχoucha vowel with rough breathingοὐχ ἥλιοςouch hḗlios

ἥλιος (hḗlios) = the sun, the god Helios: the initial ἡ carries a rough breathing, so the negation takes οὐχ.

Latin corner: Modern English does the same: a book but an apple – you choose a/an by the next sound. Greek goes one step further: it has a special form οὐχ for vowels with rough breathing (because the breathing is pronounced /h/). Latin negation is invariable: non before everything – Latin is "lazier" at this point.

Watch out – accent exception: after οὐκ (and some other sentence-initial words (details later)), the 3rd person singular of εἶναι exceptionally carries an acute on the first syllable: οὐκ ἔστι (ouk ésti) instead of ἐστί. When you meet this in a text, it is not a misprint – do not "correct" it! This is your first piece of evidence that Greek accents are not decoration.

6. τί / τι, ἄρα and the trio ἤ / ἡ / ἦ

6.1 τί and τι

τί; (tí) (always with an acute) = "what? why?" – an interrogative word; τι (ti) (no accent) = "something" – in essence an indeclinable word (treated in full in Lesson 5 (not yet)). Same letters, different accent, different job: the accent is real "script".

6.2 The particle ἄρα: circumflex question, acute conclusion

Next to the interrogative particle ἆρα with a circumflex (hinting at the answer the asker expects) there is the particle ἄρα with an acute = "so, then" (drawing a conclusion):

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ἆρα οὐ πείθεις;ára ou peítheis?You don't persuade (him), do you? (leading question, expecting "that's right")
οὐκ ἄρα ἄθεος ἐστίν.ouk ára átheos estín.So he is not godless. (conclusion)

6.3 The homophone trio: ἤ / ἡ / ἦ

WordTransliterationMeaningWord class
ἤ (acute)ḗ"or"; "than" (in comparisons)conjunction
ἡ (circumflex + rough breathing)hēfeminine article, nominative singular (lesson 3)article
ἦ (circumflex, smooth breathing)ê"was" (past of εἶναι) (lesson 6)verb

Watch out: this trio is pronounced identically (/ɛ̄/ long) – you can only tell them apart by eye, through the accents. Slow down wherever one of the three appears; it is one of many demonstrations of why the Greeks invented accents: so readers could distinguish by looking. Self-test: ἤ has the "slanted" acute → "or/than"; ἡ has the "round" circumflex → article; ἦ has a circumflex but no rough breathing → past verb.

Latin corner: the Latin interrogative particle num also "signals" the expected answer in advance (usually "no"); and since Latin has no accents, you never face the "same letters, different accent" trouble of ἤ/ἡ/ἦ – a consolation prize for Latin learners, and a reminder for Greek learners: accents are vocabulary, not decoration.

7. A vocabulary note: βασιλεύς

ὁ βασιλεύς (ho basileús) = king, ruler: a noun that does not belong to the o-group (its nominative singular -ευς has no -ος). At this stage you learn only its nominative form; the other cases follow in Lesson 18 (not yet). When you meet it in a text, read its function from word order and the verb; and do not invent other forms (e.g. an unlearned accusative) when writing your own sentences – arrange the sentence so that only the nominative is needed.

Pattern to reuse: the "know your limits" strategy for writing: a noun whose full paradigm you have not learned → use it only in the cases you know (βασιλεύς nominative only), or swap in a learned synonym (ὁ θεός, ὁ ἄνθρωπος, ὁ φίλος…). Use only what you have learned, and you will never write a wrong form.

8. Which case does a verb "take"?

Reminder: many – but not all – Greek verbs require the same case as their English counterparts. A "matching" example: worship takes subject + accusative ↔ θεραπεύω also takes subject + accusative.

Watch out – the classic "non-matching" example: "to accuse" in English takes a direct object: the humans accuse the philosopher. But Greek κατηγορέω (katēgoréō) takes the genitive: οἱ ἄνθρωποι τοῦ φιλοσόφου κατηγοροῦσιν (hoi ánthrōpoi toû philosóphou katēgoroûsin) – literally "the humans accuse of the philosopher". In your vocabulary notebook this difference is always recorded as: "κατηγορέω + Gen.". General principle: learn each verb together with its case – verb and case form one unit (the genitive as object: Lesson 4 (not yet)).

9. Avoiding the vowel clash (hiatus): movable nu & elision

9.1 What is the vowel clash?

Hiatus = two vowels meeting: one at the end of a word, one at the start of the next: ἀλλὰ ἄλλους (allà állous). Greek treats this as a "rhythm break" when speaking, so it has two avoidance strategies.

9.2 Strategy 1: add a consonant – movable nu

Review Lesson 1: the endings -σι and -ει take an optional ν when the next word begins with a vowel: θεραπεύουσιν ἄλλους (with ν) / θεραπεύουσι (without). The same mechanism as ἐστί(ν), εἰσί(ν).

9.3 Strategy 2: drop a vowel – elision

A short final vowel is dropped when the next word starts with a vowel, marked with an apostrophe:

Without elisionWith elisionTransliterationEnglish
ἀλλὰ ἐγώἀλλ' ἐγώall' egṓbut I

The accent of the first word usually vanishes with the dropped vowel (compare: ἀλλά with its grave, ἀλλ' without any accent).

Watch out – two traps:

  • Voiceless stop (τ, κ, π) + elision + rough breathing → aspirated consonant (θ, χ, φ): instead of μετὰ ἡμῶν Greek writes μεθ' ἡμῶν (meth' hēmôn) "with us": the τ of μετά now "carries the breath" of the dropped vowel and becomes θ. This is not a new word – it is the same word!
  • Some words are never elided, most importantly: περί, πρό, ὅτι, τι, τί (perí, pró, hóti, ti, tí) – they stay intact even before a vowel.

Pattern to reuse: you have met elision countless times in French: le + homme → l'homme; Greek does exactly the same: ἀλλὰ ἐγώ → ἀλλ' ἐγώ. When you meet an apostrophe in a text: "re-inflate" the lost vowel in your head before checking a dictionary – ἀλλ' = ἀλλά, μεθ' = μετά. No dictionary has an entry "ἀλλ" or "μεθ"!

Logical stopping point: you have just upgraded from "can read a sentence" (Lesson 1) to "can read fluently": all six persons (section 1), direct address (section 2), the infinitive in two roles (section 3), the double accusative (section 4), negation by sound (section 5), the homophone trio told apart (section 6), and smooth pronunciation via movable nu + elision (section 9). Now put everything together in a reading about the most famous trial in history.

10. Reading: Socrates on trial (simplified, from Plato)

Source: theme based on Plato, Apology 26c–27a – the passage where Socrates defends himself against the two charges of "corrupting the young" and "not worshipping the gods". The original (public domain) can be read on Perseus. The version below was rewritten by this project for the level of Lesson 2 – it uses only structures and vocabulary already learned.

Simplified version (composed by this project):

ὦ ἄνθρωπε, οὐ πείθεις τοὺς ἀνθρώπους. ὁ Σωκράτης οὐ φθείρει τοὺς νέους· πολλὰ γὰρ διδάσκει. ὁ Σωκράτης τοὺς θεοὺς θεραπεύει· οὐκ ἄρα ἄθεος ἐστίν. τὸν Σωκράτη σοφὸν νομίζομεν.

Ō ánthrōpe, ou peítheis toùs anthrṓpous. Ho Sōkrátēs ou phtheírei toùs néous· pollà gàr didáskei. Ho Sōkrátēs toùs theoùs therapeúei· ouk ára átheos estín. Tòn Sōkrátē sophòn nomízomen.

English (close translation)
O human, you do not persuade the humans. Socrates does not corrupt the young; for he teaches many things. Socrates worships the gods; so he is not godless. We consider Socrates wise.

Quick analysis (check with the tools you have learned):

  • ὦ ἄνθρωπε – vocative singular of an -ος noun: ending -ε, with the particle ὦ, no article, detached by a comma.
  • οὐ πείθεις – negation before the consonant π → οὐ; the ending -εις = 2nd person singular: the subject "you" is built into the ending.
  • πολλὰ γὰρ διδάσκει – γάρ is postpositive (second slot); πολλά = "many things", an accusative of content without article, object of διδάσκει.
  • οὐκ ἄρα ἄθεος ἐστίν – οὐκ before the vowel ἄ- (no rough breathing); ἄρα with the acute = "so" (conclusion, section 6); ἄθεος is a predicative noun without article after ἐστίν.
  • τὸν Σωκράτη σοφὸν νομίζομεν – double accusative type b: τὸν Σωκράτη (object, with article) + σοφόν (predicative noun, no article) + νομίζω = "consider as".

Reading comprehension questions: (1) Which negation does the second sentence use, and why that form? (2) What accent does ἄρα carry in the reading, and what role does it play? (3) Read the original Plato, Apology 26c–27a on Perseus: into what contradiction does Socrates maneuver his accuser Meletus through the questions about the gods, and how does the simplified version compress that argument?

Answers to the reading questions (print to check)

1. οὐ φθείρει – before the consonant φ Greek uses plain οὐ, no -κ needed. 2. The acute → the conclusion particle "so": Socrates worships the gods, so he is not godless. (The circumflex ἆρα asks a leading question – not used in this reading.) 3. In the original, Socrates asks question after question until Meletus contradicts himself: at one point he says Socrates believes in new divine beings (so he must believe in gods), at another that he is a complete atheist – the two halves of the same indictment cannot both be true. The simplified version compresses that argument into two sentences: one negation (he does not corrupt the young) + one conclusion (so he is not godless).

13. Exercises

Answers appear only when printing (Ctrl+P → Save as PDF) – work through them first, then print to check.

Exercise 1 – Conjugate and translate

  1. Conjugate πείθω (peíthō) through all 6 persons + the infinitive, with transliteration.
  2. Translate using the "stem + ending" routine: θεραπεύομεν, πείθετε, θεραπεύουσι(ν).

Answer 1

  1. πείθω (peíthō) I persuade · πείθεις (peítheis) you persuade · πείθει (peíthei) he/she persuades · πείθομεν (peíthomen) we persuade · πείθετε (peíthete) you (pl) persuade · πείθουσι(ν) (peíthousi(n)) they persuade · infinitive: πείθειν (peíthein) to persuade.
  2. θεραπεύομεν → ending -ομεν = 1st person plural: "we worship"; πείθετε → -ετε = 2nd person plural: "you (pl) persuade"; θεραπεύουσι(ν) → -ουσι(ν) = 3rd person plural: "they worship".

Exercise 2 – Vocative

  1. Write the vocative singular and plural of ὁ φίλος (with the address particle).
  2. Translate: ὦ φίλοι, τοὺς θεοὺς θεραπεύετε!
  3. Find and correct the error: ὦ ἄνθρωπος

Answer 2

  1. ὦ φίλε! (ō phíle) (singular: ending -ε because it is a masculine -ος noun) · ὦ φίλοι! (ō phíloi) (plural = nominative).
  2. "O friends, worship the gods!" – ὦ φίλοι is a vocative (detached, no article, not translated as an extra word); the subject "you (pl)" is already inside the ending -ετε.
  3. Correct is ὦ ἄνθρωπε – the vocative singular of a masculine -ος noun takes -ε, it does not keep -ος.

Exercise 3 – Infinitive: subject or object?

Determine whether the infinitive is a subject or an object (with the check question), then translate:

  1. τοὺς ἀνθρώπους πείθειν δεῖ.
  2. ὁ βασιλεὺς τοὺς θεοὺς θεραπεύειν ἐθέλει.
  3. πολλὰ διδάσκειν ἐθέλομεν.

Answer 3

  1. Subject (ask: "what is necessary?" – precisely the persuading). – "One must persuade the humans." (τοὺς ἀνθρώπους is the object of the infinitive.)
  2. Object (ask: "what does the king want?" – the worshipping). – "The king wants to worship the gods."
  3. Object (after ἐθέλομεν = "we want"). – "We want to teach many things." (πολλά = accusative of content, no article.)

Exercise 4 – Double accusative

  1. Translate and label the two accusatives: πολλὰ διδάσκει με ὁ βίος.
  2. Translate and label object / predicative noun: τοὺς ἵππους φίλους νομίζομεν.
  3. Translate into Greek: "We consider life (to be) a good thing." (hint: use τὸν βίον + ἀγαθόν + νομίζω)

Answer 4

  1. "Life teaches me many things." – με = accusative of person, πολλά = accusative of content; type a ("teach someone something").
  2. "We consider the horses friends." – τοὺς ἵππους = object (with article), φίλους = predicative noun (no article); type b ("consider someone something").
  3. τὸν βίον ἀγαθὸν νομίζομεν. (tòn bión agathòn nomízomen.) – object with article + predicative noun without article + νομίζω = "consider as".

Exercise 5 – οὐ / οὐκ / οὐχ and ἔστι

Fill in the correct negation (and mind the accent in the last sentence):

  1. ___ πείθει.
  2. ___ ἐθέλει.
  3. ___ ἥλιος ἐστίν. (That is not Helios.)
  4. ___ βασιλεύς, ἀλλὰ ἄνθρωπος ἐστίν. (Not a king, but a human.)
  5. οὐκ ___ (εἶναι, 3rd person singular)

Answer 5

  1. οὐ πείθει. – before the consonant π.
  2. οὐκ ἐθέλει. – before the vowel ἐ- (no rough breathing).
  3. οὐχ ἥλιος ἐστίν. – ἥλιος carries a rough breathing → οὐχ.
  4. οὐ βασιλεύς, ἀλλὰ ἄνθρωπος ἐστίν. – before the consonant β; since ἀλλά stands before the vowel ἄ- it may also be written elided: ἀλλ' ἄνθρωπος.
  5. οὐκ ἔστι. – exception: after οὐκ the acute moves to ἐ- (not οὐκ ἐστί).

Exercise 6 – Avoiding hiatus

  1. Apply elision: ἀλλὰ ἐγώ → ?
  2. Apply the τ→θ rule: μετὰ ἡμῶν → ? (translate the whole phrase)
  3. Choose with/without movable nu: θεραπεύουσι(ν) before ἄλλους → ? and at the end of a sentence → ?
  4. Which of the following is never elided: ἀλλά, περί, μετά, ὅτι, οὐ?

Answer 6

  1. ἀλλ' ἐγώ (all' egṓ) – the final vowel of ἀλλά is dropped, the apostrophe marks it; the grave accent vanishes with it.
  2. μεθ' ἡμῶν (meth' hēmôn) – "with us": the τ of μετά becomes θ before the rough breathing of ἡμῶν.
  3. θεραπεύουσιν ἄλλους (nu added before the vowel); at the end of a sentence: θεραπεύουσι (no nu).
  4. περί and ὅτι (locked together with πρό, τι, τί) – never elided; ἀλλά, μετά, οὐ elide normally before vowels.

Exercise 7 – Putting it all together

Translate the following passage, labeling the phenomena you have learned (vocative, infinitive, double accusative, οὐκ ἔστι, particles):

ὦ φίλοι, δεῖ τὸν βίον θεραπεύειν. ὁ γὰρ βίος πολλὰ διδάσκει· ὁ δὲ βασιλεὺς οὐκ ἔστι φιλόσοφος. τὸν βίον ἀγαθὸν νομίζομεν.

Answer 7

"O friends, one must care for (worship) life. For life teaches many things; and the king is not a philosopher. We consider life a good thing."

  • ὦ φίλοι: vocative plural (= nominative), detached, no article.
  • δεῖ τὸν βίον θεραπεύειν: subject infinitive after δεῖ; τὸν βίον is the object of the infinitive (accusative with article → not a predicative noun).
  • ὁ γὰρ βίος: γάρ postpositive (second slot); πολλά accusative of content without article.
  • ὁ δὲ βασιλεύς οὐκ ἔστι φιλόσοφος: δέ postpositive; βασιλεύς in the nominative only (within the learned scope); οὐκ ἔστι accent exception; φιλόσοφος predicative noun without article.
  • τὸν βίον ἀγαθὸν νομίζομεν: double accusative type b – object with article + predicative noun without article + νομίζω = "consider as".
