1. What is the subjunctive? The map of the four moods of the Greek verb

Every verb form you have conjugated so far – present, imperfect, future, aorist, perfect – belongs to the indicative: the mood of reporting, drawing the event "as it is / as it was / as it will be". But language also needs to speak about things that are not yet: wanting to do something in order to reach a result, setting a condition "if tomorrow is sunny", giving an order "don't do it!". Those regions of meaning are the land of the other moods.

Tense answers when / from which angle the event is viewed; mood answers where the speaker stands while speaking: reporting reality (indicative), issuing commands (imperative), or opening up a possibility not yet real (subjunctive, optative). The two axes are independent: each mood has its own set of "tenses" – and the subjunctive needs only two of them, because "in order that / if" does not measure a point in time but an aspect (Lesson 13): is the event spread out, or complete in a single stroke?

The Latin name subiunctīvus (literally "sub-joined, arranged beneath") describes its position exactly: the subjunctive almost never stands on its own – it is subordinate, growing inside dependent clauses opened by ἵνα "in order that", ἐάν "if", ὅταν "whenever". Later Greek grammarians call it ὑποτακτική – a Greek word that translates subiunctīvus exactly ("arranged beneath"), showing how two grammatical traditions described the same thing in two languages.

An image to remember: the indicative draws a photograph – person, event, and time all fixed. The subjunctive holds up a mirror facing forward: the event has not happened; it is only "placed" before the eyes so that one can weigh whether to do it, what for, and what follows if one does. That is why it travels with three big questions: for what purpose? (purpose), what if? (condition), should we? (deliberation). When you meet these three regions of meaning, your eyes must go looking for a subjunctive on their own.

Latin corner: learners of Latin meet the subjunctive much earlier, because Latin owns it: ut veniat "that he come", nē dicat "that he not speak", quid faciam? "what should I do?", si hoc dicat "if he were to say this". Translated into Greek, the four sentences match almost word for word: ἵνα ἔλθῃ, ἵνα μὴ λέγῃ, τί ποιῶμεν;, ἐὰν τοῦτο λέγῃ. All four of today's major uses (purpose, condition, deliberation, prohibition) Latin expresses with the subjunctive – learning today is partly meeting an old acquaintance.

2. Active forms: present & aorist – the "long-vowel endings" and "no augment" rules

Building the subjunctive is astonishingly easy, because it reuses everything you already have. The active formula:

  • Present: take the present stem (familiar since Lesson 2) and lengthen the thematic vowel: -ο- → -ω-, -ε- → -η-. The endings therefore look "grown up" next to the indicative endings: -ω, -ῃς, -ῃ, -ωμεν, -ητε, -ωσι(ν).
  • Aorist: take the aorist stem (Lesson 13: λυσ- or a thematic stem like λαβ-), drop the augment, and add exactly that long set of endings.

Achtung – 1st singular λύσω collides with the future: the aorist subjunctive λύσω and the future indicative λύσω are written exactly alike (both without augment, both in -ω). Tell them apart by another person of the same paradigm (future λύσεις vs subjunctive λύσῃς) and by context: a λύσω after ἐάν/ἵνα cannot be a future. This is one of the two most dangerous twin pairs of the course – the other pair (λύσει vs λύσῃ) waits in section 4.

Pattern to carry over – "long endings, no augment" buys a whole mood: (1) Want to build a form? Take the familiar stem (present or aorist), drop the augment, lengthen the thematic vowel. (2) Want to recognise one? Look for long η/ω in exactly the thematic-vowel slot, with no initial ἐ-. (3) Want to look a form up in a dictionary? Cut the long ending, return the σ to the present stem (the strategy of section 4). Self-check: write out the two chains λύω, λύῃς, λύῃ, λύωμεν, λύητε, λύωσι(ν) and λύσω, λύσῃς, λύσῃ, λύσωμεν, λύσητε, λύσωσι(ν) on paper – if you own these two chains, 90% of the subjunctive forms in real texts are just these chains in different clothes.

Achtung – forms that look identical and cannot be told apart: (1) subjunctive τιμᾷ (2nd and 3rd person) is written exactly like indicative τιμᾷ (3rd person) – only the words around it can decide; (2) subjunctive τιμᾶτε collides with indicative τιμᾶτε; (3) ἦτε of εἰμί collides with the imperfect ἦτε "you were" (Lesson 11). The lesson to take: with contract verbs and εἰμί do not trust the shape – read the whole clause for ἵνα/ἐάν/ὅταν/μὴ before you rule on the mood.

One more for the "recognise by sight" shelf: the passive aorist (Lesson 14) also has its own subjunctive set, built by the same rule: ἐλύθην → λυθῶ, λυθῇς, λυθῇ, λυθῶμεν, λυθῆτε, λυθῶσι(ν) – "that it be released / if it be released". The thematic vowel still lengthens and stands after the long stem -θη-. In today's Reading II you will meet exactly one form of this family (φανῶσιν); the full passive system will be put on the map in Review #4.

The most dangerous twin pair: λύσει "he will release" (future) vs λύσῃ "that/if he release (in one complete act)" – the difference lies exactly in the ending vowel: plain short ει vs long ῃ with its iota subscript, while the accent stays quietly in its old place. Ancient scribes made a profession of telling these two words apart; you will have to do the same, but with one advantage: context.

The "peeling the subjunctive" strategy for the dictionary – four steps, extending the "peeling the aorist" strategy of Lesson 13:

  1. Meet a strange form: for example λυθῶσιν, ἀγάγῃ, μάθῃ, εἴπῃ.
  2. Peel the long ending: cut -ω/-ῃ/-ῃς/-ωμεν/-ητε/-ωσι(ν) (and remember: there is no augment to peel – the subjunctive never has one).
  3. Return the σ to its stem: if a σ remains, it is the sigmatic aorist stem → go back to the present via Lesson 13 (λυσ- → λύω); if not, compare the stem with the by-sight table of thematic aorists (λαβ-/μαθ-/εἰπ-/αγαγ-).
  4. Read the sentence again for sense: confirm with the signal word (ἵνα? ἐάν? μὴ? ὅταν? or a 1st-person question?).

Pattern to carry over – "long vowel = the not-yet-real world": in the indicative the thematic vowel is always short (λύομεν); lengthening it (λύωμεν) is the step into the world of "not yet happened". The same amplifying trick works for every verb: παιδεύω → παιδεύῃ, ποιέω → ποιῇ, εἰμί → ᾖ. End-of-section self-check: read the two sentences ὁ παῖς λύει and ἵνα ὁ παῖς λύῃ aloud – if your ear can "hear" the difference between short ει and long ῃ, you already own the ancient Greek reader's skill for the subjunctive.

Latin corner: Latin is so fond of the subjunctive that even its passive uses it (amētur "that he be loved"), while Greek keeps the two middle/passive lanes (λυθῇ vs λύσηται) more sharply distinct. One parallel worth noting: Latin sī + indicative for real conditions, but ut/nē + subjunctive for purpose – Greek draws exactly the same boundary (εἰ vs ἵνα/ἵνα μή); see sections 5–6.

5. ἄν and ἐάν: two splashes of colour in the world of "if"

ἄν is a particle: an invariant word that cannot be translated by any single meaning; it only throws a "flavour" over the clause that contains it – the flavour of possibility, indeterminacy, non-reality. Picture ἄν as a shard of coloured glass: attach it to any window and the view behind it changes colour. The four "windows" it most often attaches to:

ἐάν is a crasis of εἰ + ἄν (two words fused into one; you met the crasis προὐ- in Lesson 12; poetry and the Ionic dialect also keep the variant ἤν). When it does not fuse, the position rule for ἄν is: it clings immediately after the first word of the clause it belongs to – which is why you almost always see ὃς ἄν, ὡς ἂν stuck together.

The division of labour between the two brothers εἰ and ἐάν – one of the most important decisions of the complex sentence:

The division comes straight from usage: Greek prose uses both, but each keeps its own ground – εἰ asks "suppose it is true, then what?" (a concrete condition, past or present alike), ἐάν looks forward with "if tomorrow…" or packs up a whole series of occasions with "whenever…". The pair is present in every kind of text – from Herodotus' Histories to the Greek Bible, where ἐὰν + subjunctive is one of the densest constructions.

Read the three sentences above with the eyes of section 2: βοηθῇ, λέγῃς, ᾖ – three long endings, three subjunctives; and each "if" clause has its pay-off calculated in the main clause (νικήσομεν is future – see: the future indicative lives in the main clause, the subjunctive lives in the subordinate clause; the two moods divide the two ends of a complex sentence between themselves).

Achtung – do not write ἐὰν twice: since ἐάν = εἰ + ἄν already contains the shard of coloured glass, repeating "ἐὰν…ἄν" in the same pair of clauses is the classic beginner's error (it would be read as "εἰ + ἄν + ἄν"). After ἐάν, the main clause usually needs no ἄν when the verb is a future indicative. In theory, other "unreal" condition pairs do exist (main clause also bearing ἄν) – but that is the territory of Lesson 21; there you will see what ἄν does in the main clause, and why we do not need it today.

Pattern to carry over – "each signal word owns its mood": getting to know today's three signal words means owning three master keys: see ἵνα/ὅπως/ὡς (purpose) → expect a subjunctive; see ἐάν/ὅταν (condition / indefinite time) → expect a subjunctive; see μὴ before a verb (prohibition, section 7) → expect a subjunctive or an imperative. The signal word stands at the head of the subordinate clause; the verb of that clause switches its mood accordingly. Self-check: cover the translations of the three sentences in the table above and look only at the Greek – if you can "hear" the expectation "what will follow the signal word", this section has done its job.

6. Purpose clauses: ἵνα / ὅπως / ὡς + subjunctive

Now for use number one of the new mood. A purpose clause (also called a final clause) answers the question "for what purpose?" – the speaker announces the goal of an action in advance: "I am sending the boy so that he fetch the wine". Three openers, one job:

  • ἵνα – the most widespread word, in prose and in the Bible alike. Its origin will make you smile: ἵνα is originally the neuter of the relative pronoun (Lesson 15!) meaning "to the place where / in which" – and then "purpose" = "(so) that that may happen". Lesson 15 and Lesson 16 are therefore not two separate worlds: a relative clause describes what is already there, a purpose clause sets up what is wanted – one pronoun, its two poles.
  • ὅπως – originally "how" (a relative of πῶς "how", Lesson 2), very common after verbs of "seeing to it, taking care": σκόπει ὅπως μάθῃς "see to it (that) you learn".
  • ὡς – the short, slightly literary form; careful not to confuse it with ὡς "as" (comparison, section 8).

The negative of a purpose clause is μή – remember the rule drilled in Lessons 4/7: οὐ negates reality, μή negates will/purpose: ἵνα μή = "so that… not / lest". Never ἵνα οὐ.

GreekTransliterationEnglish
πέμπω τὸν παῖδα, ἵνα τὸν οἶνον ἀγάγῃ.pémpō tòn paîda, hína tòn oînon agágē(i).I send the boy in order that he fetch the wine.
ἔκρυψεν τὰς βοῦς, ἵνα μὴ φανῶσιν.ékrypsen tàs boûs, hína mḕ phanôsin.She hid the cattle so that they not be seen.

Three observations for reading correctly:

  • A purpose clause whose subject differs from the main action is the natural habitat of this clause type (I send the boy, so that he fetch the wine). With the same subject, Greek usually chooses the infinitive: "I come to help" = ἔρχομαι βοηθῆσαι – an aorist infinitive of purpose, exactly the infinitive-after-willing-verbs structure you have practised since Lessons 2/4.
  • Present or aorist? Ask the aspect (Lesson 13): present = the purpose as ongoing, aorist = the purpose as one complete event. In practice prose uses the aorist more often; do not be alarmed to see ἵνα ἀγάγῃ (aorist ἀγάγῃ!) instead of ἄγῃ.
  • Do not translate "so that he will": the subjunctive has no future. The meaning of "that / so that" already contains the forward direction; in English "so that he fetch" is enough.

Latin corner: Latin expresses exactly this formula with ut/ne + coniunctīvus: mittō puerum ut vīnum dūcat ↔ πέμπω τὸν παῖδα ἵνα τὸν οἶνον ἀγάγῃ – word for word. Both languages press the "so that" sense of a subordinating axis into service; Latin ut was even originally a relative pronoun (just like ἵνα!), so the parallel reaches down to the common root.

A 3-step pattern – writing a purpose clause: (1) Find the main action and its subject. (2) Ask: by whom is the purpose to be carried out, and in which aspect (ongoing / complete)? (3) Open with ἵνα, put the verb in the correct person of the subjunctive – for "so that not", insert μή immediately after ἵνα. Self-check while reading: a clause opened by ἵνα whose verb turns out to be indicative → you have almost certainly misread the form (unless it is an infinitive after a verb requiring an infinitive – a true purpose clause never uses the indicative).

7. Prohibitions & the deliberative question: μὴ + subjunctive; τί ποιῶμεν;

Lessons 4/10 taught commands and the imperative way of forbidding with the present imperative: μὴ λῦε "don't release (this in general)". The subjunctive completes the missing part of the system: μὴ + aorist subjunctive – and two more uses reserved for the 1st person.

StructureExampleNuance
μὴ + aorist subj.μὴ λύσῃς!forbidding a single, complete act: "don't (go and) release (this time / when it happens)!"
μὴ + present imperativeμὴ λῦε!forbidding a habit / state: "don't (keep) releasing (like that)!"
μὴ + 3rd-person aorist subj.μὴ λύσῃ!the 3rd person has no other negative command: "he must not release!"

The pair μὴ λύσῃς / μὴ λῦε is aspect (Lesson 13) knocking for the third time: the same "don't" – the aorist views the act as one complete decision, the present as a habit stretching on. In real texts: μὴ φοβηθῇς "do not be afraid (of what is coming)" – and you will never see οὐ open a prohibition: οὐ is for facts, μή for the will.

Two 1st-person uses – two unmistakable "voices" of Greek:

  • The deliberative question: when the speaker asks himself (or his group) "what should we do?", Greek does not use the indicative but the 1st-person subjunctive: τί ποιῶμεν; "What should we do?" – the classic line of dialogue, occurring hundreds of times in Plato and the comedies of Aristophanes; πῶς εἴπω; "How am I to put it?". Compare: τί ποιοῦμεν; (indicative) merely asks a factual "what are we (now) doing?".
  • The hortatory use: a 1st-person subjunctive without a question mark is an invitation "let's…": ἐρωτῶμεν αὐτόν "let us ask him", ἴωμεν! "Let's go!" – the most famous rallying cry of Greek, from the camp of the Ten Thousand (Xenophon, Anabasis, a relative of Lesson 12's reading) to every translation of the Bible. (ἴωμεν is the 1st-person plural subjunctive of εἶμι "to go" – a companion verb of ἔρχομαι, to be learned by sight; it will be dissected in Lesson 20.)

Latin corner: all three uses are covered exactly by the Latin mould: nē + perfect coniunctīvus (μὴ λύσῃς ↔ nē solverīs), quid faciamus? (τί ποιῶμεν;), eamus! (ἴωμεν!). For the parallel learner this is one of the rare moments where the two grammatical systems coincide person for person – memorise one side, and the other side comes free.

Pattern to carry over – 1st person = "the door opens inward": the 1st-person indicative reports ("I do"); the 1st-person subjunctive deliberates with itself or makes a proposal ("should we / let's"). When translating, give the sentence an interior breath: τί ποιῶμεν; is not "what are we doing?" but "what should we do?". Self-check: if a 1st-person question still makes sense with "should / shall we" inserted → subjunctive; if it needs "are/were doing" → indicative.

8. Extending Lesson 15: ὅς ἄν, ὅταν, ὡς ἂν – the "whoever / whenever" formulas

Lesson 15 gave you the relative pronoun ὅς "the one which/who" – pointing back to an identified noun. Today one single shard of ἄν is enough to "sweep" the whole system across into indefinite meaning – "whoever, whatever, whenever, as if" – exactly as promised by the "coloured glass" table of section 5:

PhraseMeaningGreekEnglish
ὅς ἄν / ὅστις ἄν + subj."whoever"ὃς ἂν τὴν ἀλήθειαν λέγῃ, εὐδαίμων ἐστίν.Whoever speaks the truth is happy.
ὅ τι ἄν + subj."whatever"ὅ τι ἂν λέγῃς, ἀκούομεν.Whatever you say, we hear it.

Read rows 3–4 as a test: ὅταν + subjunctive packs up a whole series of occasions not fixed in advance; ὅτε + indicative anchors one real occasion. The full system of temporal clauses gets its own lesson (Lesson 22); today you need exactly one thing: see ὅταν → expect a subjunctive; see ὅτε → expect an indicative.

ὡς ἄν "as if" does a completely different job: it tints a comparison. μέσος δὲ ὁ κράτιστος, ὡς ἂν κορυφαῖος χοροῦ (Reading I): the best place goes to the best man, after the manner of the chorus-leader standing mid-chorus – ἄν throws in the nuance "alike but not the same", exactly like the English "as it were".

The combined message of sections 5+8 – "one shard of ἄν, four uses": ἐάν (if / whenever), ὅταν (whenever), ὅς ἄν (whoever), ὡς ἂν (as if) – four clusters, four meanings, but one single move: attach ἄν to the opening word and pull the verb into the subjunctive. This is one of the biggest "buy one, get four" patterns of Greek grammar; review it by writing the four clusters from memory in their correct order and reading them aloud as one complex sentence.

Self-check, end of the grammar part: say aloud (or write down) today's four gate phrases – ἵνα μάθῃς (purpose), ἐὰν ᾖ (condition), μὴ λύσῃς (prohibition), τί ποιῶμεν; (deliberation) – and ask yourself for each: which (1) signal word, (2) person, (3) present or aorist does it contain? If the three answers come within a second each, you are ready for the two readings – where all four appear in their true shape, in the real Greek of Posidonius and Parthenius.

Quick analysis (checked with the tools of this lesson):

  • ἐὰν δὲ ᾖ τι δυσαπόσπαστον, μαχαιρίῳ μικρῷ παρατέμνουσιν: an "any time that" condition – ἐάν + ᾖ (3rd-person subjunctive of εἰμί, section 3), while the main clause keeps the present indicative παρατέμνουσιν "they cut".   • ὅταν δὲ πλείονες συνδειπνῶσι: "whenever" – ὅταν (= ὅτε + ἄν, section 5) + συνδειπνῶσι(ν) (3rd-person plural subjunctive). Compare: if Posidonius were reporting one specific dinner, the clause would be ὅτε συνεδείπνουν + indicative (section 8).

  • ἵνα πάντες πίνωσιν: a purpose clause (section 6) – the subjects differ: the boy carries the cup round, so that everyone may drink; πίνωσι(ν) long ending, exactly by the rule.
  • κρέα δὲ πολλὰ ἐστίν: neuter plural subject + singular verb (Lesson 3) – as in the original text; κρέα is the plural of τὸ κρέας, an s-stem neuter (Lesson 8), and ἐστίν stays singular by the "neuter plural travels light" rule.

  • προτίθενται: the athematic verb προτίθημι "to set out" – learn by sight, together with the family δίδωμι/ἔδωκεν (Lesson 15) and τίθημι; the full dissection comes in Lesson 23. Note the build: prefix προ- + reduplication τι- + root θη- (Lesson 23 will dissect it).

The two subjunctive sentences stay almost word for word: ἐὰν δὲ ᾖ τι δυσαπόσπαστον, μαχαιρίῳ μικρῷ παρατέμνοντες and ὅταν δὲ πλείονες συνδειπνῶσι, κάθηνται μὲν ἐν κύκλῳ, μέσος δὲ ὁ κράτιστος ὡς ἂν κορυφαῖος χοροῦ, διαφέρων τῶν ἄλλων ἢ κατὰ τὴν πολεμικὴν εὐχέρειαν ἢ κατὰ γένος ἢ κατὰ πλοῦτον – "surpassing the others either in martial skill, or in lineage, or in wealth". General grammar points to remember: three subjunctive moulds in a single passage: ἐὰν + subjunctive for a repeated condition (ᾖ, sentence 3), ὅταν (ὅτε + ἄν) for repeated time (συνδειπνῶσι, sentence 4), and ἵνα for purpose (πίνωσιν, sentence 6). Recognise them by the long endings, against the indicative πίνουσιν of sentence 5.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1: ἐὰν leads ᾖ (3rd-person singular subjunctive of εἰμί); ὅταν leads συνδειπνῶσι; ἵνα leads πίνωσιν.

Step 2: the three clauses: ἐὰν ᾖ is the condition "any time there is a piece hard to tear off", with the main clause παρατέμνουσιν keeping the present indicative; ὅταν συνδειπνῶσι is "whenever"; ἵνα πάντες πίνωσιν is a purpose clause, whose subject (πάντες) differs from the main-clause subject (ὁ παῖς). The three μὲν δὲ pairs: little bread against much meat (C2); sitting in a circle against the best man in the middle (C4); the rich with wine against the many with beer (C5): both menu and seating draw a horizontal line of social rank.

Step 3, directions for discussion: the exact name (κόρμα), the little knife, the seating in a circle show that the writer had witnessed the scene or was citing a source; but the split "the rich, the many" is the outsider's lens. Today, a trustworthy report about a different culture needs correct names, verifiable details and named sources; read in reverse, it also tells us where the writer stood.

  • ἀφίκετο: the aorist of ἀφικνέομαι "to arrive" – deponent, learn by sight; the original uses exactly this form.
  • τῷ δὲ ἦν θυγάτηρ: the possessive dative "he had (a) daughter" – the exact pay-off of Lesson 1; τῷ is the dative of ὁ Βρεταννός (the subject needs no mention, context supplies it).
  • ἐρασθεῖσα: the passive aorist participle "after falling passionately in love" – the full toolkit comes in Lesson 19; today you only need to see that it packs two ideas: having fallen in love (aorist) + a feminine subject (the girl).
  • ἔκρυψεν ἵνα μὴ φανῶσιν: negative purpose with ἵνα μή (section 6); φανῶσιν = the passive aorist subjunctive of φαίνομαι "to appear" (Lesson 14: ἐφάνην) – the long ending -ωσιν exactly by the rule; the indicative would be ἐφάνησαν.
  • ἐμὲ vs ἐμός: a pronoun to respect! This is ἐμέ (the accusative of ἐγώ, whose stem ἐγ- becomes ἐμ- after a vowel): "kiss me". Written τὸν ἐμὸν it would be "kiss mine" (possessive adjective, Lesson 15) – the classic error of beginning translators.
  • ἀποδώσω ... ἐὰν φιλῇς: future main clause in -σω (Lesson 12) + the future condition ἐάν + subjunctive φιλῇς – the momentum of section 5: "I shall give (them) back, if you kiss (me)" (an imagined future, not yet certain).
  • ἐβούλετο ... ἀνασώσασθαι: the deponent imperfect ἐβούλετο (Lesson 10) paints the background "he wanted" + the aorist middle infinitive ἀνασώσασθαι "to recover (for himself)" – the original uses exactly this form.
  • ἐθαύμαζεν: the imperfect paints the background "he kept marvelling" (Lesson 11) – against the aorist ἐθαύμασεν "he (suddenly) marvelled".
  • αὐτοῖς ἐγένετο παῖς: possessive dative + ἐγενόμην (the Lesson 13 pay-off) – "a son was born to them"; original: καὶ αὐτοῖς χρόνου περιήκοντος γενέσθαι παῖδα Κελτόν.
  • ἀφ' οὗ δὴ οἱ Κελτοὶ προσηγορεύθησαν: ἀφ' οὗ = "from (the moment) which" – elision before a vowel, the exact precedent of παρ' οὓς (Lesson 13); προσηγορεύθησαν is the 3rd-person passive aorist (the Lesson 14 pay-off): "they were named" – the Celts did not name themselves; the name came to them from the story.

General grammar points to remember: ὅτε + aorist indicative (ἤγαγεν) narrates one completed occasion, the counterpart of ὅταν "whenever" (section 8 of this lesson); the negative purpose clause ἵνα μὴ φανῶσιν is a passive aorist subjunctive, stressing the outcome "be seen"; the possessive dative τῷ (sentence 2) marks the owner; and the passive προσηγορεύθησαν (sentence 6) suits an aition: the people receive a name, they do not coin it themselves.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1: ὅτε in sentence 1 goes with the aorist indicative ἤγαγεν because it tells of one journey that was completed, not a repeated habit; the purpose clause: ἵνα μὴ φανῶσιν (keeping the cattle from being seen); the condition: ἐὰν (...) φιλῇς (inside Keltinē's words); ἐρασθεῖσα is a passive aorist participle, "after falling passionately in love".

Step 2: τῷ = Britannus (possessive dative, Lesson 1): the owner stands in the dative, and the thing owned (θυγάτηρ) is the subject. φανῶσιν is chosen because after ἵνα the verb must be subjunctive; the aorist stresses the outcome "be seen"; indicative ἐφάνησαν would turn the purpose into a report. ἀφ' οὗ ("from that point on") ties the name Keltos to the name Celt, closing the circle of the aition.

Step 3, directions for discussion: a foundation story links a people to a mythological hero to certify that its name is both ancient and noble; it cannot be checked, and in its own way does not ask to be. Today, the "origin story" of a company or a school does a similar job: it creates a pride that binds; the question for the learner is whom the story serves.

Exercise 1 – Conjugating the subjunctive

Fill in the missing forms of παιδεύω (present subjunctive active) and ἐπαίδευσα (aorist subjunctive active):

  1. Present, 1sg: παιδεύω → ___
  2. Present, 3sg: παιδεύει → ___
  3. Present, 3pl: παιδεύουσι(ν) → ___
  4. Aorist, 2sg: ἐπαίδευσας → ___
  5. Aorist, 3sg: ἐπαίδευσε(ν) → ___
  6. Aorist, 1pl: ἐπαιδεύσαμεν → ___

Answers 1

  1. παιδεύω
  2. παιδεύῃ
  3. παιδεύωσι(ν)
  4. παιδεύσῃς
  5. παιδεύσῃ
  6. παιδεύσωμεν

Notes: (1) the 1sg present is exactly identical with the indicative παιδεύω – only a conjunction can tell you this is a subjunctive; (2) παιδεύει → παιδεύῃ: contracted ending, long upsilon; (3) the long ending -ωσι(ν) – remember to compare it with the indicative -ουσι(ν); (4)–(6) aorist: take the aorist stem παιδεύσ- (augment dropped!) + long endings – the augment is the suit of the past; the subjunctive never wears one.

Exercise 2 – Peeling eight forms

Each of the following forms is the subjunctive of which verb, in which tense and person? (One form is a trap: it is not a subjunctive!)

  1. λύῃ
  2. λύσῃ
  3. ποιῶμεν
  4. τιμᾷ
  5. ᾖ
  6. ὦσι(ν)
  7. βοηθῇ
  8. μάθῃς

Answers 2

  1. present subj 3sg of λύω (contraction -ει → -ῃ)
  2. aorist subj 3sg of λύω (stem λυσ- + -ῃ)
  3. present subj 1pl of ποιέω (contraction: ποιέωμεν → ποιῶμεν; hortatory "let us do!")
  4. TRAP: τιμᾷ is spelled identically in both moods – the indicative "he honours" and the subjunctive after ἐάν/ἵνα are both written this way; you must consult the conjunction (section 12)
  5. present subj 3sg of εἰμί (the table of section 3; you need it to read ἐὰν ᾖ in Reading I)
  6. present subj 3pl of εἰμί – the indicative is εἰσί(ν)
  7. present subj 3sg of the contract verb βοηθέω (contraction: βοηθέῃ → βοηθῇ); "(that) he help"
  8. aorist subj 2sg of μανθάνω (aorist stem μαθ-, Lesson 13; "that you learn")

Exercise 3 – Filling in ἵνα / ἐάν / ὅταν / μὴ

Insert the right particle (ἵνα / ἐάν / ὅταν / μὴ) into the blanks:

  1. πίνωμεν τὸν οἶνον, ___ μὴ φανῶμεν. (let us drink the wine, so that we not be seen)
  2. ___ ὁ πατὴρ ᾖ ἐν οἴκῳ, τὰς βοῦς κρύπτομεν. (whenever the father is at home, we hide the cattle)
  3. βοηθεῖτε τῷ παιδί, ___ μὴ ἀποθάνῃ. (help the child, lest he die)
  4. ___ οἱ φίλοι συνδειπνῶσιν, οἶνον πίνουσιν. (whenever friends dine together, they drink wine)
  5. ἀποδώσω σοι τὰς βοῦς, ___ πρότερον φιλῇς με. (I shall give you back the cattle, if you kiss me first)
  6. τὸν οἶνον πίνεις; μὴ πίῃς· τὸ σῶμα σῴζεται ἄνευ οἴνου. (do you drink wine? do not drink; the body stays healthy without wine)

Answers 3

  1. ἵνα
  2. ἐάν
  3. ἵνα
  4. ὅταν
  5. ἐάν
  6. μὴ ... πίῃς → prohibition (section 7)

Notes: (1) negative purpose ἵνα μὴ + aorist subjunctive passive φανῶμεν (like the φανῶσιν of Reading II, but 1st plural because the subject is "we"); (2) the "whenever"-condition in general: ἐάν + ᾖ (or ὅταν ᾖ – the very same move!); (3) ἵνα μὴ + ἀποθάνῃ – negative purpose; (4) ὅταν + συνδειπνῶσιν exactly as in Reading I; (5) future condition: main clause -σω, dependent clause ἐάν + φιλῇς exactly as in Reading II; (6) prohibition: μὴ + aorist subj πίῃς of πίνω. Small trap in sentences 2 and 4: both mean "whenever", but 2 uses ἐάν (condition) and 4 uses ὅταν (time) – the two particles share the same move, with only a nuance of shade between them.

Exercise 4 – Indicative or subjunctive?

In each sentence below, which mood is the main verb in (indicative or subjunctive), and which sign gives it away?

  1. ἐὰν ὁ ἄνθρωπος τιμᾷ τοὺς θεούς, μακάριός ἐστιν.
  2. ὁ ἄνθρωπος τιμᾷ τοὺς θεούς.
  3. ὅτε ὁ βασιλεὺς ἦλθεν, οἱ Κελτοὶ προσεκύνησαν.
  4. ὅταν ὁ βασιλεὺς ἀφίκηται, οἱ Κελτοὶ προσκυνῶσι.
  5. σῴζετε τοὺς φίλους, ἵνα ὑγιαίνωσιν.
  6. τί ποιῶμεν; οἱ πολέμιοι ἤγαγον τὰς βοῦς.

Answers 4

  1. subjunctive – the sign: ἐάν introduces it (although τιμᾷ looks exactly like the indicative)
  2. indicative – a plain report, no indeterminacy particle
  3. indicative (aorist with the augment ἦλθ-εν) – ὅτε + one specific completed event
  4. subjunctive aorist ἀφίκηται (no augment!) and προσκυνῶσι – the sign: ὅταν = "whenever"
  5. indicative imperative σῴζετε in the main clause + subjunctive ὑγιαίνωσιν after ἵνα
  6. deliberative ποιῶμεν (section 7) – the question "what are we to do?"; ἤγαγον is an indicative aorist (augment + reporting a fact)

Exercise 5 – Translate into English

  1. ἴωμεν πρὸς τὸν ποταμόν, ἵνα τὰς βοῦς εὑρίσκωμεν.
  2. ἐὰν ὁ οὐρανὸς ᾖ καθαρός, οἱ ἄνθρωποι τὸν ἥλιον ὁρῶσιν.
  3. μὴ φάγῃς τὰ κρέα πρότερον, ἐὰν οἱ ξένοι μὴ ἔλθωσιν.
  4. ὃς ἂν τοὺς θεοὺς τιμᾷ, οὗτος σῴζεται.

Answers 5

  1. "Let us go to the river, that we may find the cattle." Note: hortatory ἴωμεν (present subj 1pl of εἶμι "to go" – learn by sight; a spelling relative of εἰμί, but a different verb!) + purpose ἵνα + εὑρίσκωμεν.
  2. "Whenever the sky is clear, people see the sun." General condition: ἐὰν + ᾖ, main clause present indicative – true at all times.
  3. "Do not eat the meat first, if the guests have not come." Prohibition μὴ + φάγῃς (aorist subj of ἐσθίω: stem φαγ-) + ἐὰν + μὴ ἔλθωσιν (subjunctive because it follows ἐάν, even though its negative is μὴ).
  4. "Whoever honours the gods, that man is saved." ὃς ἄν = indefinite relative (section 8): "whoever"; οὗτος pulls the clause back in (correlation, recall Lesson 14); the main clause is indicative.

Exercise 6 – Translate into Greek

  1. "Give me the cattle, so that we not have to fight." (use the aorist imperative of δίδωμι: δός, + the middle subjunctive of μάχομαι)
  2. "We hide the wine, so that the strangers not find it." (use κρύπτω present + εὑρίσκω)
  3. "Whenever friends dine together, they drink wine." (use συνδειπνέω)

Answers 6

  1. δὸς μοι τὰς βοῦς, ἵνα μὴ μαχώμεθα.
  2. κρύπτομεν τὸν οἶνον, ἵνα οἱ ξένοι αὐτὸν μὴ εὑρίσκωσιν.
  3. ὅταν οἱ φίλοι συνδειπνῶσι, οἶνον πίνουσιν.

Notes: (1) the second-aorist imperative δός (a second-aorist imperative – never with an augment); ἵνα μὴ + middle subj 1pl μαχώμεθα (a deponent – nobody "passivises" it); (2) the present describes the habit in the main clause; αὐτὸν accusative referring back to τὸν οἶνον; μὴ negates the purpose; the ending -ωσιν is long; (3) ὅταν "whenever" + subj, main clause present indicative – the "custom" formula of Reading I.
