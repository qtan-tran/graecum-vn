What you will be able to do after this lesson: (1) recognize third-declension nouns from their article and endings, and decline the consonant-stem patterns (neuter -μα, masculine -ην/-ωρ, and -ις) through all cases and numbers; (2) form and translate third-person imperatives (φιλείτω, θεραπευέτωσαν) alongside the 2nd-person forms you already know; (3) use μή correctly in prohibitions, checking yourself with the test "is this an imperative or a statement?".

1. What the third declension is: bare stem + ending

The two declensions you already know (Lessons 1–2) each have an easy-to-spot landmark: o-declension nouns end in -ος, a-declension nouns in -η/-α. The third declension is the "remaining box": every noun that belongs to neither group, from πόλις (city) to σῶμα (body), ποιμήν (shepherd) – and most proper names (ὁ Σωκράτης, ὁ Δάφνις).

The key concept: in the o/a groups, the nominative singular shows the stem easily (θεός → θε-, θάλασσα → θαλάσσ-). In the third declension, the nominative singular "hides" the stem: you have to go to the genitive singular before the real stem appears.

GreekTransliterationEnglishNote
τὸ σῶμα, τοῦ σώματοςto sôma, toû sṓmatosthe bodyhidden stem: σῶμα-, visible in σώματος
ὁ ποιμήν, τοῦ ποιμένοςho poimḗn, toû poiménosthe shepherdhidden stem: ποιμέν-
ἡ πόλις, τῆς πόλεωςhē pólis, tês póleōsthe cityhidden stem: πολε-

This lesson covers the largest group within the third declension: consonant-stem nouns – stems that end in a consonant (-ματ-, -μεν-, -τορ-). The vowel-stem group follows in Lesson 8.

Pattern to carry over (the golden rule of the third declension): Look a noun up by the formula "nominative singular, genitive singular" – e.g. ὁ παῖς, παιδός. Every Greek dictionary lists exactly those two forms; the genitive is the "key that unlocks the stem". Self-check: when you meet an unfamiliar noun, ask "what does its genitive look like?" – only then do you know the stem to attach the endings to.

2. The neuter pattern: σῶμα, σώματος

Neuter consonant-stem nouns (nominative in -μα) – the pattern σῶμα "body":

SingularTransliterationPluralTransliteration
Nominativeτὸ σῶμαto sômaτὰ σώματαtà sṓmata
Genitiveτοῦ σώματοςtoû sṓmatosτῶν σωμάτωνtôn sōmátōn
Dativeτῷ σώματιtôi sṓmatiτοῖς σώμασι(ν)toîs sṓmasi(n)
Accusativeτὸ σῶμαto sômaτὰ σώματαtà sṓmata

Three points to engrave:

  • A neuter noun always has nominative = accusative (in both numbers) – a general rule of all neuter nouns, first met in the article forms τό/τά in Lesson 3.
  • Dative plural -ασι(ν): the consonant stem σωμάτ- + -σι(ν) → two consonants "collide" (τ + σ) – pronounced masi(n), with the τ nearly silent at the head of the cluster.
  • The article remains an ordinary article (Lesson 1 for masculine, Lesson 3 for neuter) – only the noun changes its endings. The "read the article" strategy keeps its full power: τοῖς σώμασιν → dative plural, without knowing the word's meaning.

Latin corner: σῶμα, σώματος ≈ Latin nōmen, nōminis: the nominative "covers" the stem (nōmen), the genitive opens it up (nōmin-), and the dative plural nōminibus has -bus in exactly the position of Greek -σι(ν). Anyone who once learned corpus, corporis will meet σῶμα, σώματος as an old acquaintance – it is even the same word-family *te-sō-ma as the soma of medical English.

Watch out: (1) The nominative singular ποιμήν has a long η, but the dative singular ends in short -ι (ποιμένι) – do not "extrapolate" the ending from the nominative. (2) Dative plural: ποιμέσι(ν) (the -μεν- stem loses ν before σ) but ῥήτορσι(ν) (the -τορ- stem keeps ρ + σ). Memory hook: a stem that ends in a nasal (ν) "swallows" that nasal before σ. (3) The gender of a third-declension noun cannot be guessed from the ending – the ὁ/ἡ of the dictionary entry must be memorized (Lesson 1: gender is the noun's "ID number").

4. The -ις pattern: πόλις

The final group of this lesson: (mostly) masculine nouns (a few feminine) whose nominative ends in -ις and whose stem ends in -ε- – the pattern πόλις "city":

SingularTransliterationPluralTransliteration
Nominativeἡ πόλιςhē pólisαἱ πόλειςhai póleis
Genitiveτῆς πόλεωςtês póleōsτῶν πόλεωνtôn pólōn
Dativeτῇ πόλειtêi póleiταῖς πόλεσι(ν)taîs pólesi(n)
Accusativeτὴν πόλινtḕn pólinτὰς πόλειςtàs póleis

Quick memory hook: -ις / -εως / -ει / -ιν (singular) – the signature endings of "city" and of very many proper names (ὁ Σωκράτης, τῷ Σωκράτει). In the plural, πόλεις has nominative = accusative only because this noun is feminine (feminine plural accusative takes -ς, not -ν: πόλεις, not *πόλινς).

Latin corner: Latin has exact counterparts: cīvis, cīvis, hostis, hostis… and in particular the dative plural cīvibus ↔ πόλεσι(ν) – the same "shopfront" -σι(ν)/-bus. On bilingual inscriptions, imperial-era Athens places ἡ πόλις beside cīvitās: the same concept of a political community, almost the same family.

5. The third-declension ending map + a reading strategy

The three groups above differ in their stems, but the endings follow a shared pattern. The map (masculine, singular → plural):

CaseMasculine endingsExample (ποιμήν)Neuter endingsExample (σῶμα)
Nominative– (bare stem) / -εςποιμήν / ποιμένες-μα / -ματασῶμα / σώματα
Genitive-ος / -ωνποιμένος / ποιμένων-ος / -ωνσώματος / σωμάτων
Dative-ι / -σι(ν)ποιμένι / ποιμέσι(ν)-ι / -σι(ν)σώματι / σώμασι(ν)
Accusative-α / -αςποιμένα / ποιμένας= nominativeσῶμα / σώματα

An important observation: the genitive and dative endings "look just like the o-declension" (-ος/-ου, -ι/-ῳ… no – careful!): the genitive singular is -ος (like an o-declension nominative), the dative singular is -ι. This breeds confusion – see the confusables table at the end of the lesson. But it is also good news: these four rows of endings need to be learned only once for all three patterns.

Pattern for reading texts (the "read the article" strategy, upgraded): the article still tells you case + number + gender (Lesson 1). The third declension only changes how you look at the noun: its endings no longer match the article's endings as in the o-declension (ὁ θεός / -ος) – nouns now run a separate ending system. Always read the pair: τοῖς σώμασιν = article (dative, plural, neuter) + the noun ending confirming it (-σι(ν)). When two sources say the same thing, you never need to know the word's meaning.

A logical stopping point: you now have all three noun groups of Greek (o, a, third-consonant-stem). From Lesson 8 onward only the vowel-stem subgroup and the "mixed" types remain – which means most of the skeleton of the Greek noun system is already in place. The trio ὁ θεός, ἡ πόλις, τὸ σῶμα shows up in nearly every real sentence.

6. The imperative system: every number, every person

Lesson 4 introduced the present active imperative of the 2nd person (λῦε / λύετε); Lesson 5 added the middle/passive of the 2nd person (λύου / λύεσθε). Lesson 7 completes the system with the 3rd-person imperative – "he is to… / they are to…":

Present imperative2nd person (already known)3rd person (this lesson)
SingularPluralSingularPlural
Activeλῦε lúeλύετε lúeteλυέτω luétōλυέτωσαν luétōsan
Middle/passiveλύου lúouλύεσθε lúestheλυέσθω luéstōλυέσθωσαν luéstōsan
GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ Δάφνις φιλείτω τὰς αἶγας.ho Dáphis phileítō tàs aígas.Daphnis is to love the goats. / Let Daphnis love the goats.
αἱ δὲ αἶγες τὸν Δάφνιν θεραπευέτωσαν.hai dè aíges tòn Dáphin therapeuétōsan.And the goats are to tend Daphnis.

Three points to engrave:

  • The thematic vowel shows itself in the 3rd person: λυ-έ-τω – the ending attaches to the stem through the thematic vowel -ε- (Lesson 6). This is living proof that a thematic verb keeps working exactly the same way inside the imperative.
  • The meaning of the 3rd person: not addressed directly to the hearer, but a "decision/permission concerning a third party": laws, maxims, instructions – Greek inscriptions and decrees use it constantly (τελείτω "let (the official) perform" on decree stones).
  • The accent of the 3rd person always falls on the final syllable (-έτω/-έσθω): a circumflex on ω. A final circumflex is the "notch" by which you recognize a 3rd-person imperative in running text.

Latin corner: Latin has no 3rd-person imperative of its own – it has to borrow the subjunctive (amet "let him love"). Greek possesses a true 3rd-person imperative. This is one of the places where Greek is "finer-grained" than Latin: translating into Latin, φιλείτω → amet; translating into English, use "let him… / he is to…": the essence of the 3rd-person imperative is to assign behavior, not to describe it.

7. Prohibitions: μή + imperative

Lesson 4 established the pair of negations: οὐ negates statements (facts, assertions), while μή belongs to volition (commands, wishes). Now apply this to the imperative: a prohibition = μή + imperative.

GreekTransliterationEnglish
μὴ φοβοῦ τοὺς ἐχθρούς.mḕ phoboû toùs echthroús.Don't fear the enemies!
μὴ κακὰ ποιεῖτε, ὦ ἄνθρωποι.mḕ kakà poieîte, ô ánthrōpoi.Do not do bad things, O humans!
μὴ οὖν μέλλετε φιλοσοφεῖν.mḕ oûn méllete philosopheîn.So then, don't put off philosophizing!

The subtle point: sentence 3 is exactly the Epicurus maxim you read in Lesson 4 – back then μή was vocabulary; now you can parse its structure: prohibition + 2nd-person imperative + infinitive. Old knowledge being "dissected again" is the sign of a system closing up.

Self-check pattern (the four questions of negation): (1) Is this sentence a statement (asserting/denying a fact) or an act of volition (command/prohibition/wish)? → volition takes μή. (2) If imperative → prohibition μὴ + imperative. (3) οὐ stands before vowels, οὐκ before voiced consonants, οὐχ before voiceless consonants (Lesson 2) – whereas μή never changes its shape. (4) Test-translate: "do not… / don't…" – if the translation sounds natural, you have chosen correctly.

Watch out: μὴ + 3rd-person imperative is an indirect prohibition ("he is not to…"): μὴ φιλείτω ὁ Δάφνις τοὺς ἐχθρούς – "Daphnis is not to love the enemies". Do not confuse it with οὐ φιλεῖ – "he does not love" (a statement: describing a fact). One small word switches between "command" and "report" – back to the root rule: volition → μή, fact → οὐ.

8. Reading: The goatherd and the child

Source: the scene is based on Longus, Daphnis and Chloe 1.9–13 – the passage where the goatherd Lamon finds a child being suckled by a she-goat among the laurel bushes, takes him home, raises him and names him Daphnis. The original (public domain) can be read on Perseus/Theoi. The version below was rewritten by the project to Lesson 7 level – it uses only structures already learned, keeping the spirit of the original.

Simplified version (rewritten by the project):

ὁ Λάμων ὁ ποιμὴν ἐν τῇ αὐλῇ τῷ Δάφνιτι λέγει· ὦ παῖ, ἀγαθὸς γίγνου καὶ θεράπευε τὰς αἶγας· μὴ φοβοῦ τοὺς ἐχθρούς. ἐν μὲν τῇ πόλει πολλοὶ ἐχθροὶ εἰσιν, ἐν δὲ ταῖς αὐλαῖς ἡ εἰρήνη ἐστίν. ὁ δὲ Δάφνις ποιμὴν γίγνεται καὶ τὰς αἶγας θεραπεύει. αἱ δὲ αἶγες τὸν Δάφνιν θεραπευέτωσαν.

ho Lámōn ho poimḕn en têi aulêi tôi Dáphiti légei· ô paî, agathòs gígnou kai therápeue tàs aígas· mḕ phoboû toùs echthroús. en mèn têi pólei polloì echthroì eisín, en dè taîs aulaîs hē eirḗnē estín. ho dè Dáphis poimḕn gígnetai kai tàs aígas therapeúei. hai dè aíges tòn Dáphin therapeuétōsan.

Translation: Lamon the goatherd says to Daphnis in the farmyard: "Child, become good and tend the goats; do not fear the enemies. In the city there are many enemies, but in the farmyards there is peace." And Daphnis becomes a goatherd and tends the goats. And the goats – let them tend Daphnis.

Quick analysis (checking with the tools you already have):

  • ὁ Λάμων ὁ ποιμὴν – double article (Lesson 1: two articles = attributive): "Lamon the goatherd". Both nouns are third declension (the -ων pattern uses the endings in the same way as ποιμήν).
  • τῷ Δάφνιτι – the article τῷ announces dative singular masculine; the noun ending -ι confirms it (the ending map, section 5). A "difficult" proper name read off its article – the old strategy wins again.
  • ὦ παῖ – vocative (Lesson 2); ἀγαθὸς γίγνου – 2nd-person middle/passive imperative "become!" (Lesson 5) + predicate; θεράπευε – 2nd-person active imperative (Lesson 4). One sentence, two imperatives, two voices.
  • μὴ φοβοῦ – this lesson's prohibition: μή + imperative (φοβοῦ = deponent "fear!", Lesson 5). The grave accent on φοβοῦ because the next word is τοὺς (the accent convention, Lesson 2).
  • ἐν μὲν… ἐν δὲ… – the particle pair clamping the two clauses together (Lesson 1); ἐν τῇ πόλει – dative singular of πόλις (-ει); ταῖς αὐλαῖς – a-declension dative plural.
  • θεραπευέτωσαν – the 3rd-person plural imperative of section 6! The final sentence is a "goatherd decree": the shepherd tends the goats, the goats tend the shepherd – exactly the pastoral humor of Longus.

Reading comprehension: (1) List every third-declension noun in the passage (with case + number). (2) Find the three imperatives in the reading and give person/number/voice of each. (3) Read the original of Longus 1.9–13 (Perseus/Theoi): how does Lamon actually find Daphnis, and how does the simplified version "package" it?

Answers to the reading questions (print to check)

1. ὁ Λάμων (nominative, masculine, singular – -ων pattern), ὁ ποιμὴν (nominative, singular; second occurrence: ποιμὴν γίγνεται – predicate nominative), τῷ Δάφνιτι (dative, singular, -ις pattern), ὦ παῖ (vocative), τῇ πόλει (dative, singular, feminine, -ις pattern), τὸν Δάφνιν (accusative, singular), αἶγες/αἶγας/αἰγ-… (third declension, mildly irregular – every occurrence readable through the article). 2. γίγνου (2nd person, singular, middle/passive), θεράπευε (2nd person, singular, active), μὴ φοβοῦ (2nd person, singular, middle/passive deponent – prohibition), θεραπευέτωσαν (3rd person, plural, active). 3. In Longus, Lamon finds a child being suckled by a she-goat beside a laurel bush, takes him home to raise and names him after the laurel (δάφνη) – the simplified version packages the whole "legend" into a single instruction from the foster father.

Exercise 5 – οὐ or μή?

Choose οὐ/οὐκ/μή and justify with the volition test:

  1. ___ φιλεῖ τοὺς ἐχθρούς. (describing: "he does not love…")
  2. ___ φιλείτω τοὺς ἐχθρούς! (command: "he is not to love…")
  3. ___ ἀγαθοὶ ἐσμέν. ("we are not good")
  4. ___ κακὰ ποιεῖτε, ὦ ἄνθρωποι. ("don't do bad things")

Answers 5

  1. οὐ φιλεῖ – a statement describing a fact (indicative verb, not an imperative).
  2. μὴ φιλείτω – a negated 3rd-person imperative → volition → μή.
  3. οὐκ ἀγαθοὶ ἐσμέν – a statement (ἐσμέν = indicative); before a vowel → οὐκ.
  4. μὴ κακὰ ποιεῖτε – a prohibition of a 2nd-person imperative → volition → μή.

Exercise 6 – Translate the summary passage (Lessons 4–7)

Translate, naming at each step which tool from which lesson you used:

ὁ ῥήτωρ ἐν τῇ πόλει τοῖς πολίταις λέγει· ὦ ἄνθρωποι, μὴ κακὰ ποιεῖτε· ἡ γὰρ πόλις ὑμῶν σῶμα ἐστίν. ὁ δὲ ποιμὴν ἐν τῇ αὐλῇ ζῷα θεραπεύει· αἱ δὲ αἶγες τὸν Δάφνιν θεραπευέτωσαν. (πολίτης = citizen; ὑμῶν = of you (pl.), Lesson 4; ζῷον = animal, Lesson 1)

Answers 6

"The orator says in the city to the citizens: O humans, do not do bad things; for the city is your body. And the shepherd tends the animals in the farmyard; and the goats are to tend Daphnis."

Notes on each construction: (1) τοῖς πολίταις – dative plural of ὁ πολίτης (third declension, -ης pattern); the article τοῖς rules: dative, plural. (2) μὴ κακὰ ποιεῖτε – prohibition + 2nd-person plural imperative (Lessons 7 + 4). (3) ἡ πόλις ὑμῶν σῶμα ἐστίν – two nominatives → predicate construction (Lesson 1); ὑμῶν = the 2nd-person plural possessive pronoun (Lesson 4), here attributive after the noun. (4) ζῷα θεραπεύει – neuter plural as object with a singular verb (Lesson 3: the object never affects the verb's number). (5) θεραπευέτωσαν – 3rd-person plural imperative (Lesson 7): a "goatherd decree" with the same humor as the reading.
