Goals for this lesson: you can (1) decline the present middle/passive indicative of every thematic -ω verb you have learned, in all six persons, telling the 2nd-singular ending -ει apart from the -ῃ of the deponent group; (2) use the 3rd person middle/passive imperative (-έσθω/-έσθωσαν) both in commands and in prohibitions (μὴ + 3rd person imperative); (3) attach an agent to a passive sentence with ὑπό + genitive; (4) tell apart the four meaning "lanes" of the -ομαι form; (5) recognize and decline four new deponents: ἔρχομαι, ἀπέρχομαι, ἀποκρίνομαι, αἰσθάνομαι.

1. Review map: six persons × two voices (the παιδεύω model)

Lesson 5 opened the door of the middle/passive house; today we walk through every room to make sure. Look at the two columns side by side – the active you have owned since Lesson 2, the middle/passive differs in exactly six final endings. Model verb: παιδεύω "educate" (learned in Lesson 8).

Present indicativeActiveTransliterationMiddle/PassiveTransliterationEnglish
1 Sg.παιδεύωpaideúōπαιδεύομαιpaideúomaiI educate (myself) / I am educated
2 Sg.παιδεύειςpaideúeisπαιδεύειpaideúeiyou educate (yourself) / you are educated
3 Sg.παιδεύειpaideúeiπαιδεύεταιpaideúetaihe educates (himself) / he is educated
1 Pl.παιδεύομενpaideúomenπαιδευόμεθαpaideuómethawe educate (ourselves) / are educated
2 Pl.παιδεύετεpaideúeteπαιδεύεσθεpaideúestheyou (pl.) educate (yourselves) / are educated
3 Pl.παιδεύουσι(ν)paideúousi(n)παιδεύονταιpaideúontaithey educate (themselves) / are educated

Three points worth a close look: (1) -όμεθα is the one ending that never coincides with anyone else's form – meet it and you know instantly it is the 1st person plural middle/passive (the ending carries a mandatory accent on the ό: παιδευόμεθα); (2) the 2nd singular παιδεύει is identical with the 3rd person active – Lesson 5 warned you; only the context (who listens, who speaks) tells them apart; (3) the plural -ονται always carries the telltale τ – the "signature" of the middle/passive; see it and you will never confuse it with -ουσι(ν).

Pattern to carry over: take the stem + the thematic vowel -ο-/-ε- (already visible in the imperative systems of Lessons 6–7) and attach the middle/passive endings: -ομαι, -ει, -εται, -όμεθα, -εσθε, -ονται. The whole thematic (-ω) verb family runs on a single track – one wrong slot tells you immediately you clipped on the wrong ending.

2. Completing the system: 3rd-person imperatives & the middle/passive infinitive

The present-tense handbook now fills its last empty cells. Lesson 5 gave the 2nd-person imperatives (πέμπου! πέμπεσθε!); Lesson 7 gave the 3rd-person imperative system of the active (-έτω, -έτωσαν) and already listed the middle/passive pair – now we drill it until it is secure:

ImperativeActiveTransliterationMiddle/PassiveTransliterationEnglish
2 Sg.παιδεύε!paideúeπαιδεύου!paideúoueducate yourself! / have yourself educated!
2 Pl.παιδεύετε!paideúeteπαιδεύεσθε!paideúestheeducate yourselves! (you all)
3 Sg.παιδευέτω!paideuetōπαιδευέσθω!paideúesthōlet him be educated! (a command)
3 Pl.παιδευέτωσαν!paideuetōsanπαιδευέσθωσαν!paideuesthōsanlet them be educated!

Memorize the template: the 3rd person middle/passive = thematic vowel -ε- + -σθω/-σθωσαν – the same "final cap" -ωσαν as the active, differing only in the ending stem -σθ-. The 3rd person is the voice of commands and stone inscriptions – and it even enters indirect prohibitions (μὴ + 3rd person imperative, Lesson 7):

GreekTransliterationEnglish
μὴ παιδευέσθω τὸ παιδίον.mḕ paideúesthō tò paidíon.Let the child not be educated (by someone unworthy).
τὸ ποτήριον μὴ ἀπορριπτέσθω!tò potḗrion mḕ aporriptésthō!Let not the cup be thrown away! (etiquette of the water-drinker)

And the last cell: the middle/passive infinitive in -εσθαι (Lesson 5) now attaches to a verb you will "meet in practically every text": βούλομαι "want" + infinitive:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
βούλομαι μάχεσθαι.boúlomai mákhesthai.I want to fight.
οἱ πολῖται βούλονται ὑπὸ τοῦ νόμου παιδεύεσθαι.hoi polîtai boúlontai hypò toû nómou paideúesthai.The citizens want to be educated by the law.

Watch out – 2nd person plural vs infinitive: παιδεύεσθε is the 2nd person plural (indicative or imperative, carrying the personal ending -σθε); παιδεύεσθαι is the infinitive, carrying no person or number. Self-check: the infinitive always ends in -σθαι; the 2nd person plural always ends in -σθε. Neither of these two endings ever takes the movable ν – movable ν attaches only to the active -ουσι(ν) and short forms like εἰσίν.

3. The agent of the passive: ὑπό + genitive, Lesson 5 deepened

Lesson 5 gave you the formula in one example; today we drill it into a reflex. A passive sentence answers "done by whom?": the agent – the one who performs the action but has yielded the subject seat – stands in the phrase, attached to the preposition ὑπό + genitive:

ActivePassive + agentEnglish
ὁ φύλαξ φυλάσσει τὴν οἰκίαν.ἡ οἰκία ὑπὸ τοῦ φύλακος φυλάσσεται.The guard watches the house → the house is watched by the guard.
ὁ βασιλεὺς παιδεύει τοὺς πολίτας.οἱ πολῖται ὑπὸ τοῦ βασιλέως παιδεύονται.The king educates the citizens → the citizens are educated by the king.
ὁ Πλάτων γράφει τοὺς λόγους.οἱ λόγοι ὑπὸ τοῦ Πλάτωνος γράφονται.Plato writes the words → the words are written by Plato.

Three "sentence-flip" moves to watch: (1) the accusative object becomes the subject – and it dictates the number of the passive verb (οἱ πολῖται plural → παιδεύονται); (2) the old subject moves into ὑπό + genitive – with the article declining exactly by the rules (τοῦ βασιλέως, τοῦ Πλάτωνος); (3) the endings -εται/-ονται replace -ει/-ουσι(ν). It is exactly the "role reversal" of the AcI you learned in Lesson 4: one sentence, two ways of telling it.

Latin corner: Latin does exactly the same with ā/ab + ablative: cīvēs ā rēge dūcuntur "the citizens are led by the king". Greek chooses the genitive, Latin the ablative – one "by whom" block, two kinds of packaging. And seen in reverse: in both languages the agent is always the "hidden subject" of the action.

Detail worth knowing: ὑπό has three faces depending on the case that accompanies it: + genitive = "by" (the passive agent); + dative = "under" (local meaning); + accusative = "because of" (causal meaning). This lesson needs only the first. And the irony: Greek prose prefers passives without a named agent (λέγεται "it is said", "people say" – Lesson 5) – when an agent is named with ὑπό, the author wants to stress "precisely this person".

4. The meaning lanes of the -ομαι ending: direct middle, indirect middle, passive

Lesson 5 taught "one form, several translations". Today we give each lane a name, so that every time you meet -εται you can ask the right question – and answer it yourself:

LaneGreekTransliterationEnglishTest question
Direct middle
(direct middle)		ὁ ἄνθρωπος λούεται.ho ánthrōpos loúetai.The man bathes himself.Does the subject perform the action on itself?
Indirect middle
(indirect middle)		ὁ στρατηγὸς μισθόεται ναῦται.ho stratēgòs misthoétai naútai.The general hires sailors for himself.Does the subject act in its own interest?
Passive
(passive)		ὁ ἄγγελος ὑπὸ τοῦ βασιλέως πέμπεται.ho ángelos hypò toû basileōs pémpetai.The messenger is sent by the king.Does the subject undergo the action (agent present or not)?
Deponent
(middle form, active meaning)		ὁ στρατηγὸς μάχεται.ho stratēgòs mákhetai.The general fights.Dictionary form in -ομαι? → translate actively.

λούεται "bathes himself" is the classic example of the direct lane: the action turns back onto the subject itself, with no other object. The indirect lane widens "for himself" into "in his own interest": μισθόεται "hires for himself" (Lesson 5) – the action still lands on someone else (ναῦται "sailors", dative – met in many readings), but the benefit belongs to the subject. English has no corresponding voice of its own – you translate with "himself / for himself", and when the context is clear you simply translate smoothly.

The 4-step decision tree when you meet -εται/-ονται: (1) check your vocabulary: the dictionary form is listed in -ομαι → deponent, translate actively, done; (2) the sentence contains ὑπό + genitive → passive; (3) the action points back at the subject itself → direct middle; (4) otherwise, if the context says "in the subject's own interest" → indirect middle. In real reading, steps 1 and 2 handle more than nine cases out of ten.

The pattern of all eleven: the dictionary form is always -ομαι; the meaning is always active; the 2nd person singular usually carries the long -ῃ. So when you meet ἔρχεται, your brain must jump in exactly this order: ending -εται (a middle/passive form) → check the dictionary → ἔρχομαι a deponent → translate "he comes", NOT "he is come". ἀποκρίνεται = "he answers". αἰσθάνεται τοὺς πολεμίους = "he perceives the enemies".

Watch out – a deponent can still "sleep in the passive role": some deponents, when the meaning fits, accept an agent ὑπό + genitive and translate as passive: ἡττῶμαι ὑπὸ παιδίου "I am beaten by a child" (met in Reading II). The form does not change, only the context switches lanes – which is why the decision tree of section 4 always starts with "is there a ὑπό", and only then "check the dictionary: is it a deponent". The two tests are used crosswise; neither replaces the other.

Latin corner: Latin deponents are all over familiar territory: loquitur "he speaks" (not *loquit), ūtitur "he uses", moritur "he dies" – and the 3rd person plural loquuntur with its ending -ntur is precisely the relative of -ονται: both languages preserve the same "fossil" of the era when the middle was still a fully living voice in the Indo-European verb system.

6. Reading I: The guardian and the philosophical dog (Plato, Politeia 375b–e)

In the Politeia – the "ideal state" – Plato and Glaukon discuss the guardians of the city: gentle toward their own people, harsh toward the enemy – two qualities that sound contradictory, and Plato illustrates them with… a well-bred puppy. The passage below was rewritten by the project at Lesson 1–10 level; unlearned words are collected in a separate Notes block right after the reading.

1 ὁ Πλάτων περὶ τῶν φυλάκων γράφει· ὁ φύλαξ πρὸς μὲν τοὺς οἰκείους πραεῖός ἐστιν, πρὸς δὲ τοὺς πολεμίους χαλεπός ἐστιν. 2 τὸ δὲ σκυλάκιον τοῦτο ποιεῖ· ὑπὸ γὰρ τοῦ σκυλακίου οἱ μὲν οἰκεῖοι εὖ φυλάσσονται, ὁ δὲ ξένος οὐ θεραπεύεται. 3 διὸ ὁ Πλάτων λέγει· τὸ σκυλάκιον φιλόσοφόν ἐστιν.

ho Plátōn perì tôn phulákōn gráphei· ho phílax pròs mèn toùs oikeíous praeiós estin, pròs dè toùs polemíous khalepós estin. tò dè skulákion toûto poieî· hypò gàr toû skulakíou hoi mèn oikeîoi eû phulássontai, ho dè xénos ou therapeúetai. diò ho Plátōn légei· tò skulákion philósophon estin.

Notes:

  • ὁ φύλαξ, τοῦ φύλακος = guardian – a guttural consonant stem: κ + ς → ξ in the nominative, while the genitive keeps the κ- stem intact; πρᾶος = gentle; πολέμιος = hostile
  • τὸ σκυλάκιον = puppy; ὁ ξένος = stranger – Lesson 6; φυλάσσομαι/φυλάσσεται = to guard – Lesson 9 already used it in the exercises
  • διό = therefore

Three reading steps: work through them in order before scrolling down to the translation; the answers appear only in print, for checking. The text is numbered by sentence: C2 means sentence 2.

  1. Step 1, Formal observation: circle every verb carrying the ending -ται/-ονται and say for each one whether it expresses "something is done to the subject" or "the subject does something for itself"; circle the agent phrase that goes with ὑπό and name its case; circle the contrast pair πρὸς μὲν … πρὸς δὲ in sentence 1.
  2. Step 2, Meaning and structure: build a two-row table "guard – puppy" with two columns "toward the household" and "toward strangers": quote the Greek evidence with its sentence number in every cell. Then answer: why does Plato conclude that the puppy is a "philosopher" – which behavior of the dog is being treated as an act of recognition?
  3. Step 3, Discussion and connection: the original (Politeia 375b–e) goes on to treat the disposition "fond of the familiar, on guard against the stranger" as the root of the philosophic character. What claim does the comparison "dog = philosopher" prove, and what limit does such an analogical argument have? Greek uses one verb of guarding for both sides; how many words does your own language need to carry the two attitudes?

Translation:

English (free translation)
"Plato writes about the guardians: the guard is gentle toward his own people, but harsh toward the enemies."
"And this puppy does exactly that: for by the puppy the members of the household are well guarded, while the stranger is not welcomed."
"Therefore Plato says: the puppy is a philosopher."

Dissecting each sentence (checking with the tools you have already learned):

  • Sentence 1, dissected: περὶ τῶν φυλάκων – preposition + article genitive plural (Lesson 1); ὁ φύλαξ – nominative singular: the job title "guard"; πρὸς + accusative (Lesson 1) "toward"; μὲν … δὲ – the contrast pair (Lesson 1): "on the one hand… on the other"; πραεῖός ἐστιν, χαλεπός ἐστιν – two o/a-pattern adjectives as predicates (Lesson 1), nominative singular masculine matching ὁ φύλαξ. Two "opposite-sign" qualities in one person – that is exactly the problem Plato poses.
  • Sentence 2, dissected: τοῦτο – "this one" pointing straight at the puppy (the demonstrative of Lesson 6); ὑπὸ τοῦ σκυλακίου – the agent of section 3: "by the puppy"; οἱ οἰκεῖοι – nominative plural (m./f., o-pattern); φυλάσσονται – 3rd person plural middle/passive: "are guarded" (Lesson 9: φυλάττω, the ττ stem meets σ → σσ); εὖ – the irregular adverb of Lesson 9: "well"; ὁ δὲ ξένος οὐ θεραπεύεται – the counterweight: "the stranger, however, is not cared for / welcomed". The subject οἱ οἰκεῖοι is plural so the verb is plural; ὁ ξένος is singular so θεραπεύεται is singular – the gender-number-case agreement of Lesson 1.
  • Sentence 3, dissected: διὸ – the concluding conjunction "therefore" (a near relative of ἄρα "so then" from Lesson 2); τὸ σκυλάκιον – neuter singular subject (o-pattern noun, the article τό rules); φιλόσοφόν ἐστιν – a neuter predicate adjective matching the gender; the verb stays singular with a neuter subject (exactly the Lesson 3 rule, even when the noun denotes a living being); ἐστίν carries movable ν before a vowel.

General grammar points to remember: the passage is a playground of the middle/passive ending: φυλάσσονται (3rd person plural "are guarded", with the agent ὑπό + genitive), θεραπεύεται ("is welcomed" – negated by οὐ because a real fact is denied), and the pair πρὸς μὲν … πρὸς δὲ, still clamping the two attitudes into one person – exactly Plato's "one man, two faces" model.

The original for comparison (Plato, Politeia 375c, Burnet): Ἀλλὰ μέντοι δεῖ γε πρὸς μὲν τοὺς οἰκείους πραεῖους αὐτοὺς εἶναι, πρὸς δὲ τοὺς πολεμίους χαλεπούς "But surely they must be gentle toward their own, harsh toward the enemies" – our simplified sentence 1 keeps nearly the same logic and vocabulary; Plato uses δεῖ "it is necessary" (learned in Lesson 1) + infinitive. The original text is public domain – continue reading on Perseus (see the footer).

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. Verbs in -ται/-ονται: φυλάσσονται (C2, 3rd person plural, middle/passive "are guarded"), θεραπεύεται (C2, 3rd person singular, "is welcomed", negated with οὐ because a real, ongoing fact is denied). Agent: ὑπὸ γὰρ τοῦ σκυλακίου (C2, ὑπό + genitive: "by the puppy"). Contrast pair: πρὸς μὲν τοὺς οἰκείους … πρὸς δὲ τοὺς πολεμίους (C1) – "on the one hand… on the other".

Step 2, Meaning and structure. The table: the guard – gentle toward the household (πρὸς μὲν τοὺς οἰκείους πραεῖός ἐστιν, C1), harsh toward the enemy (πρὸς δὲ τοὺς πολεμίους χαλεπός ἐστιν, C1); the puppy – the household is well guarded (οἱ μὲν οἰκεῖοι εὖ φυλάσσονται, C2), the stranger is not welcomed (ὁ δὲ ξένος οὐ θεραπεύεται, C2). Plato's conclusion: τὸ σκυλάκιον φιλόσοφόν ἐστιν (C3) – the behavior "knowing the familiar, caring for one's own; spotting the stranger, keeping him off" counts as an act of recognizing true and false, and "recognizing" is the philosopher's job.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. The comparison is not a whim: Plato reads the dog's ability to tell friend from foe as "loving what is familiar (already known to be good) and growling at the unfamiliar (not yet known)" – the attitude of "trusting only what one has verified". The limit of the analogy: the dog distinguishes by the instinct of smell, the philosopher by argument; fully identifying the two abilities would turn philosophy into a reflex. Today the word "protecting" the home goes hand in hand with "guarding" the border: each language chooses its own way of packing the two attitudes into one word.

7. Reading II: The cup and the child (Diogenes Laertios 6,37)

Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412–323 BC), the "dog philosopher", lived in a barrel and pushed the simple life to the extreme. Diogenes Laertios records the anecdotes; passage 6,37 tells: seeing a child drink from its two hands, Diogenes threw away his own cup – "I have been beaten by a child in the matter of simplicity".

1 ὁ Διογένης ποτὲ παιδίον βλέπει· τὸ παιδίον ἐκ τῶν χειρῶν πίνει. 2 καὶ ὁ Διογένης τὸ ποτήριον ἀπορρίπτει καὶ λέγει· ἡττῶμαι ὑπὸ παιδίου ἐν τῇ ἁπλότητι τοῦ βίου. 3 ὁ δὲ Διογένης λέγει· πάντα μὲν τῶν θεῶν ἐστιν· οἱ δὲ σοφοὶ θεῶν φίλοι εἰσίν· ἄρα πάντα τῶν σοφῶν ἐστιν.

ho Diogénēs potè paidíon blépei· tò paidíon ek tôn kheirôn pínei. kaì ho Diogénēs tò potḗrion aporríptei kaì légei· hettômhai hypò paidíou en têi haplótēti toû bíou. ho dè Diogénēs légei· pánta mèn tôn theôn estin· hoi dè sophoí theôn fíloi eisín· ára pánta tôn sophôn estin.

Notes:

  • ποτέ = once, at some time; βλέπω = look at, see; τὸ παιδίον = child; ἡ χείρ, τῶν χειρῶν = hand – an ρ stem with vowel gradation (ablaut) – see the extension section in Lesson 7
  • ἀπορρίπτω = throw away; τὸ ποτήριον = cup; ἡττῶμαι = am beaten, am inferior – a root deponent with a contracted ending you will meet in Lesson 12; ἡ ἁπλότης = simplicity; ὁ βίος = life – Lesson 2
  • πάντα = all things – Lesson 2; ὁ σοφός = the wise one – substantivized, Lessons 5/9; ἄρα = so then – Lesson 2

Three reading steps: work through them in order before scrolling down to the translation; the answers appear only in print, for checking. The text is numbered by sentence, starting afresh for Reading II: C2 means sentence 2.

  1. Step 1, Formal observation: circle the deponent verb ἡττῶμαι and its agent: which verb builds the meaning "is beaten", and which ὑπό phrase goes with it? Circle the two prepositions of origin (ἐκ, ἐν) and name the case each one takes; in sentence 3, mark the three limbs of the syllogism and the concluding particle.
  2. Step 2, Meaning and structure: compress the first two sentences into a short story (who does what, and why the cup is thrown away); sentence 3 lays out a three-step argument: draw the scheme "all things → … → … → so all things…" and say what Diogenes is "excusing" with it.
  3. Step 3, Discussion and connection: Diogenes is beaten by a child in simplicity and … delighted about it. How does "racing" simplicity differ from "advertised" simplicity? And is his syllogism really airtight or merely elegant – who could ever check the premise that all things belong to the gods?

Translation:

English (free translation)
"Once Diogenes sees a child: the child drinks from its hands."
"And Diogenes throws away the cup and says: I am beaten by a child, in the simplicity of life."
"And Diogenes says: all things belong to the gods; the wise are friends of the gods; therefore all things belong to the wise."

Dissecting each sentence (checking with the tools you have already learned):

  • Sentence 1, dissected: ὁ Διογένης – nominative singular (a proper name with the Σωκράτης pattern: genitive τοῦ Διογένους); ποτὲ – the indeclinable adverb "once"; παιδίον βλέπει – neuter singular accusative as the object (a child is a "thing" in Greek!); ἐκ τῶν χειρῶν – preposition + genitive plural "from the hands" (Lesson 1: ἐκ + gen.); πίνει – present 3rd person active.
  • Sentence 2, dissected: two active verbs joined by καὶ … καὶ; τὸ ποτήριον ἀπορρίπτει – neuter accusative + present 3rd person (subject implied: "he"); then the quotation holds both of the newest tools at once: ἡττῶμαι a deponent carrying a passive meaning with an agent ὑπὸ παιδίου "by a child" (sections 4 + 3: the two tests meet); ἐν τῇ ἁπλότητι – dative singular after ἐν (Lesson 1); τοῦ βίου – dependent genitive "of life".
  • Sentence 3, dissected: this is the syllogism with which Diogenes "justifies" claiming all things as his own. πάντα τῶν θεῶν ἐστιν – neuter plural subject + the genitive "of the gods" as a possessive predicate: "all things belong to the gods" (Lesson 1: genitive = "of"); οἱ σοφοὶ θεῶν φίλοι εἰσίν – substantivization (Lessons 5, 9): "the wise are friends of the gods"; ἄρα – the conclusion "therefore"; the third clause flips the genitive to "of the wise". εἰσίν carries movable ν before a vowel.

General grammar points to remember: Reading II displays a deponent carrying a passive meaning with an agent (ἡττῶμαι ὑπὸ παιδίου), which shows that the ending -ται does not tell the whole story on its own: you must also ask "who is the author of the action". The closing syllogism is a miniature piece of argumentative prose: three limbs, two joining particles (μέν, δέ), one concluding word [ἄρα] – the frame of all later rhetoric.

The original for comparison (Diogenes Laertios 6,37): παιδίον ἰδὼν ἐκ τῶν χειρῶν πίνοντα τὸ ποτήριον ἀπέρριψεν εἰπών· "νενίκηκαί τι," ἔφη, "ὑπὸ παιδὸς ἐν τῇ ἁπλότητι τοῦ βίου" – "seeing a child drinking from its hands, he threw away the cup, saying: 'I have been somewhat defeated,' he said, 'by a child, in the simplicity of life'". Notes: ἰδὼν/εἰπών are participles ("having seen / having said" – a later lesson); νενίκηκαί is a perfect ("has been defeated" – Lesson 15); but the agent ὑπὸ παιδὸς keeps its key position unchanged – the section 3 formula lives on through every tense and every form. The syllogism above is also nearly verbatim: πάντα τῶν θεῶν εἶναι· τοὺς δὲ σοφοὺς θεῶν φίλους· τοὺς δὲ φίλους κοινὰ ἔχειν· ἄρα πάντα τῶν σοφῶν εἶναι. The original text is public domain.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. Deponent: ἡττῶμαι (C2, "is beaten, is inferior") – a passive meaning with the agent ὑπὸ παιδίου ("by a child", ὑπό + genitive). Prepositions: ἐκ τῶν χειρῶν (C1, "from the hands", + genitive), ἐν τῇ ἁπλότητι (C2, "in the simplicity", + dative). Syllogism (C3): πάντα μὲν τῶν θεῶν ἐστιν / οἱ δὲ σοφοὶ θεῶν φίλοι εἰσίν / ἄρα πάντα τῶν σοφῶν ἐστιν, concluded with ἄρα.

Step 2, Meaning and structure. The story: Diogenes sees a child drinking from its two hands (C1), immediately throws away the cup and admits he is beaten by a child in the simplicity of life (C2). The scheme of the argument (C3): all things belong to the gods → the wise are friends of the gods → so all things belong to the wise. He is "excusing" the habit of borrowing everything in life: what belongs to friends is one's own.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. Diogenes loses and rejoices in losing: the anecdote flips the convention "losing is shameful" into "losing can still teach you something new" – simplicity is a contest, and being beaten still pays. The syllogism is beautiful in form, but its first premise ("all things belong to the gods") cannot be checked – tight form, hanging content. For comparison: Diogenes Laertios 6,37 (see the comparison block above).

Two vocabulary notes: (1) βλέπω often appears with εἰς + accusative: βλέπω εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν "look into the sky". When you translate into English, the prepositional phrase often folds into a plain direct object: "watch the sky" – don't force a literal "into": a prepositional object in the original can become a direct object in the translation, because every language pays in its own currency (the "case government" pattern of Lesson 1; compare κατηγορέω + genitive, Lesson 4). (2) ποτέ "once, at some time" is an unaccented enclitic; its interrogative sibling πότε "when?" always carries an accent on the first syllable – exactly the accent pattern of τις (indefinite) vs τίς (interrogative) from Lesson 5.

A note on two families: (1) παιδίον, σκυλάκιον, ποτήριον share the neuter -ιον template (o-pattern) – Greek makes anything small and dear with this suffix, and even a "child" is literally a "little thing"; (2) πρᾶος, χαλεπός, οἰκεῖος, πολέμιος are four perfectly regular o/a-pattern adjectives – but note the subtitle: ὁ οἰκεῖος and ὁ πολέμιος, wearing the masculine article, turn into the nouns "a member of the household / an enemy" – exactly the substantivization of Lessons 5 and 9.

10. Exercises

Answers appear only when printing (Ctrl+P → Save as PDF) – work through everything before you look.

Exercise 1 – Decline both voices

Build the present indicative in all six persons of ἀπέρχομαι (deponent) and φυλάσσω (active and middle/passive in two columns), with the transliteration of the 2nd person singular.

Answers 1

ἀπέρχομαι is a deponent: active meaning "depart" but middle/passive forms – ἀπέρχομαι, ἀπέρχῃ (apérkhēi), ἀπέρχεται, ἀπερχόμεθα, ἀπέρχεσθε, ἀπέρχονται. φυλάσσω active: φυλάσσω, φυλάσσεις, φυλάσσει, φυλάσσομεν, φυλάσσετε, φυλάσσουσι(ν); middle/passive: φυλάσσομαι, φυλάσσῃ (the σσ stem merges with -εαι contracting to -ῃ, the same contraction as the 2 Sg. of the deponent group), φυλάσσεται, φυλασσόμεθα, φυλάσσεσθε, φυλάσσονται.

Exercise 2 – Flip into the passive with an agent

Turn into passive sentences with ὑπό + genitive, following the template of section 3:

  1. ὁ φύλαξ φυλάσσει τὴν οἰκίαν.
  2. ὁ Πλάτων γράφει τοὺς λόγους.
  3. ἡ μήτηρ παιδεύει τὸ παιδίον.
    (ἡ μήτηρ, τῆς μητρός = mother)

Answers 2

  1. ἡ οἰκία ὑπὸ τοῦ φύλακος φυλάσσεται.
  2. οἱ λόγοι ὑπὸ τοῦ Πλάτωνος γράφονται.
  3. τὸ παιδίον ὑπὸ τῆς μητρὸς παιδεύεται.

Run three checks on each sentence: the old object becomes the subject (article + number-matching ending), the verb switches to -εται/-ονται, and the old subject moves into ὑπό + genitive with the right article (τοῦ, τῆς). Sentence 3: τὸ παιδίον neuter singular → the verb stays singular.

Exercise 3 – Label the meaning lane

Which lane of section 4 does each form belong to (direct middle / indirect middle / passive / deponent)? Translate:

  1. ὁ στρατηγὸς λούεται.
  2. ὁ στρατηγὸς μισθόεται ναῦται.
  3. ὁ ἄγγελος ὑπὸ τοῦ βασιλέως πέμπεται.
  4. ὁ στρατηγὸς ἔρχεται.
  5. ὁ στρατηγὸς ἀποκρίνεται.

Answers 3

  1. Direct middle: "the general bathes (himself)".
  2. Indirect middle: "the general hires (for himself) sailors".
  3. Passive (with agent): "the messenger is sent by the king".
  4. Deponent: "the general comes" (never "is come"!).
  5. Deponent: "the general answers".

Procedure: (1)(2)(5) check the dictionary first; (3) spot the ὑπό first; only (1)(2) need the "for himself / in his own interest" question.

Exercise 4 – 3rd-person imperatives & prohibitions in the middle/passive

Translate into Greek (3rd person middle/passive imperatives, using section 2):

  1. "Let the child be educated by the law." (child = τὸ παιδίον, law = ὁ νόμος)
  2. "Let not the house be guarded by the enemy."
  3. "Let the word be answered by the king." (word = ὁ λόγος)

Answers 4

  1. τὸ παιδίον ὑπὸ τοῦ νόμου παιδευέσθω.
  2. μὴ ἡ οἰκία ὑπὸ τοῦ πολεμίου φυλασσέσθω.
  3. ὁ λόγος ὑπὸ τοῦ βασιλέως ἀποκρινέσθω.

Template: subject (acted upon) + ὑπό + genitive agent + 3rd person middle/passive imperative -έσθω. Prohibition: insert μὴ before the whole command (Lesson 7). Sentence 2: ὁ πολέμιος substantivized "the enemy" (Lessons 5/9), genitive τοῦ πολεμίου (o-pattern).

Exercise 5 – Ask and answer (drilling ἀποκρίνομαι)

Translate the question, then answer in Greek (a short answer, possibly a nominal clause – Lesson 3):

  1. "Τί βλέπει ὁ Διογένης;"
  2. "Πῶς πίνει τὸ παιδίον;"
    (πῶς = how?)
  3. "Τί ἀπορρίπτει ὁ Διογένης;"

Answers 5

  1. "What does he see?" → βλέπει παιδίον (or: τὸ παιδίον βλέπει) – "he sees a child".
  2. "How does the child drink?" → ἐκ τῶν χειρῶν πίνει – "it drinks from its hands".
  3. "What does Diogenes throw away?" → τὸ ποτήριον ἀπορρίπτει – "he throws away the cup".

This is the ask–answer pattern to use right in class: a question with τί (Lesson 5) + an elliptical answer.

Exercise 6 – Cumulative passage (Lessons 1–10), translate + analyze

ἐν ταῖς Ἀθήναις ὁ Πλάτων περὶ τῶν φυλάκων γράφει· ὁ δὲ Διογένης ἐν ταῖς ὁδοῖς μένει καὶ παιδίον βλέπει. τὸ παιδίον ἐκ τῶν χειρῶν πίνει· διὸ ὁ Διογένης τὸ ποτήριον ἀπορρίπτει καὶ λέγει· ἡττῶμαι ὑπὸ παιδίου ἐν τῇ ἁπλότητι τοῦ βίου. οἱ δὲ ἄνθρωποι θαυμάζουσιν καὶ ἀποκρίνονται· σὺ εὖ πράττεις, ὦ Διόγενες.

Notes:

  • ἐν ταῖς ὁδοῖς = in the streets
  • μένω = stay
  • θαυμάζω = admire

Answers 6

"In Athens Plato writes about the guardians; Diogenes stays in the streets and sees a child. The child drinks from its hands; so Diogenes throws away the cup and says: I am beaten by a child, in the simplicity of life. The people admire and answer: you do well, O Diogenes."

Structure notes: (1) ἐν ταῖς Ἀθήναις / ἐν ταῖς ὁδοῖς – dative plural after ἐν (Lesson 1); Ἀθῆναι is a plural noun (its original meaning "the villages of Athena") so the dative article is ταῖς. (2) μὲν … δὲ contrasts the two philosophers (Lesson 1). (3) διὸ draws the conclusion (Reading I). (4) ἡττῶμαι ὑπὸ παιδίου – a deponent carrying an agent like a passive (section 5). (5) θαυμάζουσιν takes movable ν before a vowel (καὶ): θαυμάζουσιν καὶ ἀποκρίνονται. (6) σὺ εὖ πράττεις, ὦ Διόγενες – vocative singular of a name ending in -ης: the ὦ Σώκρατες template (Lesson 2); εὖ the irregular adverb (Lesson 9); πράττεις 2nd person active.
