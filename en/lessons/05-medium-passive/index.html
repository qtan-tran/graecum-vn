What you will be able to do after this lesson: (1) recognize and conjugate the present middle/passive in the indicative, imperative and infinitive; (2) choose correctly among the possible translations (passive with "is…-ed", impersonal "they…", four styles of middle); (3) recognize deponent verbs and translate them with active meaning; (4) translate adjectives used as nouns (τὸ ἀγαθόν = "the good"); (5) tell apart τί (the question word, accented) from τις/τι (enclitic, indefinite).

1. The present middle/passive endings

1.1 The most important discovery of the lesson

Lesson 2 covered all six persons of the active. Today we fill the last box of the present system: middle and passive. The most important discovery: in the present tense, the middle and passive forms are completely identical! That is why they are treated together as middle/passive. Whether a form "is" middle or passive is not decided by the ending – it lies in the context and in the verb stem itself (section 2).

2nd person singular: ending -η or -ει? The ending -η regularly appears only in the familiar deponent group: μάχομαι (fight), οἴομαι (believe), βούλομαι (want), δέομαι (ask for, need) – see section 3: μάχῃ, βούλῃ, οἴῃ mákhēi, boúlēi, oíēi. Other verbs have -ει – and this ending is identical in form with the active 3rd person! So πέμπει can be active 3 Sg. ("he sends") or middle/passive 2 Sg. ("you send for yourself / you are sent") – only the context can tell them apart.

You only need to learn one 2 Sg. form well: active πέμπε, middle/passive πέμπου. The 3rd person imperative follows in Lesson 7; the subjunctive and optative moods are not yet.

2. One form, several translations: passive & middle

2.1 What does "middle/passive" mean?

The same form can be understood (and translated) in two voices. Take θεραπεύομαι therapeúomai: (1) present indicative middle: "I heal (for myself)"; (2) present indicative passive: "I am healed". The decision comes from the context and from the verb itself (the vocabulary table notes how each verb is used).

2.2 How do the three voices differ?

  • Active: the subject is the doer of the action: πέμπω τὸν ἄγγελον – "I send the messenger".
  • Passive: the subject is acted upon; the doer (the agent) is either not mentioned or expressed with ὑπό + [Gen.]: ὁ ἄγγελος ὑπὸ τοῦ βασιλέως πέμπεται – "the messenger is sent by the king". English uses "is sent", or the impersonal rendering "they send…".
  • Middle – the voice with no exact counterpart in English – the subject both performs the action and is involved in it: a double role, or an intermediate role.

2.3 Translation 1: as a passive sentence

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ Ἑρμῆς πέμπεται πρὸς τὸν Ἥλιον.ho Hermês pémpetai pròs tòn Hélion.Hermes is sent to Helios.

Or translate the passive impersonally with an active verb: "they… / people…":

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ Σωκράτης σοφὸς λέγεται.ho Sōkrátēs sophòs légetai.They call Socrates wise. (lit.: Socrates is called wise.)

Pattern to reuse: λέγεται = "they say / they call" is one of the most common renderings you will meet when reading texts: σοφὸς λέγεται sophòs légetai – "he is considered wise". Latin translates it exactly the same way: dicitur = "they say". Tip: when you meet the endings -ται / -νται and the sentence leaves it unclear "who does it" → try inserting "they/people" in front; the sentence usually flows at once.

2.4 Translation 2: middle

In the middle, the subject does not merely perform the action – it is itself affected by the action, or especially deeply involved in it. Four styles of use (the boundaries between them are not absolute – this is a way of describing, not a hard law):

a) Direct reflexive: the action turns back onto the subject itself

GreekTransliterationEnglish
κοσμέομαι.kosméomai.I adorn myself.
παρασκευάζομαι.paraskeuázomai.I prepare myself.

b) Indirect reflexive: the action is for the subject's own benefit

GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἰκίαν μισθόομαι.oikían misthóomai.I rent a house for myself.

A classic exam pair: the same verb, active and middle, yields two opposite meanings: οἰκίαν μισθόω oikían misthóō (active) = "I rent out a house"; οἰκίαν μισθόομαι (middle) = "I rent a house (for myself)". The vocabulary table records the two meanings separately. Self-test: ask "who benefits from this action?" – the subject itself → middle.

c) Causative: "let, allow"

GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἱ φαῦλοι τοῖς κακοῖς ταράττονται.hoi phaûloi toîs kakoîs taráttontai.The base let themselves be shaken by bad things.

Morphology note: ταράττω taráttō is a -ττω contract verb; 3 Pl. middle/passive: stem ταράττ- + -ονται → ταράττονται. The dative τοῖς κακοῖς answers "by/through what?" – an adverbial complement of the action (Lesson 4: a close relative of the dative of means).

d) Dynamic middle: deep involvement, a change of state

GreekTransliterationEnglish
εἰρήνην ποιέομαι.eirḗnēn poiéomai.I make peace.

This style is especially important with the deponent verbs (section 3).

Self-test strategy when you meet a middle/passive form: ask in order: (1) Does the passive meaning "is…-ed" make sense? → translate passively (or with "they…"). (2) It does not? → try an active translation with "myself / for myself / let", or a plain active. (3) Is the verb a deponent (check the vocabulary table)? → always translate actively. The notes in the vocabulary (such as "for oneself", "let") are precisely the hints for the right style.

3. Deponent verbs: middle/passive form, active meaning

By default, every verb "starts out" in the active – the dictionary form (the vocabulary entry) is the 1 Sg. present indicative active, from which the middle and passive are built. But Greek has a number of verbs that have middle/passive form while carrying active meaning:

Greek (dictionary form)TransliterationTypeEnglish
★ μάχομαιmákhomaideponentfight
★ βούλομαιboúlomaideponentwant, wish
★ οἴομαιoíomaideponentbelieve, suppose (triggers an AcI – Lesson 4)
★ δέομαιdéomaideponentask for; need (+ [Gen.])

They are called deponent verbs (Latin deponere = "to put down, lay aside"): they have "laid aside" their active form → they have no active form at all, and are translated with active meaning.

The classic beginner's error: seeing the ending -ται and translating passively. But μάχεται mákhetai = "he fights", not "he is fought"! Self-test: meet a middle/passive form → look it up in the vocabulary; the meaning is active and the dictionary form already ends in -ομαι → deponent → translate actively. A further recognition sign: the familiar deponent group has 2 Sg. -η (section 1.2): μάχῃ, βούλῃ, οἴῃ.

Latin corner: this phenomenon is not unique to Greek – Latin has its familiar series of deponentia: sequor "I follow", loquor "I speak", patior "I suffer" – passive in form, active in meaning, and they too have their own 2 Sg. ending (loquerīs). Compare the endings pair by pair: Greek -ται / -νται ↔ Latin -tur / -ntur. The common source is the "middle voice" of ancient Indo-European: the subject is both agent and involved in the action – two sister languages, each preserving part of that heritage.

4.2 Adjectives without a head noun: the article "wraps" the adjective into a noun

  • The adjective stands alone, with the article (rarely without).
  • Very common in the neuter singular / plural or the masculine plural.
  • Translation: the adjective is substantivized (used as a noun).

Some adjectives are substantivized so often that the vocabulary lists their noun meaning separately:

Greek (adjective)TransliterationAdjective meaningSubstantivized formEnglish

GreekTransliterationEnglish
τὸ ἀγαθόνtò agathónthe good

ὁ ἐχθρός / οἱ ἐχθροίho ekhthrós / hoi ekhthroíthe enemy / the enemies

The neuter plural has two legitimate translations: τὰ ἀγαθά = (1) translate it like the substantivized singular ("the good"), or (2) supply a noun: "good things". In philosophical texts (Plato!) it is almost always style (1); in narrative texts usually style (2), with the meaning "possessions, goods". The key point: the adjective must not be left "floating" without a noun in your translation – English requires you to choose the corresponding "the… / … things" rendering. The crasis form τἀγαθόν is not yet (later).

Pattern to reuse: English already performs exactly this operation when it says "the good", "the bad" – the article "the" is precisely the τὸ of the Greeks. Latin does the same: bonum = τὸ ἀγαθόν. Self-test: article + adjective with no noun standing next to it → it is certainly substantivized.

5. τις, τι: "someone, something"

5.1 Review of Lesson 2: the question word τί;

τί; tí (always accented!) = "what? which? why?" – a question word, freely placed, very often at the start of the sentence.

5.2 The unaccented form: the indefinite pronoun

The same three-letter sequence, but without an accent → a completely different meaning: the indefinite pronoun:

GreekTransliterationGenderEnglish
τις / τινέςtis / tinésm./f. (Pl.)someone, some people
τιtineutersomething

Genitive (all three genders): τινός or τινος tinós / tinos.

5.3 The full paradigm of τις, τι

Masculine and feminine share one form (only the neuter has its own); the plural separates m./f. from the neuter as usual:

Sg. m./f.Sg. n.Pl. m./f.Pl. n.
Nom.τιςτιτινέςτινά
Acc.τινάτιτινάςτινά
Gen.τινόςτινῶν
Dat.τινίτισί(ν)

Transliteration: tis, ti, tiná, tinós, tiní, tinés, tinás, tiná, tinôn, tisí(n).

  • Accent rule of the enclitic: the one-syllable forms (τις, τι) carry no accent; the two-syllable forms (τινά, τινές, τινάς, τινί) keep an accent of their own on the final syllable, while the word before them may still receive an extra accent – as already seen: ἄνθρωπός τις, λόγόσ τις.
  • Two variants: the genitive τινός / τινος are both correct; the dative plural τισί(ν) has the movable ν (Lesson 2).
  • The question word τίς, τί follows exactly this paradigm too, but always carries its accent on the first syllable: accusative τίνα "whom?", dative τίνι "to whom?", plural τίνες "who (pl.)?". One rule of thumb for the whole table: question = accent on the first syllable; indefinite = accent on the final syllable or none at all.
  • This pattern has adverb siblings: πότε "when?" (question word, accent on the first syllable) vs ποτέ "at some time, ever" (indefinite, enclitic – you will meet it in the Lesson 10 vocabulary): the very same accent rule carried over to another word family, so learn the frame once and reuse it in many places.

5.4 Two styles of use: alone & attached to a noun

GreekTransliterationEnglishNote

Rule of thumb: standing alone = "someone / something"; attached to a noun = "a certain X / a few X" – exactly point (3) of the Achtung box above.

Three stumbling points: (1) The accent is the only thing that distinguishes the question word from the indefinite pronoun: τί λέγει; tí légei = "what is he saying?" – τις λέγει. tis légei = "someone is speaking." (2) τις / τι is an enclitic (Lesson 4: like μου, μοι, με) – it never stands at the start of a sentence, and the word before it may take an extra acute: λόγόσ τις lógos tis "a certain word". (3) Do not reverse it: "noun + τις" = "a certain X"; τις alone = "someone".

6. Reading: three maxims about the wise man (simplified)

Source: the theme and the genre of the "ethical maxim in A–B dialogue" follow the spirit of the ancient maxim collections (Stobaios II 7,11g–i preserves many philosophical maxims; Marcus Aurelius, Tà eis heautón 9,40 is likewise in the self-questioning genre "the base man…, the wise man…"). The original (public domain) can be read on Perseus. The version below was rewritten by this project for the level of Lesson 5 – each sentence showcases one tool of this lesson: deponent, dynamic middle, causative middle, passive with ὑπό + [Gen.], "they…", substantivization, indefinite pronoun.

Simplified version (composed by this project):

τινὲς τὰ κακὰ ποιεῖν βούλονται· ὁ δὲ σοφὸς οὐ μάχεται, εἰρήνην γὰρ ποιεῖται. οἱ μὲν φαῦλοι τοῖς κακοῖς ταράττονται· ὁ δὲ σοφὸς οὐ ὑπὸ τῶν κακῶν ταράττεται. μακάριος οὖν ὁ σοφὸς λέγεται.

Tinès tà kakà poieîn boúlontai· ho dè sophòs ou mákhetai, eirḗnēn gàr poiéitai. Hoi mèn phaûloi toîs kakoîs taráttontai· ho dè sophòs ou hypò tôn kakôn taráttetai. Makários oûn ho sophòs légetai.

English (close translation)
Some people want to do evil things; but the wise man does not fight, for he makes peace. The base are shaken by bad things; but the wise man is not shaken by the bad. Therefore the wise man is called blessed.

Quick analysis (check with the tools of this lesson):

  • τινὲς τὰ κακὰ ποιεῖν βούλονται: the plural indefinite pronoun τινές as subject; τὰ κακά substantivized (section 4); βούλονται 3 Pl. deponent – translate actively "want"; ποιεῖν an object infinitive of βούλομαι (Lesson 2: not an AcI – there is no accusative subject).
  • ὁ σοφὸς οὐ μάχεται, εἰρήνην γὰρ ποιεῖται: two deponent forms in a row – μάχεται "fights"; ποιεῖται dynamic middle "makes (for himself)" (section 2.4d); γὰρ postpositive in the second slot (Lesson 1).
  • οἱ μὲν φαῦλοι … ὁ δὲ σοφός: the pair μέν … δέ contrasting the two figures (Lesson 1); ταράττονται causative middle (section 2.4c) with the dative τοῖς κακοῖς "by bad things".
  • ὁ δὲ σοφὸς οὐ ὑπὸ τῶν κακῶν ταράττεται: the same verb, but this time translated passively – the agent ὑπό + [Gen.] (section 2.2). One form, two translations – the whole lesson condensed into a single verb.
  • μακάριος οὖν ὁ σοφὸς λέγεται: "they call…" (section 2.3); note that the passive sentence keeps two nominatives (subject ὁ σοφός + predicate noun μακάριος) – the exact mirror image of the double accusative in the AcI (Lesson 4): embedded in a passive sentence, they stay nominative; only when embedded in an AcI do they turn into accusatives.

Reading comprehension questions: (1) Find the four deponent forms in the text and name their dictionary forms. (2) ταράττονται and ταράττεται are the same verb – why is one sentence translated "let themselves be shaken" and the other "is shaken"? What is the deciding factor? (3) μακάριος οὖν ὁ σοφὸς λέγεται: if translated with "they…", who is the doer of the "calling"? Why do we not need to name that person?

Answers to the reading questions (print to check)

1. βούλονται (βούλομαι), μάχεται (μάχομαι), ποιεῖται (ποιέομαι – the middle of ποιέω used like a deponent in this meaning), and ταράττεται too, in its causative-middle translation. 2. Because the middle and passive forms coincide in the present (section 1.1): the first sentence has the instrumental dative "by bad things" and describes how the base allow themselves to be affected → middle; the second names the agent explicitly with ὑπό + [Gen.] "by the bad" → passive. The deciding factors: context + sentence structure, not the ending. 3. People in general – "they" – because Greek allows a passive sentence to leave the agent unnamed; the "they + active" rendering (section 2.3) is precisely the tool for reading naturally without inventing a subject that is not in the text.

Exercise 1 – Analyzing forms (person · number · mood · voice)

Analyze the following forms of θεραπεύω and μάχομαι:

  1. θεραπεύεται
  2. θεραπευόμεθα
  3. θεραπεύου
  4. θεραπεύεσθαι
  5. μάχῃ

Answer 1

  1. 3 Sg. present indicative middle/passive (he is healed / he heals for himself).
  2. 1 Pl. present indicative middle/passive (we are healed / we heal for ourselves).
  3. 2 Sg. present imperative middle/passive (let yourself be healed! / heal yourself!).
  4. Present infinitive middle/passive (to be healed / to heal for oneself).
  5. 2 Sg. present indicative of the deponent μάχομαι (you fight) – the ending -η, one of the few verbs that keeps this form.

Exercise 2 – Translating middle/passive (choose the fitting rendering)

  1. ὁ Ἑρμῆς πέμπεται πρὸς τὸν Ἥλιον.
  2. ὁ φιλόσοφος πρὸς τὸν βασιλέα πέμπεται.
  3. ὁ Σωκράτης σοφὸς λέγεται.
  4. εἰρήνην ποιούμεθα.
  5. κοσμεῖσθε.

Answer 2

  1. Passive: Hermes is sent to Helios.
  2. Passive: The philosopher is sent to the king.
  3. Passive, "they" style: They call Socrates wise. (lit.: Socrates is called wise.)
  4. Dynamic middle: We make peace. – the ending -όμεθα: 1 Pl.
  5. κοσμεῖσθε kosmeîsthe = 2 Pl. middle/passive: indicative "you (pl.) adorn yourselves" or imperative "adorn yourselves!" – only the context can tell them apart (2 Pl. indicative = imperative).

Exercise 3 – Deponent (remember: translate with active meaning!)

  1. οἱ Ἀθηναῖοι μάχονται.
  2. βουλόμεθα εἰρήνην ποιεῖν.
  3. οἴεσθε τὸν φίλον σοφὸν εἶναι.

Answer 3

  1. The Athenians fight. – 3 Pl. deponent μάχομαι.
  2. We want to make peace. – βουλόμεθα 1 Pl. deponent; ποιεῖν is an object infinitive (Lesson 2: not an AcI – there is no accusative subject at all!).
  3. You suppose the friend to be wise. – οἴομαι is an AcI trigger verb (Lesson 4): accusative subject τὸν φίλον + infinitive predicate εἶναι, with the double accusative σοφὸν.

Exercise 4 – Adjectives as nouns

Translate:

  1. τὸ ἀγαθόν
  2. τὰ ἀγαθά
  3. ὁ ἐχθρός / οἱ ἐχθροί
  4. τὸ κακόν / τὰ κακά

Answer 4

  1. the good
  2. the good / good things
  3. the enemy / the enemies
  4. the evil / evil things

Common denominator: article + adjective with no head noun → substantivized.

Exercise 5 – τί or τις/τι?

Fill in the correct form and translate:

  1. ___ λέγεις;
  2. ___ λέγει.
  3. ὁ φιλόσοφος ποιεῖ ___ ἀγαθόν.
  4. λόγος ___ (genitive: "a word of someone")
  5. ὁ φιλόσοφος ___ παιδεύει. (accusative: "the philosopher teaches someone")
  6. τίνα παιδεύεις; (translate)
  7. ἐν λόγῳ ___ ("in a certain word")

Answer 5

  1. τί λέγεις; – What are you saying? – question word, always accented.
  2. τις λέγει. – Someone is speaking. – enclitic, unaccented.
  3. ὁ φιλόσοφος ποιεῖ τι ἀγαθόν. – The philosopher does something good. – τι neuter, modifying ἀγαθόν.
  4. λόγος τινός / τινος – a word of someone. – genitive, both variants correct.
  5. ὁ φιλόσοφος τινὰ παιδεύει. – The philosopher teaches someone. – accusative of the indefinite pronoun; the dictionary records τινά, and before another word the acute changes to a grave.
  6. τίνα παιδεύεις; – Whom do you teach? – question word in the accusative: the accent always on the first syllable.
  7. ἐν λόγῳ τινί – in a certain word. – singular dative after ἐν (Lesson 4).

Exercise 6 – Translating into Greek

  1. "Hermes is sent by the king."
  2. "They call the friends wise."
  3. "The Athenians want to fight."

Answer 6

  1. ὁ Ἑρμῆς ὑπὸ τοῦ βασιλέως πέμπεται. – the agent with ὑπό + [Gen.]; the verb 3 Sg. middle/passive -εται.
  2. οἱ φίλοι σοφοὶ λέγονται. – 3 Pl. -ονται; the English prompt uses the "they…" style → Greek still uses the passive form.
  3. οἱ Ἀθηναῖοι μάχεσθαι βούλονται. (or βούλονται μάχεσθαι) – both verbs are deponent: both carry middle/passive form yet translate fully actively; μάχεσθαι is the middle/passive infinitive (ending -εσθαι).
