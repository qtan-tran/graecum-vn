1. The three degrees: positive, comparative, superlative

When a language talks about a quality, it has three degrees available: the positive (the plain degree, no comparing), the comparative and the superlative. English says "wise – wiser – wisest" or "wise – more wise – most wise"; German says "weis – weiser – am weisesten". Greek already owns its full adjective stock (Lesson 9: the o/a group, the third-declension group, two-ending and three-ending adjectives alike) – what is added now are two sets of comparison endings attached to the adjective's stem:

DegreeEnglishGreek (model σοφός)What it says
positive (plain degree)wiseσοφός, σοφή, σοφόν"wise" – states the quality, not yet comparing it with anyone
comparativewiserσοφώτερος, -τέρα, -τερον"wiser (than someone/something)" – or elative "rather wise"
superlativewisestσοφώτατος, -τάτη, -τατον"wisest (of the group)" – or elative "very wise"

Two things to remember right from the start:

  • Both adjectives and adverbs take comparison. The positive adverb is built from the adjective with the -ως suffix of Lesson 9 (σοφῶς "in a wise way"); the comparative and superlative of adverbs have their own pattern – section 5.
  • Morphologically, Greek has only two comparison "types": Type I -τερος / -τατος (the widest) and Type II -ίων / -ιστος (short, with a trimmed stem). Beyond them lies one small irregular group – learn it by sight and be done. Which type an adjective takes is decided by the adjective, not by you: but once you know the two patterns, you can "unpack" any form you meet.

Latin corner: Latin does the same job with -ior (m./f.) / -ius (neuter) for the comparative and -issimus for the superlative: sapiēns → sapientior → sapientissimus ↔ σοφός → σοφώτερος → σοφώτατος. One difference worth knowing: Latin folds both genders into the single ending -ior, while Greek declines the comparative through all three genders like a complete o/a adjective (σοφώτερος, σοφωτέρα, σοφώτερον).

2. Type I: -τερος / -τατος – the most widespread pattern

The formula is compact: the positive plus one printing block:

-τερος, -τέρα, -τερον = comparative; -τατος, -τάτη, -τατον = superlative

Attach it to the masculine stem of the positive adjective. Comparison works "like the positive plus one printing block": keep the stem that the positive would use before attaching an ending. The result is always a three-ending adjective declined in the o/a group – which means the familiar declension table of Lesson 1 applies unchanged: δικαιότερος, δικαιοτέρας, δικαιότερον… δικαιοτέρων…. Three secondary rules come with it:

Zusatzregel – the rule for ν-stems: a two-ending third-declension adjective in -ων/-ον (Lesson 9: εὐδαίμων, εὐδαίμον) enters Type I by inserting -έσ- between the bare stem and the ending: εὐδαιμον-έσ-τερος → comparative εὐδαιμονέστερος, -τέρα, -τερον; εὐδαιμον-έσ-τατος → superlative εὐδαιμονέστατος, -τάτη, -τατον. Same pattern: σώφρων → σωφρωνέστερος. Distinguish it from Type II (section 3): ἡδίων, ἡδίον is the comparative of ἡδύς with a trimmed stem; the Zusatzregel keeps the stem intact and only adds the buffer step -έσ-. Self-check: remove the comparison ending, strip -έσ- off, and what remains must be exactly the original adjective's stem (εὐδαιμον-).

Three observations to make the system run in a chain:

  • The accent always jumps onto the linking vowel: read and write δικαιότατος, σοφώτερος, ἀληθέστερος – no matter where the positive carries its accent. This is also the quick recognition sign: an accent on the vowel directly before -τερος/-τατος means a Type I comparison.
  • Comparison is the door to "three-ending-hood": every adjective, whether two-ending or with a single m./f. form, takes all three genders once it enters the Type I comparison. The reverse holds too: meet an o/a form with three endings whose positive is two-ending → chances are you are looking at a comparative (ἀδικώτερος – no base-degree adjective "ἀδικώτερος" exists).
  • The -ο-→-ω- rule is a rule about a short penultimate syllable, not a rule about particular words: σοφός (penultimate σο short) → σοφώτερος; but δίκαιος (penultimate και long) → keeps -ο-: δικαιότερος. Self-check: measure the penultimate syllable of the positive before you write.

Achtung – do not build a form "σοφαίτερος": the stem to attach the ending to is the masculine stem of the positive, not a "shared stem" or a "feminine stem": σοφ- (from σοφός) + -ώτερος = σοφώτερος; δίκαι- (from δίκαιος) + -ότερος = δικαιότερος. The error of "taking the wrong stem" produces non-existent forms like *σοφαίτερος or *δικαίτερος. Quick test: replace the comparative ending with the masculine positive ending (-ος) and see whether you get the base word back.

3. Type II: -ίων / -ιστος – the short pattern with a trimmed stem

Many familiar adjectives do not use the long Type I pattern but a shorter pair of endings: comparative -ίων and superlative -ιστος. Three differences from Type I:

  • The stem is "trimmed" before the ending is attached: there is no linking vowel -ο-/-ω-; the adjective's stem loses its final part. Base example: ἡδύς, ἡδεῖα, ἡδύ "sweet, pleasant" → only ἡδ- remains → ἡδίων "more pleasant", ἥδιστος "most pleasant".
  • The two degrees decline in two different ways: the Type II comparative is a two-ending third-declension adjective (like εὐδαίμων, εὐδαίμον from Lesson 9): nominative ἡδίων, ἡδίονος, neuter ἡδίον; while the superlative declines three-ending in the o/a group like Type I: ἥδιστος, ἥδιστη, ἥδιστον.
  • Breathing marks and accent position must be learned word by word (table below) – Type II has few members, so learning the whole set by sight is realistic.

One row you already know from Lesson 9, now with its proper name: the pair βελτίων, βέλτιον "better" (Lesson 9 promised "a thorough review in the comparison lesson" – this is the lesson) is the Type II comparative without a standing positive; its superlative is βέλτιστος, -η, -ον "best". Close kin: κρείττων, κρεῖττον "mightier, superior" with κράτιστος "utterly strong, best of all" – these two likewise have no standing positive; ceremonial greetings and forms of address use κράτιστε – a vocative, read as an honorific "most excellent one" (you met the typical greeting + vocative construction with Protagoras in Lesson 14).

Achtung – dare to see two layers in "-ων/-ον": the nominative pair -ων, -ον appears on both layers: (1) positive two-ending adjectives of the third declension (εὐδαίμων, εὐδαίμον, σώφρων, σῶφρον – Lesson 9); (2) the Type II comparative (ἡδίων, ἡδίον, βελτίων, βέλτιον). Tell them apart by three signs: (a) the Type II comparative of a familiar adjective usually trims the stem against the positive (ἡδ-, not ἡδύ-ων); (b) around it often stands ἤ or a contrasting genitive (section 6); (c) in the dictionary, the entry says "comparative of X". When unsure, read by the sense for now and look it up later – no Greek sentence at this layer allows only a single reading.

Pattern to apply: the Type II and irregular tables are "learn-by-sight" tables – do not try to derive, just read them aloud like phone numbers: ἀγαθός → ἀμείνων → ἄριστος; κακός → χείρων → χείριστος; μέγας → μείζων → μέγιστος; πολύς → πλείων → πλείστος; ὀλίγος → ἐλάττων → ἐλάχιστος; ταχύς → θάσσων → τάχιστος; ἡδύς → ἡδίων → ἥδιστος. The first six pairs belong to the "upper deck" of every piece of argumentative prose – Plato's Apology (reading I) pulls out four pairs in a single passage.

Achtung – the "orphan comparative" (defective comparative): βελτίων and κρείττων have no corresponding positive and yet serve in serious literary use: they are Greek's "born-comparative" adjectives. The practical consequence: when you meet a -ων/-ον form and cannot find a matching positive, do not force yourself "to hunt the base adjective" – the dictionary will simply say "comparative". And when translating back into Greek, "better" in formal prose usually chooses ἀμείνων/βέλτιον rather than any Type I "ποιη" coinage.

Latin corner: Latin keeps the parallel irregular set: bonus → melior → optimus ↔ ἀγαθός → ἀμείνων → ἄριστος; magnus → maior → maximus ↔ μέγας → μείζων → μέγιστος; multus → plūs → plūrimus ↔ πολύς → πλείων → πλείστος. It is the same "trend of trimming frequently used words" that produced -ior/-issimus in Latin and -ίων/-ιστος in Greek. Anyone studying the two languages in parallel still needs to learn a single logical table – with two sets of labels.

5. The periphrastic route μᾶλλον / μάλιστα & comparison of adverbs

The periphrastic route: comparing without changing shape

Just as English may choose "more beautiful" instead of "beautifuller", Greek may leave the adjective untouched and add an intensifying adverb:

FormulaExampleTranslation
μᾶλλον + positiveμᾶλλον φίλος"more of a friend", "rather a friend"
μάλιστα + positiveμάλιστα φίλος"most of all a friend", "a true friend above all"

This periphrastic route fits especially well when: the adjective is long or complex (compound adjectives), when you want to stress the idea "more, and yet the word 'friend' keeps its shape, no change of form", or when a philosophical argument grades a degree rather than competing (μᾶλλον is also the handy adverb for other comparisons: "and more than that"). Both words are already familiar faces: μᾶλλον "more, rather" has appeared among the adverbs/assertions of the early lessons; μάλιστα "above all, most of all" is the crowning word of an assertion – and both now take on one new job: forming the degrees of comparison.

Comparison of adverbs: compact, inside two neuter forms

Lesson 9 gave the pattern for the positive adverb: neuter stem + -ως (σοφῶς, ἀληθῶς, εὐδαιμόνως). The two remaining degrees need no new pattern – just open the right two rows of the comparative adjective table:

Degree of the adverbWhere to take itModel σοφόςModel ταχύς (irregular)Meaning
positiveneuter stem + -ως (Lesson 9)σοφῶςταχέωςwisely / quickly
comparativesingular neuter of the comparative adjectiveσοφώτερονθᾶττονmore wisely / faster
superlativeplural neuter of the superlative adjectiveσοφότατατάχισταmost wisely / fastest, as fast as possible

The principle "one ending, two lives": the neuter singular of the comparative (-τερον / -ον) and the neuter plural of the superlative (-τατα / -ιστα) take on the adverb's role. Meet τάχιστα in ancient letters ("as fast as possible!") or θᾶττον in the folk song "run faster" – you are looking at an adverb, though the shape is a "neuter adjective". The Greeks made no distinction between the two jobs: the comparative adverb is the neuter form of that adjective.

Pattern to apply: to form or translate a comparative adverb, ask exactly two questions: (1) "is this the comparative or the superlative degree?" – comparative → singular; superlative → plural. (2) "which type does the base adjective belong to?" – Type I / Type II / irregular: take the right row of the table above. To say "speak more truthfully, act more wisely" in everyday language: ἀληθέστερον λέγειν, σοφωτέρον πράττειν – the ending -τερον is exactly the "more" of the manner.

Latin corner: Latin builds comparative adverbs on exactly the neuter shape of the comparative adjective: fortius (n. sg. of fortior) = "more bravely" ↔ εὐψυχότερον; certissime … the superlative takes -issimē from -issimus. All three degrees of the Latin adverb likewise come from the neuter (sg. comp., pl. superl.) – the principle "one ending, two lives" belongs to both sisters, not a Greek quirk.

6. Comparing with a partner: ἤ + the same case, or the genitive of comparison?

Where there is "wiser", there must be "wiser than whom". Greek opens two doors for the partner of the comparison (Vergleichsglied):

Door 1: ἤ "than" + the same case as the word compared

ἤ stands between the two sides being compared; the form of the second side must exactly match the case of the first – one and the same "mirror image":

Greek sentenceAnalysis of the two sidesTranslation
ὁ διδάσκαλος σοφώτερος ἤ σὺ ἐστιν.σοφώτερος (nom.) ↔ σὺ (nom.)"The teacher is wiser than you."
τοῦτον μᾶλλον ἢ ἐκεῖνον ἀγαπῶ.τοῦτον (acc.) ↔ ἐκεῖνον (acc.)"I love this man more than that man."

Door 2: the genitive of comparison (genitive of comparison)

Greek may drop ἤ entirely and put the partner into the genitive:

Greek sentenceContrastTranslation
ὁ διδάσκαλος σοφώτερος σοῦ ἐστιν.σου = genitive of σύ"The teacher is wiser than you." – same sentence, different door
σοφώτερος τῶν ἄλλωντῶν ἄλλων genitive plural"wiser than (all) the others"

The two doors differ subtly in shade: the ἤ door works like a clean subtraction ("more than him, than me, than someone"); the genitive door is more discreet, tucking the partner "inside" the adjective phrase – an excellent fit when the partner is a pronoun (σοῦ, ἐμοῦ, αὐτοῦ). In Attic argumentative prose: partner a personal pronoun → the genitive is close to the default; partner a full clause (ἢ … + verb) → ἤ is compulsory.

Achtung – ἤ has two jobs and is easy to drop: (1) after a comparison: "than"; (2) standing between two options: "or" – σοφὸς ἢ ἀγαθός can be "wise or good" and is not yet "wiser than good"! Alongside that, ἤ writes its variant ἢ after a vowel – the same word. How to tell them apart: after a comparative expect a noun/pronoun matching the first side; between two options expect καὶ…καὶ or a choosing-question context. Do not confuse it with ἦ "indeed, surely" (familiar from the early lessons: the stage opening "ἦ…", "indeed…") – one breathing mark, a completely different job.

Pattern to apply: when translating "X is wiser than Y" into Greek, decide in three steps: (1) X is the subject → ὁ X σοφώτερος… (2) Y a pronoun → put σοῦ/ἐμοῦ/ἡμῶν into the genitive; Y a noun or clause → ἤ + the noun in the same case. (3) Check: the two cases on either side of the ἤ door must fit each other like the two halves of a key.

7. The superlative: the partitive genitive & translating the elative "quite, very"

Superlative with a named group: two patterns to place the group

"Wisest" means wisest within a group. That group is stated in two ways:

PatternGreek sentenceDissectionTranslation
partitive genitiveὁ διδάσκαλος σοφώτατος πάντων ἐστίν.πάντων = genitive of πᾶς "all" – "of/among all""The teacher is the wisest of all."
σοφώτατος τῶν πολιτῶντῶν πολιτῶν genitive plural – "the citizens""wisest among the citizens"
noun in the same case (sequential predicate)ὁ διδάσκαλος σοφώτατος ἄνθρωπός ἐστιν.ἄνθρωπός qualifies σοφώτατος, both nominative"The teacher is the wisest of human beings."

The partitive genitive is a use you have already met under another title: τῶν δοκούντων σοφῶν "one of those considered wise" in Lesson 14, or τῶν πολιτικῶν "among the politicians" in Lesson 13 – back then we skimmed it as an "attributive genitive"; now its proper name: it names the whole of which the thing mentioned is one part / the peak. Placed after a superlative is its classic address.

No group named: a true superlative, or the elative "very"?

Meet ὁ διδάσκαλος σοφώτατός ἐστιν – with no group anywhere around the sentence – and Greek leaves two readings open:

ReadingConditionTranslation
true superlativethe context has already fixed the group ("among the teachers", "in the city"…), the group is simply not repeated"is the wisest (of them)"
elative (absolute degree)no group hidden anywhere; the idea is a degree beyond the ordinary"exceedingly wise", "very wise"

The elative pattern is rarer with the comparative (translate "rather, somewhat, too": σοφώτερός ἐστιν can be "rather wise") but flourishes with the superlative: Greek historiography and oratory love -τατος/-ιστος as a blow that sweeps away an opponent. Reading I today offers both readings within a single passage.

Achtung – between comparison and elative there is only one testing instrument: the phrase "in which group?" Try adding "in which group?" after the adjective: if the surrounding story can name a plausible group (legitimately: "among the rhetoricians", "within the circle of master and pupils") → a true superlative; if the idea is "extraordinarily / urgently / remarkably strong" with nobody ranked nearby → translate it elatively "very, exceedingly". The classic beginner error: translating every -τατος as "most", which turns a Greek sentence into a "ranking table" exactly where the author only meant "extremely".

Latin corner: Latin has the same elative phenomenon: doctissimus is both "most learned" and "exceedingly learned"; and it likewise uses the genitive of the whole for the reference group: facilis princeps omnium "easily first among all" ↔ σφαλερώτατος πάντων. The Latin sister changes only one detail: the Latin comparative usually takes the ablative (ablative of comparison: doctior fratre) where Greek uses the genitive – one "joint" to note when studying the two in parallel.

Two notes for using the map:

  • The genitive of comparison and the partitive genitive differ only in the governing word: after the comparative → comparative genitive ("than those people"); after the superlative → partitive ("best of that group"). One and the same genitive shape – the sentence's governing word decides.
  • The row order of the map is not the order you meet it in texts: the map serves to name a genitive while reading, not to write your way through it. Meet a "floating" genitive, ask the diagnostic questions from top to bottom: whose? within which group? who does? at what time? – whichever row matches, that is its proper name.

The lesson's key pattern: comparative = the partner through two doors (ἤ + the same case / the genitive); superlative = the group through the partitive genitive; no partner, no group → ask "than whom? within which group?" – if no answer, choose the elative. These three questions are the hinge of the whole comparison system.

  • ἐγὼ γὰρ οὔτε μέγα οὔτε σμικρὸν σύνοιδα ἐμαυτῷ σοφὸς ὤν: οὔτε…οὔτε "neither… nor" joins the two accusatives μέγα … σμικρόν "something great, something small" (with "οὐδέν" understood, Lesson 3); ὤν a present participle "as one who is wise", learn to recognize Lesson 19; σύνοιδα ἐμαυτῷ – a reflexive dative (Lesson 15) governed by σύνοιδα.

No – the god speaks of all mankind, and the group is "all human beings": this is a true superlative, with the group understood from the god's own pronouncement.
  • ἦλθον οὖν ἐπί τινα τῶν δοκούντων σοφῶν εἶναι: the partitive genitive τῶν δοκούντων "one of those who…"; the ending -όντων is the present participle genitive plural "those who are deemed" (learn to recognize, Lesson 19); ἐπί + accusative marks direction "to meet someone"; the whole sentence: I went to visit one of those "with a reputation for wisdom".
  • ἔδοξέ μοι οὗτος ὁ ἀνὴρ ἑαυτῷ μὲν σοφὸς δοκεῖν εἶναι, ἄλλοις δὲ οὔ: δοκέω + dative + infinitive "to seem (to someone) to be…" – two contrasting datives: ἑαυτῷ reflexive (Lesson 15: to himself) μὲν… δὲ with ἄλλοις "others" (Lesson 9): "he thinks himself wise in his own eyes alone".
  • τούτου δὲ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἐγὼ σοφώτερός εἰμι: the syntactic centre of 09b: comparison + genitive of comparison (τούτου – "than this man"). A microcosm of contrast: identical in meaning to σοφώτερος ἤ οὗτος, only through the other door.
  • ἔοικα γοῦν τούτου γε σμικρῷ τινι αὐτῷ τούτῳ σοφώτερος εἶναι: the wry closing line: "I seem to be wiser than him merely on one tiny point" – genitive of comparison τούτου; the accusative σμικρὸν of the earlier sentence has here turned into the dative σμικρῷ τινι αὐτῷ τούτῳ "in respect of just this one small point" (the dative "in what respect?" – the point, the respect); γε stresses "in this one point alone". ἔοικα + infinitive: learn to recognize.

General grammar point to remember: passage 21a is one complete "comparison chain": a question in the comparative with the genitive (ἐμοῦ) → the god's answer in the superlative with understood group (μηδένα σοφώτερον) → Socrates reading himself into a doubtful elative superlative (σοφώτατον) → the humble conclusion in the comparative with the genitive (τούτου). That is the "three-degree map" of section 1 laid open on a real text.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. Five comparison forms, all Type I on the stem σοφός, no Type II and no irregular:

  • C2 εἴη σοφώτερος – comparative; the standard of comparison goes through the genitive door: ἐμοῦ. Personal pronouns (here the genitive of ἐγώ) almost default to the genitive door in elevated prose; ἤ ἐγώ is not ungrammatical, but rare.
  • C3 μηδένα σοφώτερον εἶναι – comparative; the standard of comparison is implicit in the group "among all men" (the negation pair οὐδείς / μηδείς, Lesson 15), so it need not be spelled out.
  • C6 σοφώτατον εἶναι – superlative, standing inside the indirect-speech frame after the participle φάσκων.
  • C10 σοφώτερός εἰμι – comparative; the standard of comparison goes through the genitive door: τούτου (demonstrative pronoun, Lesson 6).
  • C11 σοφώτερος εἶναι – comparative after the verb ἔοικα ("seems") + infinitive.

Step 2, Meaning and structure. The three-row table:

  • The god: wise in the superlative (σοφώτατον εἶναι, C6) – but that is an absolute oracle-saying; it does not yet say "wisest" in which sense.
  • The man considered wise: οἴεταί τι εἰδέναι οὐκ εἰδώς (C10) – "he thinks he knows something, while not knowing".
  • Socrates: οὔτε μέγα οὔτε σμικρὸν σύνοιδα (C5) – he claims to know nothing, "nothing great and nothing small".

Socrates' own conclusion: he is wiser than the "man considered wise" only σμικρῷ τινι (C11) – "by a little something": wiser in the single point of not thinking he knows what he does not know. The gap between the two men is measured by the very word σμικρόν that C5 used in the negation.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. Both readings stand: (a) genuine humility: Socrates at once sets out to test the god's words, in good faith toward tradition; (b) polite irony: the detail "wiser by exactly one tiny bit" lets us read the oracle as a witty understatement. If the god's sentence is read as elative ("the god is very wise") instead of a superlative within a group, the passage loses its whole test-the-claim plot: without a "wisest" there is no journey to question the politicians and the sophists. For today: "knowing that one does not know" is the starting point of the scientific method (all knowledge can be wrong), while "thinking one knows" is the everyday symptom of abundant information without method.

  • ὁ δὲ ὑπὸ τοῦ ψύχους καταπονούμενος ἔτι μᾶλλον καὶ περιττοτέραν ἐσθῆτα προσελάμβανεν: ὑπό + genitive "by" (Lessons 5/10); the circumstantial participle καταπονούμενος "being battered" (learn to recognize); the pair ἔτι μᾶλλον "still more"; and the 09b point: περιττοτέραν ἐσθῆτα – a Type I comparative adjective (περιττός, περιττοτέρα – mind the spelling: the original mixes περιττ- and περισσ-, two spellings of one and the same word) as attributive before "a thicker garment".
  • ἕως ὁ Βορέας ἀποκαμὼν τῷ Ἡλίῳ παρέδωκε: ἕως + subjunctive "until" (Lesson 22 sums the pair up as "as long as / until"); the aorist participle ἀποκαμὼν "after growing weary"; τῷ Ἡλίῳ παρέδωκε – the dative "handing over to"; the root δίδωμι "to give, to offer" + the prefix παρα- "handing over" (παραδίδωμι "to hand over, to deliver").
  • κἀκεῖνος τὸ μὲν πρῶτον μετρίως προσέλαμψε: crasis κἀκεῖνος = καὶ ἐκεῖνος (learn to recognize, Lesson 16, read as one word); προσέλαμψε 3rd-person active aorist of προσλάμπω "to shine upon" (a kinsman of λάμπω from Lesson 22: "λάμπεσθαι πᾶσα (everything) shines bright"); τὸ μὲν πρῶτον … μετρίως "at first… moderately" – the pair μέν… δέ stretches over both sides: "first gently, then (as the man undressed) fiercer".
  • τοῦ δὲ ἀνθρώπου τὰ περισσὰ τῶν ἱματίων ἀποτιθεμένου, σφοδρότερον τὸ καῦμα ἐπέτεινεν: a genitive present participle ἀποτιθεμένου "while (the man) took off (his clothes)"; τὰ περισσὰ τῶν ἱματίων – a substantivized neuter plural adjective "the extra pieces" (substantivization, Lesson 9) + the attributive genitive "of the garments" (Lesson 1); and the 09b point: σφοδρότερον – a comparative adverb, the neuter singular of the comparative adjective (section 5): "more fiercely"; the subject of the main clause is τὸ καῦμα "the burning heat" – the Sun hides behind the verb ἐπέτεινεν "to tighten, to increase".
  • ἕως οὗ πρὸς τὴν ἀλέαν ἀντέχειν οὐκέτι ἠδύνατο: ἕως οὗ, a variant "until"; πρὸς + accusative "to hold out against something" (the "direction" job from Lesson 4); ἠδύνατο imperfect of δύναμαι (Lesson 6) + οὐκέτι "no longer" (Lesson 11): "he could hold out no more".
  • ὁ λόγος δηλοῖ ὅτι πολλάκις τὸ πείθειν τοῦ βιάζεσθαι ἀνυτικώτερόν ἐστι: Aesop's standard "moral" pattern: ὁ λόγος + δηλόω "to show" + the ὅτι clause (report clause, Lesson 17); two opposed substantivized infinitives: τὸ πείθειν "persuading" / τὸ βιάζεσθαι "forcing" (βιάζομαι deponent, learn by sight); ἀνυτικώτερόν ἐστι – a learn-by-sight comparative adjective: ἀνυτικώτερος "more effective": the 09b payoff sits in the genitive of comparison τοῦ βιάζεσθαι – "persuading (is more effective than) forcing". A twelve-word sentence that packs the whole lesson: substantivization – comparative – genitive of comparison.

General grammar point to remember: this fable is a "comparison playground": comparative adjectives (σφοδρότερον, περιττοτέραν, ἀνυτικώτερον), comparative adverbs (μᾶλλον, ἔτι μᾶλλον), and a closing line built on the genitive of comparison over two substantivized infinitives. Add the dense participle cover (six participles in a nine-line story): if you have not yet passed Lessons 19–20, the advice is to read it twice – first pass, "swallow" each participle as a glossed block with its meaning; second pass, come back and peel every form.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1a, Formal observation. Five comparison forms:

  • C3 μᾶλλον ἐπέκειτο – a comparative adverb (μᾶλλον, section 5) standing beside the verb.
  • C4 ἔτι μᾶλλον – the adverb pair "still more", the heightening pattern learned in section 5.
  • C4 περιττοτέραν ἐσθῆτα – a Type I comparative adjective, feminine accusative, attributive in front of the noun "garment".
  • C5 σφοδρότερον – a comparative adverb (the neuter singular of the adjective), modifying τὸ καῦμα "the heat".
  • C6 ἀνυτικώτερόν ἐστι – a comparative adjective in predication; its subject is the substantivized infinitive τὸ πείθειν. The word is glossed "learn its face": ἀνυτικώτερος "more effective".

There is no superlative anywhere in the story: Aesop weighs one action against another, crowning nobody.

Step 1b, The two participle blocks. Both are genitive singular masculine present participles hanging on τοῦ δὲ ἀνθρώπου (article + δέ + noun): only the verb meaning differs – "holding on" (resisting) and "taking off" (shedding layer after layer). Both depict a scene in progress: the wind presses while the man grips, the Sun tightens while the man undresses. The story is told in two stacked layers of action, in exactly the "one side acts – the other answers" rhythm, so the reader watches the contest change direction with every reaction of the wayfarer.

Step 2, Matching the titles.

  • (a) "The contest and its rule" → C1–C2. Keywords: ἤριζον "they were contending", ὃς ἂν … ἀποδύσῃ "whichever man strips (the cloak)".
  • (b) "The North Wind's opening move" → C3–C4. Keywords: ἀρξάμενος "charging in first", μᾶλλον ἐπέκειτο, ἀποκαμὼν "exhausted".
  • (c) "Sun up, clothes off" → C5. Keywords: μετρίως προσέλαμψε "shone mildly", σφοδρότερον τὸ καῦμα ἐπέτεινεν "tightened the heat more fiercely".
  • (d) "The moral of the story" → C6. Keywords: ὁ λόγος δηλοῖ ὅτι, ἀνυτικώτερόν ἐστι.

On the closing sentence: τοῦ βιάζεσθαι is the genitive of comparison (section 6): the two "opponents" of the comparison are the two substantivized infinitives (τὸ πείθειν / τὸ βιάζεσθαι); putting "forcing" into the genitive is exactly how the sentence says "persuading is more effective than forcing". Using ἤ here (τὸ πείθειν ἢ τὸ βιάζεσθαι…) would be slow and heavy; the genitive is compact and fits the argumentative style.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. The "soft beats hard" principle is easy to accept in advertising, education, debate: the goal of these fields is to make people change of their own accord, and force never produces that. Flip it: law, emergencies, safety sometimes must go by force; the quality of the fable is that it is right only when the desired outcome is voluntary consent (putting on a coat), not when compulsion is safety. A question worth debating: the story quietly swaps the standard "strong" (δυνάμεως, C1) for "effective on a human being" (getting the coat off) – and the closing verdict judges the contest by that new standard, which neither side ever agreed to.

Exercise 1 – Forming the comparative and superlative (Type I)

Bring each adjective below through all three degrees; state clearly why you choose -ο- or -ω- when applying secondary rule 1:

  1. δίκαιος, -α, -ον (just)
  2. σοφός, -ή, -όν (wise)
  3. ἄδικος, ἄδικον (unjust – a two-ending adjective!)
  4. ἀληθής, ἀληθές (true, an s-stem)
  5. φίλος, -η, -ον (dear)
  6. μακρός, -ή, -όν (long)

Answers 1

  1. δικαιότερος / δικαιότατος
  2. σοφώτερος / σοφώτατος
  3. ἀδικώτερος / ἀδικώτατος
  4. ἀληθέστερος / ἀληθέστατος
  5. φίλτερος / φίλτατος
  6. μακρότερος / μακρότατος

Notes: (1) δίκαιος: the penultimate -και- is long → keep -ο-. (2) σοφός: the penultimate -σο- is short → -ω- (secondary rule 1). (3) ἄδικος is two-ending → its comparison is three-ending, and the penultimate is short → ἀδικώ- (secondary rules 2 and 1 at the same time). (4) ἀληθής with the stem ἀληθε-, a long-stemmed adjective: ἀληθέ-στερος – built on the bare stem (the Lesson 9 rule), accent on the connecting -ε-. (5) φίλος is the table's only exception: the stem φιλο- loses -ο- before the ending → φίλ-τερος, φίλ-τατος. (6) μακρός: the form traditional in the literature is μακρότερος, keeping -ο- despite the short penultimate; the accent already sits on -ό- exactly as the rule requires – learn it by sight, do not derive it.

Exercise 2 – Matching the irregular pairs

Match each positive with its correct pair (each pair used once): ἀμείνων/ἄριστος · χείρων/χείριστος · μείζων/μέγιστος · πλείων/πλείστος · ἐλάττων/ἐλάχιστος · θάσσων/τάχιστος · ἡδίων/ἥδιστος; then say which pairs have no standing positive: βελτίων/βέλτιστος · κρείττων/κράτιστος – these two pairs match with "(no positive)".

Answers 2

  1. ἀγαθός → ἀμείνων → ἄριστος
  2. κακός → χείρων → χείριστος
  3. μέγας → μείζων → μέγιστος
  4. πολύς → πλείων → πλείστος
  5. ὀλίγος → ἐλάττων → ἐλάχιστος
  6. ταχύς → θάσσων → τάχιστος
  7. ἡδύς → ἡδίων → ἥδιστος
  8. (no positive) → βελτίων → βέλτιστος
  9. (no positive) → κρείττων → κράτιστος

Notes: the two "orphan" pairs – βελτίων and κρείττων have no positive to hunt for; when translating "better/superior" back into Greek in argumentative prose, Greek calls for exactly these two pairs.

Exercise 3 – Choosing the door: ἤ or the genitive of comparison?

Translate each sentence into Greek; note down why you chose that door (a pronoun → the genitive; a clause or noun → either door works, but the cases must match):

  1. "The teacher is wiser than I." (a 1st-person pronoun)
  2. "This orator is more persuasive than that orator." (two nouns compared, concrete partners set side by side)
  3. "The river flows all year round, by day and by night alike." – where "by day and by night alike" is a genitive of time (Lesson 11): write the Greek phrase for "by day and by night alike".

Answers 3

  1. ὁ διδάσκαλος σοφώτερός μου ἐστιν. (= σοφώτερος ἤ ἐγώ)
  2. οὗτος ὁ ῥήτωρ πιθανώτερος ἢ ἐκεῖνος ἐστίν. (= πιθανώτερος ἐκείνου)
  3. καὶ ἡμέρας καὶ νυκτός

Notes: (1) the partner is the personal pronoun ἐγώ → the genitive door μοῦ/ἐμοῦ is the canonical route of formal prose. (2) two clearly contrasted nouns → both doors work, but the ἤ door with the same case (ἢ ἐκεῖνος, nominative like οὗτος) sounds like a bolder "subtraction"; the genitive door is more discreet. (3) the time phrase exactly as in Bielefeld: καὶ ἡμέρας καὶ νυκτός – the pair καὶ…καὶ + two genitives of time, "by day and by night alike".

Exercise 4 – Naming the genitive in the original text

For each original sentence below (all taken from the two readings), give the correct name of the genitive: (a) possessive; (b) partitive; (c) comparison; (d) time:

  1. τούτου δὲ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἐγὼ σοφώτερός εἰμι
  2. ἦλθον ἐπί τινα τῶν δοκούντων σοφῶν εἶναι
  3. ἀνεῖλεν δὲ ἡ Πυθία μηδένα σοφώτερον εἶναι… τοῦ θεοῦ δὲ οὐ θέμις ψεύδεσθαι (see the phrase after the "…")
  4. ποταμοῦ παραρρέοντος (the full original of Aesop 18)

Answers 4

  1. (c) genitive of comparison: "wiser than this man" – after the comparative.
  2. (b) partitive genitive: "one of those considered wise" – the numeral "one of" + group.
  3. (a) possessive genitive: "it is not lawful for the god (to lie)" – a relation of belonging.
  4. (d) none of the four above – see the note.

Notes on sentence (4): ποταμοῦ παραρρέοντος is the absolute genitive (Lesson 20): "a river flowing past (there)" – the wayfarer's swimming spot in the full original; in our condensed version this phrase has been cut. It is not among the four options (a)–(d) of the question above – the question's purpose was for you to discover: a "participle + genitive standing on its own" is the fifth door of the section 8 map; do not force it into comparison or time.

Exercise 5 – Comparative adverbs

Form the comparative and superlative adverbs of: σοφός, ἀληθής, ταχύς; then translate the three phrases below (one adverb each):

  1. ἀληθέστερον λέγειν
  2. θᾶττον τρέχειν
  3. τάχιστα ἀπιέναι

Answers 5

  1. σοφώτερον / σοφότατα
  2. ἀληθέστερον / ἀληθέστατα
  3. θᾶττον / τάχιστα

Translations: (1) "to speak more truly" (to say what is truer); (2) "to run faster"; (3) "to go as fast as possible / go at once". Notes: all three are neuter (comparative: singular; superlative: plural) – the "one ending, two lives" principle of section 5; τάχιστα ἀπιέναι is the elative type: in ancient letters this is the command "go at once!", not a ranking of who goes fastest.

Exercise 6 – Translating into Greek

Use exactly the tools of 09b (comparative, superlative, genitive of comparison or partitive, elative):

  1. "No one is wiser than the teacher." (use οὐδείς + the genitive door)
  2. "The Sun is mightier than the North Wind." (use the irregular "mightier/superior" pair)
  3. "Persuasion is more effective than force." (translate close to Aesop's closing line: two substantivized infinitives + ἀνυτικώτερος)
  4. "The teacher is the wisest of all the citizens." (superlative + partitive genitive)

Answers 6

  1. οὐδεὶς σοφώτερος τοῦ διδασκάλου ἐστίν.
  2. ὁ Ἥλιος κρείττων τοῦ Βορέα ἐστίν.
  3. τὸ πείθειν τοῦ βιάζεσθαι ἀνυτικώτερόν ἐστιν.
  4. ὁ διδάσκαλος σοφώτατος τῶν πολιτῶν ἐστίν.

Notes: (1) also possible: οὐδεὶς σοφώτερος ἢ ὁ διδάσκαλος – but with a simple noun, argumentative prose prefers the genitive; note that τοῦ διδασκάλου "of the teacher" is only formally a possessive shape, its job here is comparison. (2) κρείττων must agree in gender and number with its masculine singular subject; the genitive τοῦ Βορέα follows the proper-name ending already met in the vocabulary table. (3) the structure copies the original line exactly: the subject is a neuter substantivized infinitive, so the predicate is neuter too, ἀνυτικώτερόν. (4) after the superlative, the group as a plural partitive genitive τῶν πολιτῶν – or extended: σοφώτατος πάντων τῶν πολιτῶν "wisest of all the citizens".
