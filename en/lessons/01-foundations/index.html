1. Greek "delegates the work" to word endings

Look at these two English sentences:

English
The god worships the human.
The human worships the god.

English depends on word order: swap god and human and you swap who worships whom. The order subject → verb → object (SVO) is the backbone of the English sentence.

Ancient Greek works differently: it delegates the work to word endings (inflection). Who is the subject, who is the object — all of that is written into the ending of the noun, not its position. Word order is therefore free, and order has only one remaining job: emphasis.

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ θεὸς θεραπεύει τὸν ἄνθρωπον.ho theòs therapeúei tòn ánthrōpon.The god worships/cares for the human.
τὸν ἄνθρωπον θεραπεύει ὁ θεός.tòn ánthrōpon therapeúei ho theós.The god worships the human. (position changed, meaning unchanged)
ὁ ἄνθρωπος θεραπεύει τὸν θεόν.ho ánthrōpos therapeúei tòn theón.The human worships the god.

In sentence 2, τὸν ἄνθρωπον stands at the front of the sentence but is still the object, because the ending -ον (with the article τὸν) has already "stamped" its function. Pushing it to the front only adds emphasis: "the human — him the god cares for."

Pattern to reuse: To find out what a Greek word is doing in the sentence, don't look at position — look at the ending. Word order only answers "what does the author emphasize?", while endings answer "who does what to whom?". This rule holds for all 48 lessons of this course.

Latin corner: Latin behaves exactly like Greek: deus hominem colit = hominem deus colit ("the god worships the human"; changing the order changes nothing), because -um (hominem) has already marked the object. Romans found Greek easy to learn, and vice versa — the two inflectional systems use almost the same "keyboard".

2. Word classes: two big groups

Before we start inflecting, you must know what kind of word you are holding. Every Greek word class falls into one of two groups:

GroupCharacteristicWord classesExamples in this lesson
Inflectable (endings change with function)Declines by case/gender/number (nominal classes) or by person/number (verbs)noun, proper name, adjective, pronoun, numeral, article, verbὁ ἄνθρωπος, ὁ θεός, θεραπεύει, εἶναι
Uninflected (a single fixed form)Never changes its endingadverb, preposition, conjunction, particleἐκεῖ, ἐν, καί, δέ, γάρ

Three points to engrave immediately:

  • The article is a word class of its own, not a "scrap" attached to the noun: it is fully inflected, changing for the case + gender + number of the noun that follows. That makes the article the most informative "signpost" in the sentence — we will exploit it constantly.
  • Proper names decline just like nouns (ὁ Ζεύς, ὁ Ποσειδῶν in the reading).
  • The verb is the most heavily inflected word class: its ending encodes person, number, tense, mood, voice. This lesson needs only the 3rd person.

Latin corner: Have you heard amo, amas, amat… amant? Each ending is a person + number package. Greek: θεραπεύω – θεραπεύεις – θεραπεύει – θεραπεύουσι(ν) — same logic, different ending "color".

Conjugating verbs & declining nouns

"Inflecting" (systematically changing endings) happens on two fronts:

3.1 Conjugating verbs

A Greek verb answers 5 questions when it inflects:

CategoryQuestionAnswer in this lesson
PersonWho is speaking / spoken to / spoken about?Here: 3rd person (he/she/it, they)
NumberOne or many?singular / plural
TenseWhen? (present, past, future…)present (not yet covered)
MoodWhat attitude? (statement, command, wish…)indicative (not yet covered)
VoiceActive or passive?active (not yet covered)

Lesson 1 needs only 3 forms: θεραπεύει (therapeúei) "he/she worships", θεραπεύουσι(ν) (therapeúousi(n)) "they worship", θεραπεύειν (therapeúein) "to worship" (infinitive — section 9).

3.2 Declining nouns

A noun answers 3 questions when it inflects:

CategoryQuestionExample
CaseWhat does this word do in the sentence?5 cases — see section 4
NumberOne or many?ὁ θεός / οἱ θεοί
GenderMasculine, feminine, or neuter?masculine: ὁ θεός; feminine (not yet covered); neuter (not yet covered)

Watch out: Grammatical gender is a fixed property of each noun (like an ID number), it does not change with case, and it usually has nothing to do with real-world sex. ὁ οὐρανός (the sky) is masculine, ἡ θάλασσα (the sea) is feminine — there is nothing "masculine" about the sky. Learn the gender together with the meaning, as a single unit.

The article always agrees (the KNG principle: Kasus–Numerus–Genus, i.e. case–number–gender): the article carries exactly the case + number + gender of the noun that follows. So τῷ θεῷ is a match (both dative, masculine, singular), while τῷ θεόν is an error.

Pattern to reuse: You don't need to know a noun to read the sentence — just read the article. The article gives you case + gender + number; the verb ending gives you person + number. Put them together and you have the frame of the sentence. This is the reading strategy for every Greek text, from lesson 1 to Plato.

4. Sentence parts & the five cases

A Greek sentence is built from familiar components:

PartJobEnglish example
SubjectWho performs the action"The human worships…"
Predicate (verb)Action / state"The human worships…"
ObjectReceiver of the action"…worships the gods."
AdverbialCircumstance: where, when, by what means"…worships… in the house."
AttributeDecorates/qualifies a noun"the house of the god"

English marks these roles with word order and with "of/to/at". Greek marks them with case: each case is a "functional passport" attached to the end of the noun. There are 5 cases:

CaseTest questionMain functionEnglish example
Nominative (Nom.)who / what? (as subject)naming, subject; after εἶναι"the god is king" (king = Nom.)
Genitive (Gen.)of whom / of what?belonging to, originating from"the house of the god"
Dative (Dat.)to whom / where / with what?direction toward, location, instrument"gives to the reader", "lives in the house", "writes with a pen"
Accusative (Acc.)sees / meets / worships whom?goal of the action, extent"reads the book", "enters the house"
Vocative (Voc.)addressing whom?direct address"O Zeus!" (lesson 2)

Latin corner: Latin has 6 cases: the 5 above plus the ablative ("from where / by what means"). Greek has no ablative: its work is divided between the dative (by what means, where) and the accusative (motion toward). When you meet a dative doing "ablative work" — don't be surprised.

5. The masculine article & o-declension nouns

The paradigm of the masculine article — the first paradigm you must memorize in your Greek career:

SingularTransliterationPluralTransliteration
Nominativeὁhoοἱhoi
Genitiveτοῦtoûτῶνtôn
Dativeτῷtōiτοῖςtoîs
Accusativeτὸνtònτοὺςtoùs

And the masculine o-declension noun (ending -ος) follows the article exactly — model word ἄνθρωπος (ánthrōpos) "human being". Look at the two tables side by side: the noun endings and the article endings move in step.

SingularTransliterationPluralTransliteration
Nominativeὁ ἄνθρωποςho ánthrōposοἱ ἄνθρωποιhoi ánthrōpoi
Genitiveτοῦ ἀνθρώπουtoû anthrṓpouτῶν ἀνθρώπωνtôn anthrṓpōn
Dativeτῷ ἀνθρώπῳtōi anthrṓpōiτοῖς ἀνθρώποιςtoîs anthrṓpois
Accusativeτὸν ἄνθρωπονtòn ánthrōponτοὺς ἀνθρώπουςtoùs anthrṓpous

How to split the word: ἄνθρωπος = stem ἀνθρώπο- + case-number ending. Declining a noun = keep the stem, swap the ending. Every masculine o-declension noun works exactly like this, so ὁ θεός, τοῦ θεοῦ, τῷ θεῷ, τὸν θεόν… you already "know" them without studying each word.

Pattern to reuse (golden rule): Ending -ος → -ου → -ῳ → -ον (singular) and -οι → -ων → -οις → -ους (plural). Memorize the article first; the noun endings will "follow along" by themselves. Self-test: write out the 4 cases of δῆμος (dêmos, community): ὁ δῆμος, τοῦ δήμου, τῷ δήμῳ, τὸν δῆμον / οἱ δῆμοι, τῶν δήμων, τοῖς δήμοις, τοὺς δήμους — if you can do that with a new word, the pattern is yours.

Latin corner: The Greek o-declension ≈ Latin nouns in -us: amīcus has amīcī, amīcō, amīcum (genitive, dative, accusative) — almost a "comparative dictionary": -ος ↔ -us, -ου ↔ -ī, -ῳ ↔ -ō, -ον ↔ -um. Anyone who has studied Latin will recognize Greek like an old acquaintance.

Watch out: (1) The article is not obligatory for every noun: θεραπεύει θεούς "worships gods" — no article = general/unspecified reference, or an unspecified quantity. (2) The accent marks (acute, grave, circumflex) and the breathing marks (rough/smooth) on the article ὁ/οἱ must be preserved when writing by hand — dropping them is a spelling error. (3) Don't confuse τῷ (dative) with τοῦ (genitive): the two look "similar" but carry different passports — see section 15.

6. Third-person verbs & the infinitive

"Thematic" verbs (like θεραπεύ-ω) in the present active, 3rd person:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
θεραπεύειtherapeúeihe/she/it worships (tends, cares for)
θεραπεύουσι(ν)therapeúousi(n)they worship
θεραπεύεινtherapeúeinto worship — infinitive

The most important Greek verb — εἶναι (eînai) "to be / to have" — is slightly irregular:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ἐστί(ν)estí(n)he/she/it is
εἰσί(ν)eisí(n)they are
εἶναιeînaito be

Why the (ν) in brackets? That is the movable nu: the endings -σι and -ει add a ν when the next word begins with a vowel — to avoid two vowels "colliding" and becoming hard to tell apart (this is called hiatus avoidance). It is purely a matter of pronunciation, not meaning: θεραπεύουσι τοὺς θεούς = θεραπεύουσιν τοὺς θεούς.

Latin corner: The Latin 3rd-person endings are -t / -nt (amat / amant) — Greek corresponds with -ει / -ουσι(ν); the Latin infinitive ends in -re (amare) ↔ Greek -ειν (therapeúein). They sound very different, but the "keyboard" is the same.

Watch out: Don't mix up θεραπεύει (he worships — finite verb) with θεραπεύειν (to worship — infinitive): they differ by one letter ν and by the accent position. Self-test: if the word follows δεῖ or another verb, it is probably an infinitive; if it is the "axis" of the sentence, it is a finite verb.

7. Translating a sentence: hidden subjects & the four-step analysis

Greek is a "subject-dropping" language: the ending -ει already contains the meaning "he/she", so the sentence needs no separate pronoun:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
θεραπεύει τοὺς θεούς.therapeúei toùs theoús.He worships the gods.
ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ ἐστίν.en tōi oíkōi estín.He is in the house.

This is called a hidden subject: not written out, but spoken by the verb ending. English translation must supply it ("he…") — while the Greek original has no corresponding word at all.

When you meet a new sentence, follow the four-step analysis:

  1. Find the (finite) verb: usually with the ending -ει / -ουσι(ν), or ἐστί(ν) / εἰσί(ν). The ending tells you person + number.
  2. Read the articles: each unit τὸν/τῷ/τοῦ/ὁ/οἱ/τῶν/τοῖς/τοὺς tells you the case + gender + number of the following noun — even without knowing the noun's meaning.
  3. Assign functions: nominative → subject; accusative → object; dative with a preposition → adverbial of place/instrument; genitive → "of…".
  4. Translate into an SVO frame (subject–verb–object), then polish until it reads naturally.

Apply this to ὁ ἄνθρωπος θεραπεύει τοὺς θεούς: (1) verb θεραπεύει → 3rd person singular; (2) ὁ ἄνθρωπος = nominative masculine singular → subject; τοὺς θεούς = accusative plural → object; (4) "The human worships the gods." Done — and you could build the sentence frame without knowing either noun beforehand.

Watch out: Because word order is free, do not translate "first word first". The sentence τοὺς θεοὺς θεραπεύει ὁ ἄνθρωπος does NOT mean "The gods worship the human" — the article τοὺς has already ruled: the gods are the object. Always consult the endings before the order.

8. εἶναι: one verb, three jobs

εἶναι appears in three constructions, all of which occur right in the reading:

8.1 Copula + predicative noun

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ Ζεὺς θεός ἐστίν.ho Zeùs theós estín.Zeus is a god.
ὁ ἵππος ζῷον ἐστίν. (not yet covered: ζῷον)ho híppos zôion estín.The horse is an animal.

Greek says "A is B" with two nominatives: subject + ἐστί(ν) + predicative noun. The predicative noun always agrees with the subject (same case, same number) — if you see two nominatives in a sentence, one of them is almost certainly predicative. Often ἐστίν slips in between the two words.

8.2 With a preposition: εἶναι + dative

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ θεὸς ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ ἐστίν.ho theòs en tōi ouranōi estín.The god is in the heaven.
ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ εἰσίν.en tōi oíkōi eisín.They are in the house.

ἐν "in" always takes the dative — so the phrase ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ plays the role of an adverbial of place.

8.3 Dative of possession: "someone has"

GreekTransliterationEnglish
Τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ ὁ οἶκος ἐστίν.Tōi anthrṓpōi ho óikos estín.The man has the house. (literally: to-the-man is the house)
Τῷ θεῷ ἵπποι εἰσίν.Tōi theôi híppoi eisín.The god has horses.

This construction is called the dative of possession: dative = the possessor, nominative = the thing possessed. Greek has no everyday verb "to have" — instead it says "toward person X, the Y exists". In English translation, simply convert it to "X has Y".

Latin corner: Latin has exactly the same construction: mihi liber est = "I have a book" (literally: to me, a book exists) — the Latin dative mihi matches the Greek dative exactly. Learning one language deepens your understanding of the other.

Watch out: Do not turn the dative of possession into a genitive: Τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ ὁ οἶκος ἐστίν ✓, but τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ✗ here. Self-test: the possessor (dative) is usually a person/living being; the thing possessed is always in the nominative. See dative + εἶναι + nominative together — think "possession construction" immediately.

9. What the infinitive does in a sentence

The infinitive is a verb "not yet attached to a person" — the name of an action. In this lesson it enters one fixed pattern:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
δεῖ τὸν θεὸν θεραπεύειν.deî tòn theòn therapeúein.One must worship the god.
δεῖ τοὺς ἀνθρώπους θεραπεύειν.deî toùs anthrṓpous therapeúein.People must worship.

δεῖ (deî) = "it is necessary" — a special verb: it has only one single form, no personal endings, and it takes an infinitive. The accusative before the infinitive (τὸν θεόν, τοὺς ἀνθρώπους) is the logical subject of the infinitive's action: "the god must be worshipped" → rearranged: "one must worship the god".

Pattern to reuse: δεῖ + [accusative] + [infinitive] = "[someone] must [do something]". When you meet δεῖ in a text, it is this formula 95% of the time. (Later we will expand the infinitive into a bigger construction — the accusative-with-infinitive, AcI, in lesson 4.)

10. Particles: the "connectors" of the sentence

Greek uses particles very densely — small uninflected words that steer the flow of the text. This lesson needs 4:

ParticleTransliterationMeaningNote
καίkaíand; also, evencan stand first in the sentence
δέdébut, and (mild contrast)never sentence-initial — always slips in after the first word
γάρgárfor, because (explanatory)never sentence-initial
μένménon the one hand… (setting up a contrast)almost always paired with δέ

δέ / γάρ / μέν are postpositive: they never open a sentence, always standing in "second position". So when reading, don't detach them as the "first word" of a new clause:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ μὲν ἵππος ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ ἐστίν, ὁ δὲ ἄνθρωπος ἐκεῖ.ho mèn híppos en tōi oíkōi estín, ho dè ánthrōpos ekeî.The horse is in the house, but the man is there.
θεραπεύει τοὺς θεούς, ὁ γὰρ θεὸς ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ ἐστίν.therapeúei toùs theoús, ho gàr theòs en tōi ouranōi estín.He worships the gods, for the god is in the heaven.

Latin corner: Latin has two particles that behave exactly the same way: autem (≈ δέ) and enim (≈ γάρ) — neither can open a sentence, both take second position. Even the Greek translators of the Bible into Latin (the Vulgate) kept this habit exactly.

Pattern to reuse: μέν … δέ is a "two-clause clamp": one clause with μέν, the other with δέ. In translation: "on the one hand… but…". If you see a lone μέν, wait for the δέ — it is almost certainly coming.

11. Attributive position

An attribute (e.g. the phrase "of the god") stands in one of two positions, both of which lie inside the "article zone":

GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἱ τοῦ θεοῦ ἵπποιhoi toû theoû híppoithe horses of the god
οἱ ἵπποι οἱ τοῦ θεοῦhoi híppoi hoi toû theoûthe horses of the god (same meaning, different order)

Same meaning — the second article is the identifying sign: "this phrase is an attribute, it belongs to the noun in front of it". Compare with the sentence that has only one article:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
οἱ ἵπποι τοῦ θεοῦ εἰσίν.hoi híppoi toû theoû eisín.The horses belong to the god. (not: "the god's horses are…")

Here τοῦ θεοῦ is not inside the article zone of ἵπποι — it is its own predicative statement: "they are of the god". Two articles = attribute; one article = predicative statement. The same two words, different position, different meaning — clear proof that "ending + position" work together.

The construction also works with prepositional phrases as attributes (you will meet one in exercise 7): ὁ ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ θεός (ho en tōi oíkōi theós) = ὁ θεὸς ὁ ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ "the god [who is] in the house". How to recognize it: the article stands before the prepositional phrase, and the noun comes after — the phrase is "embraced" into the attribute zone; in the second variant, the article is repeated after the main noun.

Watch out: Don't mechanically translate ὁ ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ θεός as "the god is in the house" and move on — that is not a predicate! Self-test: look at the article's position. If a prepositional phrase follows immediately after the article, before the main noun (ὁ [ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ] θεός) → the phrase is an attribute → read it as one block: "the god [who is in the house]". Compare the predicative sentence: ὁ θεὸς ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ "the god [is] in the house" — the prepositional phrase follows after the noun, not "embraced" by the article.

12. Adverbs & prepositions: an opening journey

Adverbs are uninflected and answer "where / how": ἐκεῖ (ekeî) "there". Later we will learn how to build adverbs from adjectives (lessons 3 and 9).

13. Reading: Three brother gods

Source: the theme is based on Homer, Ilias 15,187–193 — the passage where Zeus recounts how the three brothers Zeus–Poseidon–Hades drew lots to divide three realms. The original (public domain) can be read on Perseus. The version below was rewritten by this project for lesson-1 level — it uses only structures already covered, keeping the spirit of the original.

New words in the reading:

ὁ ἀδελφός, -οῦho adelphós, -oûthe brothermasculine, o-declension — declines exactly like ἄνθρωπος
τρεῖςtreîsthreenumeral, uninflected in this lesson
ἡ θάλασσα, -ηςhē thálassa, -ēsthe seafeminine, a-declension (not yet covered) — the case shows through the article

Simplified version (composed by this project):

Τρεῖς ἀδελφοὶ εἰσίν· ὁ Ζεύς, ὁ Ποσειδῶν, ὁ Ἅιδης. Τῷ μὲν Διὶ ὁ οὐρανός ἐστίν, τῷ δὲ Ποσειδῶνι ἡ θάλασσα, τῷ δὲ Ἅιδῃ ἡ γῆ. Οἱ δὲ ἄνθρωποι τοὺς θεοὺς θεραπεύουσιν.

Treîs ádelphoi eisín· ho Zeús, ho Poseidôn, ho Háidēs. Tōi mèn Diì ho ouranós estín, tōi dè Poseidôni hē thálassa, tōi dè Háidēi hē gê. Hoi dè ánthrōpoi toùs theoùs therapeúousin.

Translation:

There are three brothers: Zeus, Poseidon, Hades. To Zeus belongs the heaven, to Poseidon the sea, to Hades the earth. And the humans worship the gods.

Quick analysis (checking with the tools you have learned):

  • Τρεῖς ἀδελφοὶ εἰσίν — nominative plural + εἰσί(ν): the "there are…" construction. The article is absent → general reference: "there are three brothers".
  • Τῷ μὲν Διὶ ὁ οὐρανός ἐστίν — dative of possession! The possessor (τῷ Διί, dative of Zeus) + the thing possessed (ὁ οὐρανός, nominative) + ἐστίν. The article τῷ reports "dative" even though you haven't yet learned the declension of the name Διί — exactly the "read the article" strategy.
  • μέν … δέ … δέ — particles clamping three clauses: "on the one hand… while… while…". Clauses 2 and 3 omit ἐστίν (understood from clause 1).
  • Οἱ δὲ ἄνθρωποι θεραπεύουσιν — δέ is postpositive, standing after the article οἱ; θεραπεύουσιν carries the movable nu because the next word begins with a vowel.

Reading comprehension questions: (1) Which particle pair "clamps" the three clauses together? (2) In which case do the three divine names stand — what is the evidence? (3) Read the original Homer (Ilias 15.187–193 on Perseus): which realm is Hades actually assigned — and how has the simplified version "compressed" it?

Answers to the reading questions (print to check)

1. The three contrasting clauses use μέν…δέ…δέ. 2. τῷ Διί / τῷ Ποσειδῶνι / τῷ Ἅιδῃ — all datives, recognized by the article τῷ. 3. In Homer, Hades is assigned the realm "under the dark earth" (not the habitable surface) — the simplified version compresses this into ἡ γῆ "the earth"; read the original at Perseus, Ilias 15.187–193, to see the difference.

16. Exercises

Answers appear only when printing (Ctrl+P → Save as PDF) — finish first, then print to check.

Exercise 1 — Word classes & sentence parts

Analyze the English sentence: "The man worships the gods in the house." List: (a) the word class of each word, (b) the sentence parts (subject, predicate, object, adverbial).

Answer 1

(a) man = noun; worships = verb; gods = noun (with the article "the"); house = noun; in = preposition. (b) Subject = "the man"; predicate = "worships"; object = "the gods"; adverbial of place = "in the house". In Greek, the same content would need: an article before each noun, and endings marking case (accusative for "the gods", dative for "in the house" after ἐν).

Exercise 2 — Reading the article like a road sign

Looking only at the article (and the ending), determine the case + number of each unit: (a) τῷ οἴκῳ (b) τοὺς θεούς (c) τῶν ἀνθρώπων (d) ὁ οὐρανός (e) τοῖς ἵπποις.

Answer 2

(a) dative, singular (τῷ, -ῳ); (b) accusative, plural (τοὺς, -ους); (c) genitive, plural (τῶν, -ων); (d) nominative, singular (ὁ, -ος); (e) dative, plural (τοῖς, -οις).

Exercise 3 — Declining a noun

Decline ὁ ἵππος (the horse) through all 4 cases × 2 numbers (8 cells), with the article.

Answer 3

ὁ ἵππος, τοῦ ἵππου, τῷ ἵππῳ, τὸν ἵππον — οἱ ἵπποι, τῶν ἵππων, τοῖς ἵπποις, τοὺς ἵππους.

ho híppos, toû híppou, tōi híppōi, tòn híppon — hoi híppoi, tôn híppōn, toîs híppois, toùs híppous.

Exercise 4 — Translating with the four steps

Translate, writing out each step (verb → article → function → translation): (a) ὁ θεὸς τοὺς ἀνθρώπους θεραπεύει. (b) θεραπεύουσι τοὺς θεούς. (c) Τῷ θεῷ ὁ οὐρανός ἐστίν.

Answer 4

(a) verb θεραπεύει (3rd person singular); ὁ θεός nominative → subject; τοὺς ἀνθρώπους accusative plural → object. "The god cares for the humans."

(b) verb θεραπεύουσι (3rd person plural) — no nominative around → hidden subject "they"; τοὺς θεούς accusative plural → object. "They worship the gods."

(c) ἐστίν + dative τῷ θεῷ + nominative ὁ οὐρανός → dative of possession. "The god has the heaven." (literally: toward the god, the heaven exists)

Exercise 5 — The three jobs of εἶναι

Choose the right construction (predicative noun / dative after a preposition / dative of possession) and translate: (a) ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ ἐστίν. (b) ὁ Ποσειδῶν θεός ἐστίν. (c) Τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ ἵππος ἐστίν. — what is special about the article in sentence (c)?

Answer 5

(a) εἶναι + ἐν + dative (adverbial of place): "The man is in the house." (b) two nominatives → predicative noun: "Poseidon is a god." (c) dative of possession: "The man has (a) horse." — the article is missing before ἵππος → general/indefinite: "some horse".

Exercise 6 — Attributive position

(a) Write "the horses of the god" in both attributive orders. (b) Translate οἱ ἵπποι τοῦ θεοῦ εἰσίν. and explain how it differs from (a).

Answer 6

(a) οἱ τοῦ θεοῦ ἵπποι = οἱ ἵπποι οἱ τοῦ θεοῦ — both variants have two articles → attribute; sentence (b) has only one article → τοῦ θεοῦ is a separate predicative statement: "The horses belong to the god." In short: two articles = "the god's horses"; one article = "the horses are the god's".

Exercise 7 — Putting it all together (translate the passage)

Translate the following passage, using every tool you have learned (sentences with harder words have hints in the answer):

δεῖ τοὺς ἀνθρώπους θεραπεύειν τοὺς θεούς· ὁ γὰρ ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ θεὸς τοὺς ἀνθρώπους θεραπεύει. τοὺς δὲ ἵππους οἱ ἄνθρωποι θεραπεύουσιν.

Answer 7

"Humans must worship the gods; for the god [who is in the heaven] cares for humans. And the horses — the humans care for them."

Notes on each construction: (1) δεῖ + τοὺς ἀνθρώπους + θεραπεύειν = the "must" pattern — the accusative τοὺς ἀνθρώπους is the logical subject of the infinitive; τοὺς θεούς is the object of the infinitive. (2) ὁ ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ θεός = attribute with a prepositional phrase: the article stands before the phrase, the main noun follows → "the god [who is in the heaven]"; γάρ is postpositive, introducing the reason. (3) τοὺς δὲ ἵππους = object pushed to the front for emphasis + postpositive δέ; the verb θεραπεύουσιν stands last, keeping the movable nu before the sentence break (both variants are valid: θεραπεύουσι); the subject οἱ ἄνθρωποι stands in between: "the horses — the humans care for them".
