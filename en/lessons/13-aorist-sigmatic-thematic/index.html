1. The aorist: the tense of the complete event

So far you have held two views of an action: "in progress" (present, imperfect) and "about to unfold" (future). Today we add the third view – and it is the backbone of every Greek narrative: the aorist – the tense that views an event as one complete block: it happened, it closed, and we do not cut into its inside to see "how it was unfolding".

TenseThe viewVisualExample with λύω "loosen"
Presentunfolding at the moment of speaking──●──▶ (now)λύω – I am loosening
Imperfect (Lesson 11)unfolding at a point in the past──▶●──▶ (back then)ἔλυον – (back then) I was loosening
Aorist – new todaythe event as a complete block in the past───▮─── (done)ἔλυσα – I loosened (one event)
Future (Lesson 12)the event still to come──▶▶●λύσω – I shall loosen

The two past tenses divide the work very clearly in narrative: the imperfect paints the background (scenery, unfolding developments, habits – Lesson 11), while the aorist narrates the main events, the events that "click the story forward beat by beat". If a story is a film, the imperfect is the camera panning around the room; the aorist is the "cuts" that carry the story on: he arrived, spoke, took, went – each verb one complete block.

The name: ἀόριστος (aóristos) means "not bounded": ἀ- "not" + ὁρίζω "to set a boundary" (its cognate noun is τὸ ὅρος "boundary, standard" – also the root of the English word horizon). The Alexandrian grammarians named it so because the aorist says nothing about the duration or the internal course of the event: it only says "the thing happened, viewed as a block". In the indicative the aorist stands in the past; but the "complete block" view also lives on in imperatives and subordinate clauses – you will meet these gradually in later lessons.

Latin corner: the role of "narrating a completed event" is played by the Latin perfect: amāvī "I loved (and the matter is closed)". Latin wraps two things into one tense: "the complete event" and "the result still standing today". Greek splits the two: the aorist for the complete event (today's lesson), while "the result still standing" is the perfect (Lesson 14). Practical lesson: when translating the aorist, it nearly always corresponds to the Latin perfect in narrative – Caesar wrote vēnī, vīdī, vīcī "I came, I saw, I won"; if Caesar had been a Greek, that sentence would be a chain of three aorists: ἦλθον, εἶδον, ἐνίκησα (the first two are thematic aorists, the last a sigmatic aorist of the contract verb νικάω "win" – today you will be able to build all three).

2. The sigmatic active aorist: ἔλυσα

The most common aorist is packaged with the letter σ – familiar from the future of Lesson 12: the sigmatic aorist, also called the first aorist. The formula, with the two σ brothers side by side:

Three details to engrave at once: (1) the augment is obligatory in the indicative – an aorist without ἐ- is not a narrative aorist (see the achtung below); (2) the accent recesses by the general rule: ἔλυσα, ἐλύσαμεν, ἔλυσαν – the accent anchors on the last eligible syllable (ἔ- because the word begins with the augment, which never carries the accent); (3) movable ν (nu) in 3 Sg. ἔλυσε(ν) – exactly like ἐπαίδευε(ν) of the imperfect: the ν appears when the next word begins with a vowel or at the end of a sentence.

Achtung – the augment is the border between two shores: the most dangerous pair of the whole σ system: λύσω (future, no augment) vs ἔλυσα (aorist, with augment). One ἐ- prefix turns "still to come" into "already happened". Quick self-check: see a σ → ask "where is the augment?": with ἐ- or a lengthened initial vowel → an aorist in the past; without → a future. Note the third shore: the augment does not belong to the aorist alone – the imperfect also has one (ἐπαίδευον); the σ is what tells them apart.

How to memorize the endings: the sigmatic aorist endings = the imperfect endings "wearing" an extra -σα-: -ον/-ες/-ε → -σα/-σας/-σε; -ομεν/-ετε/-ον → -σαμεν/-σατε/-σαν. If you know the imperfect table of Lesson 11, you already know the aorist: only the front of the ending changes.

3. The consonant + σ rules return: ἔγραψα, ἐδίωξα, ἔπεισα

When the block -σα- touches the final consonant of a stem, out comes exactly the ψ/ξ/σ rule set you learned in section 2 of Lesson 12 – nothing new to learn, just swap "future" for "aorist":

Note on augmentation: verbs beginning with a short vowel take the vocalic augment (temporal augment) as in Lesson 11: ἐλπίζω → ἤλπισα (ἐ → η), ἄγω → ἦξα "led" (ἀ with rough breathing + ἐ → ἦ – the same mechanism as ἀκούω → ἤκουον). Compound verbs slot the augment between prefix and stem, by the old rule: προσβαίνω? – to stay safe, remember the pieces already met: προέκρινεν (Lesson 11), and today you will peel one more, παρῆλθον, in Reading I.

Contract verbs: an aorist without contraction, just like the future

Achtung – the two σ "cousins" of contract verbs are identical to the letter: ποιήσω and ἐποίησα share the stem ποιησ- (the η is the trace of ε + σ). They differ in two points: the augment (only the aorist has it) and the accent – the future keeps the accent standing still on the long stem vowel throughout the paradigm (ποιήσω, ποιήσεις, ποιήσομεν – its own rule from Lesson 12), while the aorist accent recesses by the general rule: ἐποίησα, ἐποίησας, ἐποίησε(ν), ἐποιήσαμεν, ἐποιήσατε, ἐποίησαν. Lay the two paradigms side by side and read them aloud three times: your ear will pick up this accent pair for the rest of your reading life.

Latin corner: the σ of the Greek aorist and the -s- of the Latin perfect are, in many verbs, the very same relic of the common parent language: dīcō "say" → perfect dīxī (dīc- + s- → dīxī, with long ῑ); Greek: δεικνύω "show" → ἔδειξα (δεικ- + σα → ἔδειξα, the same ξ!). Two languages, one ancient snap of the fingers: insert -s- to close off the event as a block. That is why historical grammarians read these two endings as true brothers: dīxī and ἔδειξα carry the same inherited scar.

5. The thematic aorist: ἦλθον, ἔλαβον, ἐγενόμην

Besides the σ family, Greek has a second aorist family with a completely different packaging: the thematic aorist, also called the second aorist. The name "thematic" reminds you of the thematic vowel -ο-/-ε- from Lesson 6: these forms join stem and ending with the thematic vowel, imperfect-style. And the formula is strikingly simple:

augment + a new stem + imperfect endings

The key lies in the words "new stem": the verb switches to a different stem – usually shorter, older – and conjugates it with the secondary endings of the imperfect. Compare the sigmatic sibling: ἔλυσα takes the present stem λυ-; but ἦλθον abandons the present stem ἐρχ- for the stem ἐλθ-. The four most familiar pairs (plus one):

Three notes: (1) 1 Sg. = 3 Pl., just like the imperfect (ἦλθον is both "I came" and "they came" – context decides); (2) εὗρον and εἶπον have the long-vowel form of the augment at the front (εὑ- with rough breathing, εἰ-) – the same ε-lengthening phenomenon as Lesson 11; (3) ἐγενόμην is a thematic middle/passive aorist: the deponent γίγνομαι conjugates with the secondary m/p endings of Lesson 11: ἐγενόμην, ἐγένου, ἐγένετο, ἐγενόμεθα, ἐγένεσθε, ἐγένοντο – and you will read the exact form κατεθέμεθα "we deposited" in Reading II: the ending -εμεθα is in its slot, only the accent differs.

Achtung – the thematic aorist "wears the imperfect's coat": ἔλαβον and ἐλάμβανον differ by one syllable in front: the "complete block" view (ἔλαβον) vs the "in progress" view (ἐλάμβανον). In a story, the first narrates the event, the second paints the background. Self-check: strip off the augment and ask "is the remaining stem the present stem?": ἐλάμβαν- is precisely the present stem (the βαν fits λαμβάνω) → imperfect; ἐλαβ- is a different stem (check the pair table above) → thematic aorist. Dictionaries list the present–aorist pair ready-made, and the vocabulary table at the end of this lesson also lists pairs: learn the pair, never the single form.

Reading trick: meet a form with augment + imperfect endings and the familiar verb "does not come out" – odds are you are looking at a thematic aorist: try looking up the stripped stem (remove the augment, remove the ending): ἦλθ-, ἐλαβ-, εὑρ- – these three short stems are the "code names" of three extremely common verbs.

6. The aorist of liquid stems: ἔμεινα, ἔκρῑνα, ἀπεκρινάμην

Lesson 12 taught you the liquid future of verbs with stems in λ/μ/ν/ρ: μένω → μενῶ. This family has a matching "liquid" aorist – the liquid aorist, in essence a first aorist in which the σ drops out before the liquid consonant and the preceding vowel lengthens in compensation (compensatory lengthening, as in Lesson 12):

PresentLiquid future (Lesson 12)Liquid aorist (today)English
μένωμενῶἔμειναremain – remained
κρῑνωκρῑνῶἔκρῑναjudge – judged
στέλλωστελῶἔστειλαsend – sent

The endings attach exactly as in the sigmatic aorist set (-α, -ας, -ε(ν), -αμεν, -ατε, -αν): ἔμεινα, ἔμεινας, ἔμεινε(ν), ἐμείναμεν, ἐμείνατε, ἔμειναν. A familiar deponent also switches to its aorist on the liquid basis: ἀποκρίνομαι "answer" (Lesson 10) → aorist ἀπεκρινάμην "I answered": the augment slots between the prefix ἀπο- and the stem κρῑν-, then come the thematic m/p endings: ἀπεκρινάμην, ἀπεκρίνω, ἀπεκρίνατο, ἀπεκρινάμεθα, ἀπεκρίνασθε, ἀπεκρίναντο. Compare with the imperfect ἀπεκρινόμην (Lesson 10) to watch one and the same verb change endings as the tense changes.

Achtung – three pieces of one μένω: μένω (present) – μενῶ (liquid future, circumflex) – ἔμεινα (liquid aorist, augment + recessive accent). Same family, same σ-loss, but only the aorist has the augment; only the future has the circumflex standing at the end of the stem. Put the three forms in one row in your notebook: they are the "three ages" of the verb, and you will draw this same row for κρῑνω, στέλλω in the exercises.

7. The four-tense clock & the strategy of "peeling the aorist"

With all four indicative tenses in hand, you now hold the entire basic "clock" of the Greek verb. These four tenses are not just four points on a timeline, but two axes stacked on each other: time location (past / non-past / future) and aspect – the view of how the event unfolds:

"In progress" view (imperfective)"Complete block" view (perfective)
Non-pastPresent: λύω "I am loosening"– (the perfective in the past: the aorist)
PastImperfect: ἔλυον "(back then) I was loosening"Aorist: ἔλυσα "I loosened (one block)"
LaterFuture: λύσω "I shall loosen"(Lesson 14 adds the perfect – a third kind of view: "the result still standing")

When reading narrative, think of it this way: aorist = the beat of events, imperfect = the background scene, historic present (Lesson 12) = a camera close-up. A typical stretch of Xenophon opens with an imperfect setting the scene, then a chain of aorists carries the story: ἐπορεύετο "he was marching" → ἦλθεν "he arrived", εἶπεν "he spoke", ἔλαβεν "he took". Reading I today shows you both views interwoven in a single dream.

The "peel the aorist" strategy – four steps

  1. Find the augment: ἐ- at the front (or slotted inside a compound prefix), or a lengthened initial vowel: ἦ- (ἦλθον), εὑ- (εὗρον), εἰ- (εἶπον), ἠ- (ἤλπισα). No augment → not a past tense → try the future.
  2. Find the block -σα-: present → a sigmatic aorist (active or m/p according to the ending that follows it).
  3. Strip the secondary ending (-ον/-ες/-ε/-ομεν/-ετε/-ον or the m/p set): what remains is the stem – if the stem is different from the present stem → a thematic aorist; check the pair table or the dictionary.
  4. Liquid stem (λ/μ/ν/ρ) + aorist endings attached after a long vowel → a liquid aorist.

Achtung – the augment is not an aorist certificate: the imperfect also has one. The form προὔτεινεν in Reading I below has an augment (with vowel crasis: προε- → προὐ-) but no σα, and the ending -εν stands on the present stem τειν-: that is an imperfect of προτείνω "hold out, extend". The golden question, always, after finding the augment is: "where is the σα? – does the ending stand on the present stem or on a different stem?"

One vocabulary key accompanies narrative aorists: ἔπειτα "after that" – an adverb announcing a chain of events, which you will meet densely in later readings. And to round off today's ration, here is a small map for quick consultation while reading:

8. Reading I: The dream of Plato's temple (Aristides, Or. 51 Keil 61–63)

Context. Aelius Aristides (AD 117–181) was the most celebrated orator of the Roman Empire after Demosthenes – his contemporaries ranked the two men as a pair. He suffered from a wasting illness for decades, and his Hieroi Logoi (Sacred Tales) record his symptoms, remedies and the commands the god Asclepius sent him through dreams. Book V tells of a summer of stomach pain and a midnight march on the god's orders; its final part is a chain of dreams about the city of Athens, in which Asclepius promises him an oratorical rank beside Plato's. The passage below is one scene from that dream chain; the retelling keeps nearly the full original wording, replacing only participles (not yet learned) with indicative verbs and trimming a few clauses – each spot is noted. (Section numbers follow the Keil edition: Or. 51 Keil = Or. 27 Dindorf, because the two collections number differently.)

1 μετὰ ταῦτα ἐχωροῦμεν πρὸς τὸ Λύκειον, καὶ νεώς τις ἐξεδέχετο πάνυ μέγας καὶ καλός. 2 ἔβαινον ἄνω, καὶ παιδάριον προὔτεινεν ᾠὰ τρία· ἐγὼ δὲ παρῆλθον καὶ οὐκ ἔλαβον. 3 ἐνθύμιον ἐποιησάμην ὅτι χρὴ λαβεῖν τοῦ συμβόλου ἕνεκα. 4 ἐπέστρεψα οὖν καὶ ἔλαβον τὰ ᾠά. 5 ἐν δὲ τῷ προνάῳ ὁρῶ τὸν νεὼ Πλάτωνος ὄντα τοῦ φιλοσόφου, καὶ τὸ ἄγαλμα αὐτοῦ μέγα καὶ καλόν. 6 εἶπέ τις· "καὶ τρεῖς ἔδει νεὼς εἶναι Πλάτωνός γε." 7 κἀγὼ ἔφην· "τί δ' οὐ καὶ Δημοσθένους ὀγδοήκοντα, καὶ Ὁμήρου γε, οἶμαι;" 8 "τοὺς μὲν νεὼς τοῖς θεοῖς προσήκει καθιεροῦν· τοὺς δὲ ἄνδρας τοὺς ἐλλογίμους τῇ τῶν βιβλίων ἀναθέσει τιμᾶν." 9 τοιαῦτα εἶπον, καὶ ἐπανῄειν.

Sentence-by-sentence dissection

Sentence 1

μετὰ ταῦτα ἐχωροῦμεν πρὸς τὸ Λύκειον, καὶ νεώς τις ἐξεδέχετο πάνυ μέγας καὶ καλός – "After that we were making our way toward the Lyceum, and a temple came into view, very large and very beautiful". Two imperfects paint the background, as discussed in section 7: ἐχωροῦμεν (from χωρέω; gloss: make one's way) "advance toward", ἐξεδέχετο (from ἐκδέχομαι; gloss: come into view) "come into view". τὸ Λύκειον (gloss: the Lyceum) "the Lyceum" – the famous gymnasium of Athens, where Aristotle once taught; ὁ νεώς, -οῦ "temple" is a new word of this lesson (note the double spelling: the same word is also written ὁ ναός). The original uses ᾗ "to where" – a relative adverb not yet learned, so the retelling substitutes πρὸς + accusative.

Sentence 2

ἔβαινον ἄνω, καὶ παιδάριον προὔτεινεν ᾠὰ τρία· ἐγὼ δὲ παρῆλθον καὶ οὐκ ἔλαβον – "I was walking up, and a little boy was holding out three eggs; but I passed by and did not take them". This is the "star" sentence of the lesson:

  • ἔβαινον – imperfect of βαίνω "walk, step" (augment + stem βαιν- + secondary ending): background scene.
  • προὔτεινεν – imperfect of προτείνω "hold out, extend": prefix προ- + augment (with vowel crasis προε- → προὐ-, a contraction of προετεῖνεν) + the 3 Sg. ending -εν. Dissected in the achtung of section 7: an augment without a σα – imperfect!
  • παρῆλθον – thematic aorist of παρέρχομαι "pass by": prefix παρ(α)- + η augment (the alpha drops and ε lengthens into η: παρ-ή-λθ-ον) + the stem ἐλθ- familiar from ἦλθον + the 1 Sg./3 Pl. ending. Context here fixes "I passed by".
  • ἔλαβον – thematic aorist of λαμβάνω "take": ἐ-λαβ-ον, the ending -ον standing on a stem different from the present stem λαμβαν-. τὸ παιδάριον (gloss: little boy) "little boy"; τὸ ᾠόν, -οῦ (gloss: egg) "egg", plural τὰ ᾠά.

Sentence 3

ἐνθύμιον ἐποιησάμην ὅτι χρὴ λαβεῖν τοῦ συμβόλου ἕνεκα – "I formed the reflection that one must take them, for the sake of the token". ἐποιησάμην – sigmatic aorist m/p 1 Sg. of the contract verb ποιέω "make" (Lesson 12): augment ἐ- + stem ποιησ- (the same η as in ποιήσω!) + m/p ending -άμην – a "make for oneself" = "I resolved, noted in my mind". The middle meaning "for oneself" lives exactly in this sentence. ἐνθύμιον (gloss: reflection) here works adverbially like "in my mind"; χρὴ (gloss: one must) "one must" + infinitive λαβεῖν (aorist infinitive of λαμβάνω – infinitives will be collected in Lesson 18); ἕνεκα + genitive (gloss: for the sake of) "for the sake of"; τὸ σύμβολον (gloss: token) "token, sign" – in the dream, the eggs are the token offered to the god.

Sentence 4

ἐπέστρεψα οὖν καὶ ἔλαβον τὰ ᾠά – "So I turned back and took the eggs". A sigmatic aorist and a thematic one stand side by side in one sentence: ἐπέστρεψα (from ἐπιστρέφω "turn back": φ + σ → ψ, 1 Sg. ending -α) and ἔλαβον (an old friend again). The original wraps both ideas into the aorist participles ἐπιστραφεὶς καὶ λαβών "having turned back and taken" – forms not yet learned, to be met in Lesson 24; today they are split into two indicative verbs.

Sentence 5

ἐν δὲ τῷ προνάῳ ὁρῶ τὸν νεὼ Πλάτωνος ὄντα τοῦ φιλοσόφου, καὶ τὸ ἄγαλμα αὐτοῦ μέγα καὶ καλόν – "And in the vestibule I see the temple of Plato the philosopher, and his statue was very large and very beautiful". ὁρῶ is a historic present (Lesson 12): the dream is still flowing and Aristides presses the "close-up" button – translate into the past: "I saw". ὁ προνάος, -ου (gloss: vestibule) "vestibule, front hall"; ὄντα is the accusative participle of εἰμί "to be" (τὸν νεὼ … ὄντα "the temple being Plato's") – the participle of εἰμί is just familiar forms of "to be"; read it through. τὸ ἄγαλμα, -ατος "votive statue" (neuter -μα, Lesson 7); ὁ φιλόσοφος "philosopher".

Sentence 6

εἶπέ τις· "καὶ τρεῖς ἔδει νεὼς εἶναι Πλάτωνός γε." – "Someone said: 'There ought to be three temples for Plato at least.'" εἶπέ τις – thematic aorist 3 Sg. of λέγω "say" (εἶπε(ν)) + τις "someone" (Lesson 5). ἔδει – imperfect of δεῖ "it is necessary" (gloss: it was necessary). τρεῖς νεώς – the numeral "three" (Lesson 8) + accusative plural of ὁ νεώς: "three temples"; γε (gloss: at least) a balancing particle, "at least, anyhow". The original uses a genitive-absolute construction ("someone said" standing in the genitive absolute) – not yet learned, so the retelling turns it into direct speech.

Sentence 7

κἀγὼ ἔφην· "τί δ' οὐ καὶ Δημοσθένους ὀγδοήκοντα, καὶ Ὁμήρου γε, οἶμαι;" – "And I said: 'Why not eighty for Demosthenes, and for Homer too, I suppose?'". κἀγὼ is a crasis of καὶ ἐγώ "and I" – the same crasis you have met before; ἔφην – imperfect of φημί "say" (Lesson 12 already read ἔφη); τί δ' οὐ "why not"; ὀγδοήκοντα (gloss: eighty) "eighty" (kin to the numerals of Lesson 8); οἶμαι – a present deponent "I suppose". A playful detail: Demosthenes and Homer are the other two "giants" of letters, so Aristides allots each of them eighty temples.

Sentence 8

τοὺς μὲν νεὼς τοῖς θεοῖς προσήκει καθιεροῦν· τοὺς δὲ ἄνδρας τοὺς ἐλλογίμους τῇ τῶν βιβλίων ἀναθέσει τιμᾶν – "Temples are fitting to consecrate to the gods; but renowned men one should honour by the dedication of books". προσήκει (gloss: it is fitting) "it is fitting" + accusative + infinitive – the same "four-component" frame as δεῖ (Lesson 4). καθιεροῦν – present infinitive of καθιερόω "consecrate (a temple)"; the men–de pair (μὲν … δὲ …) shoulders both halves: τοὺς νεώς "temples (accusative plural)" vs τοὺς ἄνδρας τοὺς ἐλλογίμους "renowned men" – an accusative string as in Lesson 11; ἐλλόγιμος (gloss: renowned) "renowned" (a two-ending adjective, Lesson 9); τῇ τῶν βιβλίων ἀναθέσει – the dative of means: "by the dedication (of) books" – ἡ ἀνάθεσις (gloss: dedication) "dedication", τὸ βιβλίον "book". This is Aristides' "law of honours": temples for the gods, books for the talented – the orator's profession embraces the second half.

Sentence 9

τοιαῦτα εἶπον, καὶ ἐπανῄειν – "Having said such words, I was going home". εἶπον – thematic aorist 1 Sg. of λέγω; τοιαῦτα "such words" (an accusative form of τοιοῦτος, learn to recognize it); ἐπανῄειν – imperfect 1 Sg. of ἐπανέρχομαι "return" (long augment ηι + the ending -ν after a vowel). The closing sentence is a textbook aspect pair: the aorist εἶπον closes off the event "spoke", the imperfect ἐπανῄειν lets the scene "on the way home" drift on inside the dream.

The original, verbatim: ἀνιόντι οὖν μοι παιδάριον προὔτεινεν ᾠὰ τρία· κἀγὼ, παρῆλθον γὰρ οὐ λαβών, ἐνθύμιον ἐποιησάμην ὅτι χρὴ μέντοι λαβεῖν τοῦ συμβόλου ἕνεκα … τοιαῦτα εἰπὼν καὶ ἀκούσας ἐπανῄειν. Three active aorist participles play the three roles in the original: λαβών "without taking", εἰπών "having spoken", ἀκούσας "having heard" – all built from aorist stems you have just learned (ἔλαβον, εἶπον and ἤκουσα). The participle system will be dissected around Lesson 24; today you only need to recognize the shell: -ών/-ᾶς attached to an aorist stem. And one piece of history worth keeping: this dream closes with a good omen for Aristides' own oratorical rank – the dream of "Plato's temple" is the god's confirmation of the very claim the whole Sacred Tales cradles.

General grammar points to remember: the passage is a museum of Lesson 13 aspect: the imperfect paints the background (ἐχωροῦμεν, ἐξεδέχετο, ἔβαινον, ἐπανῄειν), the aorist seals each event (παρῆλθον, ἔλαβον, ἐπέστρεψα, εἶπον – both families dispatched: thematic and sigmatic), and one historic present, ὁρῶ, gives the close-up. Three parallel infinitives (λαβεῖν, καθιεροῦν and τιμᾶν) open the door to Lesson 18, while ἐποιησάμην is the middle "for oneself" form: telling oneself, engraving in the mind – the same m/p aorist ending you will meet throughout the lesson.

Step 2, Meaning and structure. The line of events: the party moves toward the Lyceum (C1); going up, a boy holds out three eggs, Aristides walks past without taking (C2); he tells himself he must take, because of the token (C3, χρὴ λαβεῖν + genitive after ἕνεκα); he turns back and takes (C4); in the vestibule he sees Plato's temple and the very large and beautiful statue (C5). Step 3, Discussion and connection. In the dream, a token has value when it is received: walking past means missing it, turning back to take it means joining fully in the god's message – the story reads like a lesson in reading omens right. The eighty-temple joke reveals the ambition to rank oneself beside Plato, Demosthenes and Homer; the closing line "temples for the gods, books for the talented" sounds like a joke, yet it confirms the rhetor's profession: "books dedicated to the talented" is exactly what Aristides is doing – writing rhetoric to rival the ancients.

9. Reading II: Klearchos deposits friendship (Xenophon, Anabasis 2,5)

Context. The battle of Cunaxa (401 BC) is over: Cyrus the Younger (Lesson 12) has fallen, and ten thousand Greek soldiers stand stranded in Mesopotamia without a commander, without supplies. On the Persian side, Tissaphernes – the satrap of Sardis, the outstanding cavalryman of King Artaxerxes II – is now the direct adversary. He proposes peace and then invites Klearchos (the Spartan general you met in Lesson 12, the man who once gathered an army with ten thousand dareiks) to his tent to negotiate. At this meeting Klearchos delivers a speech declaring his trust to Tissaphernes; sections 2,5,7–9 keep the speech nearly word for word, trimming only one optative sentence (Lesson 17) and one relative clause (Lesson 15).

Sentence-by-sentence dissection

Sentence 1

πρῶτον μὲν γὰρ καὶ μέγιστον οἱ θεῶν ἡμᾶς ὅρκοι κωλύουσι πολεμίους εἶναι ἀλλήλοις – "First and greatest: the oaths by the gods prevent us from being enemies to one another". The sentence opens with two adjectives (comparatives and superlatives get their own lesson, Supplementary Lesson 09b; for now read them as set phrases): πρῶτον … μέγιστον "the first and greatest point". The subject is οἱ θεῶν ὅρκοι – "the oaths of the gods" (an embedded genitive θεῶν, Lesson 1); the verb κωλύουσι (gloss: prevent) "prevent" + the accusative + infinitive frame (AcI, Lesson 4): "prevent us from being enemies"; ὁ πολέμιος (Lesson 11) "enemy" stands here in the accusative plural πολεμίους; ἀλλήλοις (gloss: one another) "one another" – the reciprocal pronoun, learn to recognize it.

Sentence 2

περὶ μὲν δὴ τῶν θεῶν τε καὶ τῶν ὅρκων οὕτω γιγνώσκω – "Concerning the gods and the oaths, this is how I understand the matter". γιγνώσκω "know, understand" (its aorist kinsman ἔγνων will appear later); οὕτω "in this way" (Lesson 12); δὴ, τε – two balancing particles, read them through. The frame περὶ + genitive "concerning a matter" is familiar from earlier lessons.

Sentence 3

παρὰ τοῖς θεοῖς γὰρ τὴν φιλίαν ἡμεῖς κατεθέμεθα – "For we deposited our friendship with the gods". Slow down for this one:

  • κατεθέμεθα – thematic aorist m/p 1 Pl.: prefix κατ(α)- + augment ἐ- + stem θε- + the ending -μεθα, exactly the secondary m/p slot of Lesson 11. The verb is κατατίθημι "set down, deposit" – a mi-verb (dissected in Lesson 20); today learn the face of κατεθέμεθα as "we set down, we deposited".
  • The middle meaning "for ourselves" shows clearly: depositing a pledge for oneself – the oath is a shared asset, both parties lodge it with the gods.
  • The original: παρ' οὓς ἡμεῖς τὴν φιλίαν συνθέμενοι κατεθέμεθα "with the very gods with whom we, after cementing it, deposited our friendship" – it has a relative clause (οὓς, Lesson 15) and an aorist m/p participle (συνθέμενοι "after cementing", from συντίθημι); the retelling joins the pieces straight for compactness.

Sentence 4

τῶν δ' ἀνθρωπίνων σὲ ἐγὼ ἐν τῷ παρόντι νομίζω μέγιστον εἶναι ἡμῖν ἀγαθόν – "And of the things that belong to men, at present I consider you the greatest good we have". ἐν τῷ παρόντι (gloss: at present) "at present" – an idiom built on the participle of εἰμί; νομίζω "consider" (Lesson 4) opens an AcI: σὲ … μέγιστον … ἀγαθόν "you are the greatest good" – ἀγαθός substantivized as "good (thing)" (Lesson 9). This sentence – a Spartan general speaking to the man about to betray him – is the summit of the whole chapter.

Sentence 5

σὺν μὲν γὰρ σοὶ πᾶσα μὲν ὁδὸς εὔπορος, πᾶς δὲ ποταμὸς διαβατός, τῶν τε ἐπιτηδείων οὐκ ἀπορία – "For with you: every road is easy to travel, every river crossable, no lack of provisions". σύν + dative "together with" (Lesson 4); the ending -τος on an adjective expresses possibility "able to be X-ed" – εὔπορος (gloss: easy to travel) "easy to travel", διαβατός (gloss: crossable) "crossable", the opposite of δύσπορος (gloss: hard to travel) "hard to travel" in sentence 7 (eu/dus "easy/hard" – learn to recognize them; Supplementary Lesson 09b will gather them); ἡ ἀπορία (gloss: want, lack) "lack", τὰ ἐπιτήδεια (gloss: provisions) "provisions".

Sentence 6

ἄνευ δὲ σοῦ πᾶσα μὲν διὰ σκότους ἡ ὁδός· οὐδὲν γὰρ αὐτῆς ἐπιστάμεθα – "But without you: every road lies through darkness; for we know nothing of it". ἄνευ + genitive (gloss: without) "without"; διὰ σκότους "through darkness" (τὸ σκότος, -ους, a third-declension neuter); ἐπιστάμεθα – present m/p 1 Pl. of the deponent ἐπίσταμαι (gloss: know) "know" + a genitive for the thing known (αὐτῆς "of it", namely ὁδός). Sentence 6 continues the men–de flow of the whole passage: the half σὺν σοὶ (sentence 5) – the half ἄνευ σοῦ (sentence 6).

Sentence 7

πᾶς δὲ ποταμὸς δύσπορος, πᾶς δὲ ὄχλος φοβερός, φοβερώτατον δ' ἐρημία· μεστὴ γὰρ πολλῆς ἀπορίας ἐστίν – "every river hard to cross, every crowd terrifying, most terrifying of all is the desert; for it is full of many wants". A rapid string of three nominal clauses (nominal sentences, Lesson 3); ὁ ὄχλος "crowd", ἡ ἐρημία "desert, loneliness" – two new words of this lesson; φοβερώτατον is the superlative (Supplementary Lesson 09b) of φοβερός "terrifying" (Lesson 11): read "most terrifying"; μεστὴ … ἐστίν (gloss: full of) "full of …" + genitive.

The original, the sentence left out (2,5,7): τὸν γὰρ θεῶν πόλεμον οὐκ οἶδ' οὔτ' ἀπὸ ποίου ἂν τάχους οὔτε ὅποι ἄν τις φεύγων ἀποφύγοι οὔτ' εἰς ποῖον ἂν σκότος ἀποδραίη – "a war against the gods: I know not at what speed, nor where one would flee and escape, nor into what dark one would slip away". The verbs ἀποφύγοι, ἀποδραίη belong to the optative (Lesson 17), so the retelling leaves them for later. The rest of the chapter (2,5,31–32) is the tragedy: ἐπεὶ δὲ ἦσαν ἐπὶ θύραις ταῖς Τισσαφέρνους, οἱ μὲν στρατηγοὶ παρεκλήθησαν εἴσω … οἵ τε ἔνδον ξυνελαμβάνοντο καὶ οἱ ἔξω κατεκόπησαν – "when they were at the doors of Tissaphernes' tent, the generals were invited inside … those inside were arrested and those outside were cut down". Tissaphernes obeyed the king: the Greek generals were handed over (that is the meaning of the chapter's title in the source collection) and executed; παρεκλήθησαν, κατεκόπησαν are passive aorists – the form Lesson 14 will teach, while ξυνελαμβάνοντο is an m/p imperfect with the archaic spelling ξυν- (= συν-). Klearchos, the man of Lesson 12 who gathered an army with ten thousand dareiks, placed his whole friendship in a treacherous man's hands – because walking through darkness without a guide is exactly what he himself had described.

General grammar points to remember: the passage assembles blocks of Lesson 13 and earlier lessons: κατεθέμεθα (C3) is the middle/passive thematic aorist of the verb "to place, deposit", a form to learn by face, with the mi-verb family dissected only in Lesson 20; the -tos adjectives (εὔπορος, διαβατός, δύσπορος) speak of ability: easy to travel, crossable, hard to cross; the superlative φοβερώτατον (C7) is an early face of the comparison chapter in Lesson 09b; and the argument leans on the dative (παρὰ τοῖς θεοῖς in C3) for its basic rhythm.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. The -tos words: εὔπορος (C5, easy road), διαβατός (C5, crossable river), δύσπορος (C7, hard to cross, the prefix δύσ joined to the same root as εὔπορος). The word with an m/p aorist: κατεθέμεθα (C3, the -μεθα ending of the thematic m/p aorist, learn its face). The superlative: φοβερώτατον (C7, from the adjective φοβερός with the superlative ending, an early face of Lesson 09b).

Step 2, Meaning and structure. Sentence 3: the friendship is deposited with the gods (the dative marks position and keeper); sentence 4: the speaker considers Tissaphernes the greatest of human goods (μέγιστον with the infinitive εἶναι, the consider-to-be frame). The men–de table: with you the road is easy, the river crossable, no lack of provisions (C5); without you the road lies through darkness because we know nothing (οὐδὲν ἐπιστάμεθα, C6), the river hard to cross, the crowd terrifying, most terrifying of all the desert because it is full of wants (C7).

Step 3, Discussion and connection. Sentences 1 to 4 are a diplomat's promises: the gods and the oaths are made the guarantors, and then the listener is raised to the greatest of human goods. History then runs the other way: Tissaphernes invites Klearchos to a parley and arrests and kills him. The lesson: the rounder the flattery, the more pressing the question of what the speaker gains; a "we win together, we perish without you" speech still scratches the praised listener's itch, so it still persuades – and all the more deserves to be checked by deeds rather than adjectives.

12. Exercises

Exercise 1 – Decline the aorist paradigm

Decline the active aorist of λύω in all six persons and the m/p aorist of λύω; add the transliteration of the 2nd singular in each paradigm.

Answers 1

Active: ἔλυσα, ἔλυσας, ἔλυσε(ν), ἐλύσαμεν, ἐλύσατε, ἔλυσαν – 2 Sg. élusas. Middle: ἐλυσάμην, ἐλύσω, ἐλύσατο, ἐλυσάμεθα, ἐλύσασθε, ἐλύσαντο – 2 Sg. elúsō. The same stem λυσ-, only the endings differ; the recessive accent draws back near the augment in both paradigms.

Exercise 2 – Consonant + σ

For the following stems (augment supplied), write the 1 Sg. aorist: ἐ-γραφ-, ἐ-παιδευ-, ἐ-κηρυκ-, ἐ-ποιη-, ἐ-δοκ-. Give the meaning of each form (write, educate, proclaim, make, seem).

Answers 2

ἔγραψα (φ + σ → ψ) "I wrote"; ἐπαίδευσα "I educated"; ἐκήρυξα (κ + σ → ξ) "I proclaimed"; ἐποίησα "I made"; ἔδοξα (κ + σ → ξ) "I seemed".

Exercise 3 – Sigmatic or thematic?

Sort the following forms into (a) sigmatic aorist, (b) thematic aorist, (c) imperfect: εὗρον, ἐβουλευσάμην, ἐποίει, ἔμεινα, ἐγένετο, ἔλυσας, ἦλθε(ν).

Answers 3

(a) sigmatic: ἐβουλευσάμην (m/p), ἔλυσας. (b) thematic: εὗρον, ἔμεινα (the liquid aorist also belongs to the group with thematic endings), ἐγένετο (m/p), ἦλθε(ν). (c) imperfect: ἐποίει – the variable vowel ει stands between stem and ending, with no σ.

Exercise 4 – Translate into Greek

Translate: (1) "I found the book." (2) "The gods prevented the war." (3) "We deposited the oath." (use the aorists: εὑρίσκω, κωλύω, κατατίθημι).

Answers 4

(1) εὗρον τὸ βιβλίον. (2) οἱ θεοὶ ἐκώλυσαν τὸν πόλεμον. (3) τὸν ὅρκον κατεθέμεθα. All three show the augment: the past carries its "marker" at the front of the word.

Exercise 5 – Reading in context

Read the passage ἔβαινον ἄνω, καὶ παιδάριον προὔτεινεν ᾠὰ τρία· ἐγὼ δὲ παρῆλθον καὶ οὐκ ἔλαβον (Reading I, sentence 2) and answer: which verbs paint the background (imperfect) and which verbs record events (aorist)? Why do all four verbs carry an augment, yet only two of them are aorists?

Answers 5

Background: ἔβαινον, προὔτεινεν (secondary endings, no σ, carrying the flowing scene "walking up, holding out the eggs"). Events: παρῆλθον, ἔλαβον (thematic aorists: the σ is hidden inside the stem, and the ending -ον closes off "passed by, did not take"). The augment only says "past"; the shape of the stem and the ending says "background or closing beat": the imperfect carries the scene with variable-vowel secondary endings, while the aorist closes the event with its own characteristic endings.

Exercise 6 – Listen and spell

Read aloud (or have someone read to you) the following forms and then write them from memory with a tense label: ἔλυσαν, ἐλύσαντο, ἐλύσαντο, ἐλύοντο – which two forms coincide, and why is this sentence a listening "trap"?

Answers 6

ἐλύσαντο appears twice in the question because the aorist m/p 3 Pl. has only one form. The rest: ἔλυσαν (aorist active "they loosened"), ἐλύοντο (imperfect m/p "they were loosening for themselves"). The trap lies in two near-identical pairs: ἔλυσαν vs ἐλύσαντο (active vs m/p) and ἐλύσαντο vs ἐλύοντο (aorist vs imperfect): close to the ear, but in writing the variable vowel σα/ο differs.
