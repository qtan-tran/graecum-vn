Goals for this lesson: you can (1) explain how the true passive differs from the middle voice, and conjugate the passive aorist of the first class (ἐλύθην) in all six persons, contract verbs included (ἐτιμήθην); (2) recognize and conjugate the second aorist passive with the short -η- (ἐφάνην, ἠλλάγην, ἐβλάβην); (3) conjugate the passive future λυθήσομαι and explain why it borrows the endings of the middle future; (4) conjugate the perfect middle/passive λέλυμαι and grasp the meaning "a state still remaining"; (5) build the map of six stems (principal parts) of a verb and know which tenses each stem produces; (6) read two original passages: Plato, Protagoras 313d–e and Meno 89d–92c.

1. The true passive: the marker -θη- and the aorist passive ἐλύθην

In Lesson 10 you learned a mildly surprising truth: the Greek present has no passive form of its own – middle and passive share one set of endings (παιδεύεται is both "he educates himself" and "he is educated"). But in the aorist, Greek suddenly splits into two clearly marked paths: the middle aorist (ἐλυσάμην, Lesson 13) wears the middle endings, while the true passive receives an ending of its own, never met before: -θη-, inserted between the stem and the personal ending.

You meetFormMeaning
Middle aorist (Lesson 13)ἐλυσάμην"I loosened for myself / I saw to the loosing" – the subject does it himself
Passive aorist – new todayἐλύθην"I was loosened" – the subject undergoes the action, someone else acts upon him

The full paradigm of the first aorist passive with the model verb λύω:

PersonFormTransliterationEnglish
1 Sg.ἐλύθηνelýthēnI was loosened
2 Sg.ἐλύθηςelýthēsyou were loosened
3 Sg.ἐλύθηelýthēhe/she/it was loosened
1 Pl.ἐλύθημενelýthēmenwe were loosened
2 Pl.ἐλύθητεelýthēteyou (pl.) were loosened
3 Pl.ἐλύθησανelýthēsanthey were loosened

Reading this table closely reveals three familiar patterns plus one new thing: (1) the augment ἐ- stands at the front as in every past tense (Lesson 11); (2) the ending set -ν/-ς/-μεν/-τε/-σαν (the 3 Sg. has no ending) is precisely the active ending set of the sigmatic aorist (ἔλυσα, ἔλυσας, ἔλυσε(ν)…, Lesson 13) – the passive "borrows back" the active endings; (3) the only genuinely new thing is the block -θη- wedged between stem and ending. The infinitive of the aorist passive is λυθῆναι "to be loosened" – compare the present middle infinitive λύεσθαι (Lesson 10): the passive adds -θη- and ends in -ναι.

Achtung – accent: the syllable -θη- always "pulls" the accent onto itself across the whole paradigm (ἐλύθην, ἐλύθημεν, ἐλύθησαν) – do not drag the accent back to the augment in the recessive manner of ἔλυσα. This is a rule of its own for the aorist passive: the accent sits on -θη-. One quick test: if you "see" a past-tense word whose accented syllable stands immediately before the ending -ν/-ς/-μεν/-τε/-σαν, together with an augment, it is almost certainly an aorist passive.

Achtung – meaning: ἐλύθη is not "he loosened"! Same stem λυ-, but -θη- flips the roles: the subject becomes the one acted upon. Mistranslating in this direction is the classic beginner's error: ἔλυσε "he loosened" (active, Lesson 13) vs ἐλύθη "he was loosened" (passive, today) – the whole difference is one block -θη-.

Latin corner: Latin wraps the whole passive system into single endings: present docētur "he is taught" – attached the same way as Greek παιδεύεται. But for past and future passives, Latin must compound: doctus est "was taught" (participle + the verb esse), doctus erit "will be taught". Greek still packs it into one word: ἐδιδάχθη, διδαχθήσεται. The consequence when translating Latin into Greek: whenever you meet amātus est, think at once of an aorist passive ἐλύθη; two Latin words = one Greek word.

2. The second aorist passive & contract verbs: ἐφάνην, ἠλλάγην, ἐτιμήθην

Just as the active aorist comes in two lines (sigmatic ἔλυσα and thematic ἦλθον – Lesson 13), the passive aorist also has two lines: the first plugs in the long -θη- (section 1), the second uses a short -η- – called the second aorist passive. For some verbs this passive stem is even more "clipped" than the present stem:

PresentSecond aorist passivePassive meaning
φαίνω "shine; bring to light"ἐφάνην (ephánēn)"I was shown forth / I appeared"
ἀλλάσσω "change"ἠλλάγην (ēllágēn)"I was changed (over to the other side)"
βλάπτω "harm"ἐβλάβην (eblábēn)"I was harmed"
στέλλω "send"ἐστάλην (estálēn)"I was sent"

The paradigm with ἐφάνην – note the 3 Pl. ending:

PersonFormTransliteration
1 Sg.ἐφάνηνephánēn
2 Sg.ἐφάνηςephánēs
3 Sg.ἐφάνηephánē
1 Pl.ἐφάνημενephánēmen
2 Pl.ἐφάνητεephánēte
3 Pl.ἐφάνησανephánēsan

Two differences from the first line: the -η- is short (no θ), and the 3 Pl. ends in -σαν – exactly like the 3 Pl. of the sigmatic active aorist (ἔλυσαν, Lesson 13). The mnemonic pattern: second-passive endings = the endings of the second/third active aorist plus -η-. Contract verbs (Lesson 12), for their part, hold no surprises: the contract stem τιμη- stays intact when passivized, simply adding -θη-: ἐτιμήθην "I was honored", ἐδηλώθη "it was brought to light" – the long -η- of the contract stem and the -θη- line up neatly, with the accent resting on the stem's long vowel.

Achtung – the double trap: (1) ἐφάνη (aorist passive of φαίνω) looks just one "sip" away from ἔφην – the imperfect of φημί "to say" (Lesson 12: ἔφη "he said"). Both words are thick on the ground in Platonic dialogue and are easy to misread: accent on -άν- = the passive "appeared"; accent on the augment = "said". (2) Do not "add" -θη- onto the second aorist passive: ἐφάνην already is the passive form; the form *ἐφανθέν does not exist at this tense.

Where -θη- comes from: historical grammarians read -θη- as a causative ("make-do") element of the old language: φαίνω "bring to light" has a kinsman in Sanskrit bhā́ti. The trace of the "make" meaning is still audible in some words that carry -θη- yet are best translated actively: ἐβουλήθην "I wanted" (aorist of the deponent βούλομαι – passive in form, entirely active in meaning; compare the imperfect ἐβουλόμην of Lesson 11 and the future βουλήσομαι of Lesson 12). This is "the deponent of the aorist passive" – it returns in the confusables table at the end of the lesson.

3. The passive future: λυθήσομαι

The natural next question: if the aorist has a passive of its own, where is the future's? The answer is delicate: Greek builds no new ending for the passive future – it borrows the whole set of middle future endings from Lesson 12 (-σομαι/-σῃ/-σεται…), but attaches that set to the aorist passive stem. The formula:

ComponentAorist passivePassive future
Stemλυ-θη- (augment marking the past)λυ-θη- (no augment: future business)
Endingsthe active set -ν/-ς/-μεν/-τε/-σανthe middle future set -σομαι/-σῃ/-σεται…
Resultἐλύθην "I was loosened"λυθήσομαι "I will be loosened"

The full six-person paradigm:

PersonFormTransliterationEnglish
1 Sg.λυθήσομαιlythḗsomaiI will be loosened
2 Sg.λυθήσῃlythḗsēiyou will be loosened
3 Sg.λυθήσεταιlythḗsetaihe/she/it will be loosened
1 Pl.λυθησόμεθαlythēsómethawe will be loosened
2 Pl.λυθήσεσθεlythḗsestheyou (pl.) will be loosened
3 Pl.λυθήσονταιlythḗsontaithey will be loosened

The second aorist passive breeds its future by the same formula, simply with the short -η-: φανήσομαι "I will appear / be shown forth", ἀλλαγήσομαι "I will be changed". And the star pair in today's real text: διδαχθήσομαι "I will be taught" – from διδάσκω, the central verb of both readings today.

Achtung – the mirror pair, to be weighed by its stem: λυθήσομαι and λύσομαι (middle future, Lesson 12) differ by exactly one -θη-, just like the past pair ἐλύθην/ἐλυσάμην. When you meet a -σομαι form, ask: is this stem the present stem (middle future) or the aorist passive stem (passive future)? The anchor: if a long -η- stands next to the -σ- → it smells passive: βουλήσομαι "will want" (the inherited contract stem βουλη- of βούλομαι) vs λυθήσομαι "will be loosened" (the stem λυθη- bred by the aorist passive). Neither can have an augment – the augment cannot help you in the future; only the stem can.

A pattern for life: one dictionary line equips you to build two passive tenses: the entry "aorist passive" gives ἐλύθην → you know the stem λυθη- → the aorist passive (ἐλύθην, ἐλύθης, ἐλύθη…) and the passive future (λυθήσομαι, λυθήσῃ, λυθήσεται…) – two tenses, one stem. This is why people call the aorist passive stem "the passive stem" of a verb: it serves both past and future.

4. The perfect middle/passive: λέλυμαι, πέπαυται

Now for the last tense still missing from the map: the perfect – the tense that says "X has happened, and the state of X is still with us". Unlike the aorist (a closed event in the past, Lesson 13), the perfect stands in the present to report the legacy of the past: πέπαυται "it has stopped – and is still at rest"; κέκλημαι "I have been called – and still carry that name".

The perfect's number-one formal sign is reduplication: the initial consonant of the stem is repeated + -ε-: stem λυ- → λε-λυ-; παυ- → πε-παυ-; κλη- (the stem of καλέω) → κε-κλη-. With verbs that begin with a vowel, the initial vowel is lengthened instead (ἀ- → ᾱ-, augment-style: ἀκούω → ἀκήκοα) – precisely the "vowel augment" you know from Lesson 11.

The paradigm of the perfect middle/passive of λύω:

PersonFormTransliterationEnglish
1 Sg.λέλυμαιlélumaiI am in the state of having been loosened
2 Sg.λέλυσαιlélusaiyou are in the state of having been loosened
3 Sg.λέλυταιlélutaihe/she/it is in the state of having been loosened
1 Pl.λελύμεθαlelúmethawe are in the state of having been loosened
2 Pl.λέλυσθεlélustheyou (pl.) are in the state of having been loosened
3 Pl.λέλυνταιléluntaithey are in the state of having been loosened

A big pattern emerges: the endings -μαι/-σαι/-ται/-μεθα/-σθε/-νται are precisely the middle endings of the present (Lesson 10: λύομαι, λύῃ, λύεται…). Slotting this into the system you know: the imperfect m/p borrows the secondary endings (-μην/-σο/-το…, Lesson 11), while the perfect m/p borrows the primary ones – same stem, only reduplication and endings change. The meaning of the perfect m/p is doubled like its form: both middle ("be in a state for oneself") and passive ("have been X-ed") – the text decides which, and every dictionary lists these endings in one line.

Achtung – do not demand an augment: the perfect has no augment – the job of saying "I speak of what has happened" is already done by the reduplication. A common error: writing *ἐλέλυμαι. Quick check: reduplication λε-λε-/πε-πε-/κε-κε- at the head of the word → perfect → no augment, with the accent following the recessive rule (λέλυμαι, λελύμεθα). The awkward pair: λέλυται (perfect m/p) vs λύεται (present m/p, Lesson 10) – one reduplication apart, and a whole timeframe apart in meaning: "has been loosened (and still is)" vs "is being loosened".

The true ancestor: οἶδα. The finest verb of the perfect tense is οἶδα "I know" (oîda). In form it is the perfect of an old verb εἴδω "see" (gloss: see) – its original meaning is "I have seen, therefore I know". That is why its paradigm wears today's face: οἶδα, οἶσθα, οἶδε(ν), ἴσμεν, ἴστε, ἴσασι(ν) – with the 3 Sg. οἶδε and the 3 Pl. ἴσασι(ν) thick on the ground in Plato, all with the present meaning "know". A valuable pattern: many languages use "have seen" = "know" – Latin likewise has nōvī (perfect of nōscere) with the present meaning "I know well". In both readings today, ἴσασιν "they know" will play a lead role.

Latin corner: the Latin perfectum (amāvī) packs both views – "event closed" and "state remaining" – whereas Greek splits them: the aorist ἔλυσα packs the event (Lesson 13), the perfect λέλυκα packs the state (today + the map in section 5). Translating Latin → Greek you must ask after the intent: vēnī in a told story is an aorist ἦλθον; cognōvī "I have come to know (and still remember)" is a perfect. In the other direction, translating a Greek perfect into English usually needs an added "…and still is" so that it does not collapse into an aorist.

5. The map of six stems: λύω, λύσω, ἔλυσα, λέλυκα, λέλυμαι, ἐλύθην

Enough! After thirteen lessons of gathering pieces, today we assemble the whole map. Every Greek verb is introduced by the dictionary through a series of forms called the six principal parts – and each part is a "workshop" producing a whole cluster of tenses and voices:

#Principal part of λύωNameWhat it produces
1λύω (lúō)presentpresent active + the whole present m/p (λύω, λύομαι, λύεται…) and the imperfect (ἔλυον, ἐλυόμην, Lesson 11)
2λύσω (lúsō)future active/middlefuture active λύσω + middle future λύσομαι (Lesson 12)
3ἔλυσα (élusa)active aoristsigmatic aorist ἔλυσα + middle aorist ἐλυσάμην (Lesson 13)
4λέλυκα (léluka)active perfectλέλυκα, λέλυκας, λέλυκε(ν)… – preview: full anatomy later in the course
5λέλυμαι (lélumai)perfect middle/passivethe whole table of section 4 today
6ἐλύθην (elýthēn)aorist passiveaorist passive ἐλύθην + passive future λυθήσομαι (section 3: one stem, two tenses)

Look at row 6 one last time: the aorist passive stem is the only double-duty stem of the system – every other stem produces one cluster, this one alone produces two (past + future). That is Greek economy for you: having gone to the trouble of building a passive stem, the language exploits it to the full.

Achtung – the map does not always have six squares: whatever a verb lacks, the dictionary leaves blank, and you are not allowed to "improvise" it. ἔρχομαι has no aorist passive (the meaning "go" resists passivization); many deponents list only the m/p line. And wherever a square is empty, Greek often solves it with a suppletive verb: the present ὁρῶ "I see" borrows the aorist of an entirely different verb: εἶδον (Lesson 13); λέγω "I say" borrows εἶπον. The reading lesson: do not look up each new form as a fresh headword – look up its family of six stems once and mark it.

Latin corner: Latin needs only four principal parts: amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum – because from amāv- you can compute amāt-, and from amāre you can compute amābā-/amāb-: Latin endings can be derived from one another. Greek cannot be computed: ἦλθον is not visible from ἔρχομαι, εἶδον is not visible from ὁρῶ – hence the drill of learning the set of six forms like a phone number for each verb. This is one of the reasons a Greek dictionary is thicker than a Latin dictionary of the same size.

6. The strategy of "peeling the passive" & the agent ὑπό + genitive

Pull all the signals of thirteen lessons into one four-question sequence – the order of the questions matters like the steps of an operation: the wrapping first, the core last:

StepThe question shone onto the formConclusion
1Is there an augment ἐ- or an initial-vowel lengthening?Past (imperfect, aorist, aorist passive); none → present, future, or perfect (reduplication replaces the augment)
2Is there reduplication λε-λε-/πε-πε-/-κε-?Perfect; endings -μαι/-σαι/-ται → middle/passive (λέλυμαι)
3Is there a -θη- before the ending?First aorist passive (ἐλύθην) – or a deponent (ἐβουλήθην "wanted": check the stem in the dictionary)
4Bare stem (no augment) + -θήσομαι?Passive future (λυθήσομαι) – aorist passive stem + middle future endings

A passive needs to say who performed the action: the agent stands in the genitive with ὑπό (review of Lesson 10) – the twin of Latin ā/ab + ablative:

ActivePassive
ὁ σοφιστὴς ἐβλάψε τὸν νέον
"the sophist harmed the young man"		ὁ νέος ἐβλάβη ὑπὸ τοῦ σοφιστοῦ
"the young man was harmed by the sophist"

The mechanics of the transformation: the accusative object τὸν νέον rises to become the nominative subject ὁ νέος; the old subject ὁ σοφιστής drops into a genitive phrase after ὑπό; the verb swaps into passive endings. This is exactly the Latin chain of transformation (sophistam laesit → iuvenis ā sophista laesus est) – except that Greek wraps it into one word.

Achtung – the two detours: (1) -θη- has no monopoly on the passive: the deponent ἐβουλήθην "wanted" wears a passive face with an active meaning – only the dictionary can judge (section 2). (2) Conversely, not every passive meaning carries -θη-: in the present, the passive still uses the middle endings (παιδεύεται, Lesson 10); in the middle aorist, some verbs carry a nearly passive meaning (ἠγάσθη "was admired", Lesson 12 – middle endings, translated passively). The reading rule: the face tells you the tense, the dictionary tells you the meaning – two independent pieces of information; do not merge them.

8. Reading I: The market of knowledge (Plato, Protagoras 313d–e)

Context: the young Hippokrates wakes Socrates in the dead of night: tomorrow morning he wants to hand over money to Protagoras – the most famous sophist in Greece – in order to "learn to be outstanding". Socrates draws him out into the courtyard and reads him a comparison: buying knowledge is more dangerous than buying food. The whole passage is a story about a market – and in a market, sellers praise everything.

1 οἵ τε κάπηλοι ὧν ἄγουσιν ἀγωγίμων οὔτε αὐτοὶ ἴσασιν ὅτι χρηστὸν ἢ πονηρόν· ἐπαινοῦσι δὲ πάντα πωλοῦντες. 2 οὔτε οἱ ὠνούμενοι παρ᾽ αὐτῶν, ἐὰν μή τις τύχῃ γυμναστικὸς ἢ ἰατρὸς ὤν. 3 οὕτω δὲ καὶ οἱ τὰ μαθήματα περιάγοντες κατὰ τὰς πόλεις ἐπαινοῦσιν πάντα ἃ πωλοῦσιν. 4 εἰ μὲν οὖν σὺ ἐπιστήμων εἶ τούτων, ἀσφαλές σοι ὠνεῖσθαι μαθήματα καὶ παρὰ Πρωταγόρου καὶ παρ᾽ ἄλλου ὁτουοῦν. 5 εἰ δὲ μή, ὅρα, ὦ μακάριε· πολὺ μείζων κίνδυνος ἐν τῇ τῶν μαθημάτων ὠνῇ ἢ ἐν τῇ τῶν σιτίων.

hoí te kápēloi hō̂n ágousin agōgímōn oúte aytoì ísasin hóti khrēstòn ḕ ponērón· epainoûsi dè pánta pōloûntes. oúte hoi ōnoúmenoi par' aytō̂n, eàn mḗ tis týkhē gymnastikòs ḕ iatròs ṓn. hoútō dè kaì hoi tà mathḗmata periágontes katà tàs póleis epainoûsin pánta hà pōloûsin. ei mèn oûn sỳ epistḗmōn eî toútōn, asphalés soi ōneîsthai mathḗmata kaì parà rōtagórou kaì par' állou hotouoûn. ei dè mḗ, hóra, ō̂ makárie· polỳ meízōn kíndynos en tē̂ tō̂n mathēmátōn ōnē̂ ḕ en tē̂ tō̂n sitíōn.

Three reading steps: work through them in order before scrolling down to the translation; the answers appear only in print, for checking. The text is numbered by sentence: C4 means sentence 4.

  1. Step 1, Formal observation: label the three participle blocks in sentences 1, 2 and 3: active or middle/receptive, meaning pointing outward or toward oneself? Circle the pair of condition clauses leading sentences 4 and 5, and find one comparative in sentence 5.
  2. Step 2, Meaning and structure: Socrates' comparison runs across three levels: the food-sellers (sentences 1 and 2), the knowledge-sellers (sentence 3), the advice to Hippokrates (sentences 4 and 5). In Socrates' view, who is safe when buying, and where does the irony lie? Note the Greek phrase for the refrain of praising everything, and for the picture of carrying the wares of knowledge around the cities.
  3. Step 3, Discussion and connection: in the food market there are trainers and doctors to inspect the wares; in today's knowledge market, who plays that role (independent reviews, former students, credentials)? An advertisement that praises a course to the skies matches which refrain in the passage? Is the warning about knowledge that cannot be given back (info box at the end) still persuasive?

Translation:

English (free translation)
The hucksters do not themselves know whether the wares they carry along are good or bad; they praise everything they sell.
Nor do those who buy from them know – unless someone happens to be a trainer or a doctor.
And so those who carry lessons around through the cities likewise praise all the things they sell.
If, then, you are a knower of these things, it is safe for you to buy lessons, both from Protagoras and from anyone else at all.
But if not, take care, my blessed friend: buying lessons is a far greater danger than buying food.

Sentence-by-sentence dissection:

(1) οἵ τε κάπηλοι "the hucksters" (a noun phrase with the plural article, Lesson 7); ὧν ἄγουσιν ἀγωγίμων – a relative clause preview (Lesson 15): "(the wares) which they carry along" – ἄγω (gloss: lead) "lead, carry" + ἀγωγίμος "carriable ware", a kinsman of today's -τος adjectives; ἴσασιν is the 3 Pl. of οἶδα – perfect in form, present in meaning (section 4): "they know"; χρηστὸν ἢ πονηρόν "good or bad" – the adjective pair of Lesson 9; ἐπαινοῦσι δὲ πάντα πωλοῦντες – a present active participle (met by sight in Lesson 13): "they praise everything while selling".

(2) οἱ ὠνούμενοι – a present m/p participle of the deponent ὠνέομαι (gloss: buy) "buy": "the buyers"; ἐὰν μή τις τύχῃ γυμναστικὸς ἢ ἰατρὸς ὤν "unless someone happens to be a trainer or a doctor" – τυγχάνω + noun phrase, and ὤν the participle of εἰμί (Lesson 2): two faces met by sight, extremely common in Plato; γυμναστικός for the body, ἰατρικός for illness – whoever "knows the trade" cannot be cheated.

(3) οὕτω δὲ καὶ… "and so, likewise…" – the turn from food to knowledge; τὰ μαθήματα "the lessons" (today's lesson has this word in its vocabulary); περιάγοντες (gloss: carry around) "carrying around" – περί + ἄγω: a familiar active compound; ἃ πωλοῦσιν – the relative ἅ (Lesson 15 preview): "the things (they) sell"; the pair κατὰ τὰς πόλεις "throughout the cities" – accusative plural of ἡ πόλις (Lesson 3).

(4) The key sentence: ἐπιστήμων εἶ "you are a knower" (ἐπιστήμων, -ονος – the adjective brother of ἐπιστήμη); ἀσφαλές σοι ὠνεῖσθαι – the pattern "adjective + dative of person + infinitive" like δεῖ (Lesson 4): "it is safe for you to buy"; ὠνεῖσθαι the present m/p infinitive of ὠνέομαι; ὁτουοῦν (gloss: anyone whatsoever) "anyone at all". Socrates' logic: only someone who already knows is safe buying knowledge – the mocking paradox: if you already know, why buy at all?

(5) εἰ δὲ μή – the shorthand idiom "if not (so)" (εἰ μὲν…εἰ δὲ μή is a paired condition construction, Lesson 21 preview); ὅρα, ὦ μακάριε – the 2nd-person imperative + vocative (Lesson 4): "take care, my blessed friend"; πολὺ μείζων… κίνδυνος – a comparative (Supplementary Lesson 09b preview) "a much greater danger"; ἐν τῇ τῶν μαθημάτων ὠνῇ – a genitive inside a dative: "in the buying of lessons" – ἡ ὠνή the noun kin of ὠνέομαι, the counterpart of Latin emptiō.

The next lines (314a): Socrates continues: μὴ περὶ τοῖς φιλτάτοις κυβεύῃς "do not gamble with what is dearest to you" – food once bought can be loaded onto a cart, and an expert can be invited to taste it; but knowledge goes εἰς τὴν ψυχὴν "into the soul" the moment you learn it, and it cannot be given back. This warning will become a familiar one to every Greek family whose son pays a sophist.

General grammar points to remember: the passage is a museum of participles and conditional clauses: πωλοῦντες (C1) and περιάγοντες (C3) are present active participles, ὠνούμενοι (C2) is a present middle/receptive participle of a deponent with active meaning; the pair εἰ μὲν οὖν (C4) balancing εἰ δὲ μή (C5) is a pair of balancing condition clauses; and μείζων (C5) is a comparative, an early face of Lesson 09b.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. πωλοῦντες (C1, present active: selling), ὠνούμενοι (C2, present middle/receptive of ὠνέομαι, a deponent with active meaning: buying), περιάγοντες (C3, present active of περί + ἄγω: carrying around). The conditional pair: εἰ μὲν οὖν in C4 and εἰ δὲ μή in C5. The comparative: μείζων (C5, much greater).

Step 2, Meaning and structure. Only the person who already knows (ἐπιστήμων in C4, the consider-to-be frame) is safe when buying; the irony: the one who already knows has no need to buy, the one who needs to buy most does not know, and there is no one to inspect the wares for him. The two markets share one refrain: ἐπαινοῦσι praising everything in C1 (the food-seller) and in C3 (the knowledge-seller, whose ware is τὰ μαθήματα, carried around the cities). The conclusion in C5: buying knowledge is far more dangerous than buying food.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. The wares-inspecting role belongs to someone with real expertise and no stake in the sale; course advertisements still use exactly the praise-everything rhythm; and the warning that knowledge cannot be returned still holds in that ratings and opinions enter our heads faster than evidence – though in the open-comment era a learner also gains new channels for contacting former buyers and inspecting the wares before paying.

9. Reading II: Can virtue be taught? (Plato, Meno 89d–92c)

Context: Menon, an aristocrat from Thessaly, asks Socrates: can virtue (ἀρετή) be taught (διδακτόν)? Socrates nods in his own fashion: if virtue is knowledge, then it is certainly teachable – but let us look for who teaches it. He turns to an Athenian sitting nearby: the politician Anytos. This passage is one of the most ironic situations Plato ever wrote.

English (free translation)
If any thing at all is teachable, and not virtue alone, must it not have both teachers and learners?
And for whatever has neither teachers nor learners, we would reasonably conclude that it is not teachable.
Yet I, though I have often sought whether there are any teachers of it, cannot find one, do everything I can.
Anytos: By Heracles, hold your tongue, Socrates! Let no one of my folk take into his head such madness as to be insulted after going to these men.
For they are, plain to see, the disgrace and the ruin of those who keep company with them.
I know one man, Protagoras, who acquired more money from this very wisdom than Phidias.
Protagoras corrupted those who kept company with him unnoticed through the whole of Greece; and to this very day, while he still enjoys repute, he has not stopped in the least.

Sentence-by-sentence dissection:

(1) The whole logic of the question: εἰ ἔστιν διδακτὸν ὁτιοῦν πρᾶγμα "if any thing at all is teachable"; διδακτόν is precisely the -τος adjective from διδάσκω: "able-to-be-taught" – a kinsman of today's -τος pattern (see the info box below section 9); ὁτιοῦν "anything at all"; οὐκ ἀναγκαῖον… εἶναι; – the rhetorical question: "is it not necessary that it have both teachers and learners?" (ἀναγκαῖον + infinitive, the pattern of δεῖ from Lesson 4).

(2) οὗ μήτε… μήτε… "that which has neither… nor…"; εἶεν and εἰκάζοιμεν – two optative forms (Lesson 17): today just learn the faces, translating "were"/"we would guess"; μὴ διδακτὸν εἶναι – the negated infinitive with μὴ (Lesson 4). Socrates builds his syllogism: teachable → it has teachers; no teachers → not teachable.

(3) πολλάκις γοῦν "at all events, many times"; ζητῶν the present participle of ζητέω "seek"; αὐτῆς the genitive referring to ἀρετή (Lesson 3: the feminine genitive); πάντα ποιῶν – an idiom to trust word for word: "doing everything"; οὐ δύναμαι εὑρεῖν – δύναμαι + infinitive (Lesson 2), εὑρεῖν the aorist infinitive of εὑρίσκω – you already met εὗρον in Lesson 13: same verb, sigmatic infinitive.

(4) The grammatical star of the reading: Ἡράκλεις, εὐφήμει – an exclamation + the 2nd-person imperative: "By Heracles, hold your tongue!" (gloss: fie!, hold your tongue); τοιαύτη μανία λάβοι – τοιοῦτος the demonstrative (Lesson 6), μανία "madness", λάβοι an optative (Lesson 17); ὥστε παρὰ τούτους ἐλθόντα λωβηθῆναι – ὥστε + infinitive "so as to…": ἐλθόντα an aorist participle (met by sight in Lesson 13), and λωβηθῆναι – the aorist passive infinitive of λωβέω "insult" (the quick-reference table of section 7 demoed exactly this form): "so that, after going to them, he be insulted". The subject of the passive is Anytos' own folk – the insulters are the sophists.

(5) ἐπεὶ "since" + indicative; φανερά ἐστι – the predicative plural adjective of φανερός "manifest, plain to see" (more than one subject, hence plural), a kinsman of φαίνω/ἐφάνην from section 2 today; the noun pair ἡ λώβη "disgrace, outrage", ἡ διαφθορὰ "corruption, ruin" – from διαφθείρω (gloss: corrupt); τῶν συγγιγνομένων – the present m/p participle of συγγίγνομαι (σύν + γίγνομαι, Lesson 13): "those who keep company with them" – the genitive of the object (Lesson 3).

(6) οἶδα γὰρ ἄνδρα ἕνα "I know one man" – οἶδα again, with its present meaning; Πρωταγόραν πλείω χρήματα κτησάμενον – κτησάμενον an aorist m/p participle (Lesson 13): "having acquired for himself", accusative because it stands inside the AcI frame of οἶδα; ἢ Φειδίαν – the comparison: Protagoras earned more money than even Phidias, the sculptor of the Parthenon; πλείω the comparative (met by sight, Supplementary Lesson 09b) of πολύς.

(7) The second star: ἐλάνθανεν – the imperfect of λανθάνω "escape notice" (met by sight in Lesson 12 in ἐλάνθανεν Cyrus); here + the participle διαφθείρων: "he escaped the notice of the whole of Greece while corrupting those who kept company with him" – that is, "he corrupted the whole of Greece without anyone noticing"; καὶ ἔτι εἰς τὴν ἡμέραν ταύτην εὐδοκιμῶν οὐδὲν πέπαυται – πέπαυται is the perfect m/p of παύω: "and to this very day, while enjoying repute, he has not stopped in the least". The perfect used exactly as it means: a state begun in the past and still remaining – after forty years of practice, Protagoras' reputation is intact.

(8) ἠδίκηκέ τίς σε τῶν σοφιστῶν; – ἠδίκηκεν the active perfect (preview, section 5): "has one of the sophists wronged you?"; the oath οὐδὲ μὰ Δία "by Zeus, no"; συγγέγονα – the active perfect of συγγίγνομαι: "I have never kept company with a single one of them". One sentence, two perfects, two lingering meanings: experience leaves traces – and Anytos insists he has no trace at all (never kept company, never been harmed) – yet he still "knows" what the sophists are.

(9) μάντις εἶ ἴσως "perhaps you are a seer" – ἴσως (gloss: perhaps) "perhaps"; Socrates smiles: Anytos has never kept company with them, yet can still pronounce who is good and who is bad – then only one profession can explain it: a seer. (92c continues: ἐξ ὧν αὐτὸς λέγεις θαυμάζοιμ᾽ ἄν – "from what you yourself say, I should be amazed": to know what the sophists are like without ever having met a single one of them.)

History's iron mask: the Anytos of this passage is an Athenian politician with real power. Four years later (399 BC), together with two others, he is one of the men who sign the indictment against Socrates – the charge: corrupting the youth of Athens. Plato wrote the Meno after that trial, and the readers of the day knew this fate by heart. So when Socrates asks ἠδίκηκέ τίς σε τῶν σοφιστῶν; and Anytos answers ἄπειρος εἶ παντάπασι τῶν ἀνδρῶν "you are entirely without experience of these men" – the whole dialogue plays out like a drama steeped in destiny. Anytos' own words indict him: the man who claims to "know" without experience is the very embodiment of what Socrates calls the most dangerous kind of "knowledge".

Faces planted for Lesson 17: today's reading is a museum of the verb system: the aorist passive infinitive λωβηθῆναι (new today), the perfects πέπαυται, ἠδίκηκεν, συγγέγονα (today), participles of every kind (ἐλθόντα, κτησάμενον, διαφθείρων, συγγιγνομένων – Lessons 19–20), and three optatives εἶεν, εἰκάζοιμεν, λάβοι (Lesson 17). General grammar points to remember: the passage gathers the whole verb-aspect system of the course's first half: the -τος adjective διδακτόν (C1, able to be taught); the three optative forms εἶεν, εἰκάζοιμεν (C2) and λάβοι (C4), early faces of Lesson 17; the passive aorist infinitive λωβηθῆναι (C4), today's star; the three perfects πέπαυται (C7), ἠδίκηκέ (C8) and συγγέγονα (C8), settling traces of the past into the present; and the m/p aorist participle κτησάμενον (C6), a review of Lesson 13.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. The three optative forms: εἶεν and εἰκάζοιμεν (C2), λάβοι (C4). The aorist participles: ἐλθόντα (C4, active), κτησάμενον (C6, m/p aorist, with the meaning of acquiring for oneself). The passive aorist infinitive: λωβηθῆναι (C4, the -θη- marker). The three perfects: πέπαυται (C7, m/p, has not stopped), ἠδίκηκέ (C8, active, has wronged), συγγέγονα (C8, active, have kept company). The speaker labels: Ἄνυτος at C4, then Socrates and Anytos alternating at C8 and C9.

Step 2, Meaning and structure. Socrates' conclusion lies in sentence 2, resting on the data of sentence 3: virtue is, roughly, not teachable. Where Anytos dodges: instead of answering who could teach it, he indicts a whole profession (sentences 4 to 7). The trap question in sentence 8: if he has never been wronged and never kept company with one of them, where does his knowledge come from? The punchline in sentence 9: Socrates jokes that only a seer could explain it.

Step 3, Discussion and connection. Anytos embodies knowledge without experience, held with total certainty: today we call it prejudice. And Socrates' argument-pattern remains the root of every social inference: a quality that can be taught must show teachers and learners; if none are ever found, the quality itself must be re-examined. The seer punchline reminds us: a confident claim about something one does not know says more about the claimant than about the thing.

Exercise 2 – Building the aorist passive

Given these already-augmented stems, write the 3 Sg. aorist passive and say whether it belongs to the first or the second line: ἐ-παιδευ-, ἐ-γραφ-, ἐ-τιμη-, ἐ-φαν-, ἐ-αλλαγ-.

Answers 2

ἐπαιδεύθη (I) "was educated"; ἐγράφη (II, from γράφω – the φ drops out, leaving -η-) "was written"; ἐτιμήθη (I, contract verb) "was honored"; ἐφάνη (II) "came to light"; ἠλλάγη (II) "was changed". Note: γράφω has no first aorist passive; its only passive form is the second aorist ἐγράφην (1 Sg.) / ἐγράφη (3 Sg.).

Exercise 3 – Sorting labels

Attach a label to each form: (a) first aorist passive, (b) second aorist passive, (c) passive future, (d) perfect m/p, (e) passive-form deponent: κέκληται, ἐβουλήθη, λυθήσεται, ἐφάνημεν, λέλυσθε, ἠλλάγησαν, ἐτιμήθη, πέπαυμαι.

Answers 3

(d) κέκληται, λέλυσθε, πέπαυμαι (reduplication + middle endings); (e) ἐβουλήθη (βούλομαι, "wanted"); (c) λυθήσεται; (b) ἐφάνημεν, ἠλλάγησαν (short -η-; 3 Pl. -σαν); (a) ἐτιμήθη (-θη- after the contract verb's long vowel).

Exercise 4 – Turning into the passive

Turn each sentence into the passive, bringing the agent into the frame ὑπό + genitive: (1) ὁ σοφιστὴς ἐβλάψε τὸν νέον. (2) οἱ ἄνθρωποι ἐκάλεσαν τὸν Πρωταγόραν σοφόν. (3) ἡ πόλις τιμήσει τοὺς σοφούς.

Answers 4

(1) ὁ νέος ἐβλάβη ὑπὸ τοῦ σοφιστοῦ. (2) ὁ Πρωταγόρας ὑπὸ τῶν ἀνθρώπων ἐκλήθη σοφός (the accusative σοφόν stays in place like an object complement). (3) οἱ σοφοὶ ὑπὸ τῆς πόλεως τιμηθήσονται – passive future: the aorist passive stem τιμηθη- + middle future endings.

Exercise 5 – The map of six stems

The dictionary gives the verb καλέω as: καλέω, καλῶ, ἐκάλεσα, κέκληκα, κέκλημαι, ἐκλήθην. Place each of the following forms into the right slot of the map (name the stem number): ἐκάλεσαν, κέκληται, ἐκλήθημεν, καλῶ, κεκλήμεθα? (be careful with the last one!) and give a quick translation of each form.

Answers 5

ἐκάλεσαν – stem 3 (active aorist): "they called"; κέκληται – stem 5 (perfect m/p): "he carries the name of having-been-called"; ἐκλήθημεν – stem 6 (aorist passive): "we were called"; καλῶ – stem 1 (present, contract verb): "I call". The last trap: κεκλήμεθα is a perfectly valid 1 Pl. perfect m/p – the accent standing on -κλή- is just the recessive rule: a four-syllable form pulls the accent at most back to the antepenult, exactly the shape of λελύμεθα (accent on -λύ-). Anyone who drags the accent onto the first syllable κέ is wrong.

Exercise 6 – Reading in context

Re-read sentence (7) of Reading II: Πρωταγόρας δὲ ὅλην τὴν Ἑλλάδα ἐλάνθανεν διαφθείρων τοὺς συγγιγνομένους, καὶ ἔτι εἰς τὴν ἡμέραν ταύτην εὐδοκιμῶν οὐδὲν πέπαυται. Answer: (1) why is ἐλάνθανεν an imperfect and not an aorist? (2) why does Plato choose πέπαυται (perfect) instead of the aorist ἐπαύσατο? (3) what role do the two participles διαφθείρων, εὐδοκιμῶν play for the two finite verbs?

Answers 6

(1) ἐλάνθανεν + participle is an imperfect painting the background: the "escaping notice" drags on for forty years; it is not a single closing event. (2) πέπαυται reports a state still remaining: "to this very day he has not stopped enjoying repute" – the aorist ἐπαύσατο would cut the story back into a single event closed in the past and lose the brazen persistence of Protagoras' reputation. (3) The two present active participles carry the side meanings "while corrupting / while enjoying repute" alongside the main verbs – the very "background + closing beat" division you learned in Lesson 13.
