1. δύναμαι and two kinds of verbs: thematic / athematic

1.1 Thematic and athematic verbs

A foundational note (it will stay important later on): in the Greek verb system we distinguish two kinds:

Thematic verbsAthematic verbs
Between the tense stem and the ending stands a thematic vowel: ο or ε.
Example: παιδεύ-ο-μεν (paideú-o-men) "we educate" → -ο- is the thematic vowel.		 No thematic vowel: the ending attaches directly to the stem.
Example: ἐσ-μέν (es-mén) "we are": stem ἐσ- + ending -μέν, no vowel in between.

Quick memory hook: the present middle/passive endings learned in Lesson 5 (-ομαι, -εται, -όμεθα…) are precisely the middle/passive endings plus a thematic vowel: θεραπεύ-ε-ται. The "bare" middle/passive endings are -ται, -νται, -μεν… with no vowel.

1.2 δύναμαι: present paradigm + infinitive

Consider an athematic deponent verb (deponent = middle/passive in form but active in meaning, learned in Lesson 5):

δύναμαι (dýnamai) – to be able, to have the power, to be capable.

Pattern for telling the two kinds apart in a text: compare the same person, e.g. 1 Pl.: a thematic verb shows the thematic vowel clearly, γιγν-ό-μεθα (the accent falls one syllable earlier), while an athematic verb "squeezes" the parts together, δυνά-μεθα – and the accent usually jumps onto the very vowel of the ending. The two columns in the table above differ only in that "thin letter": γιγνόμεθα ↔ δυνάμεθα, γίγνεσθε ↔ δύνασθε. Comparing the pairs one by one when reviewing is the fastest way to memorize them.

Watch out: (1) δύναμαι translates with active meaning: "I can" – never "I am being able" (recall the deponent principle of Lesson 5). (2) δύναμαι usually comes with an infinitive: δυνάμεθα λέγειν – "we can speak". This is an object infinitive, not an AcI – not every infinitive belongs to an AcI (Lesson 4): here there is no accusative acting as logical subject. (3) γίγνεσαι drops the σ before the ending: γίγνε-σαι → γίγνῃ – the 2 Sg. form ends in -ῃ.

Latin corner: athematic verbs are the "old relatives" of the Indo-European family: the ending glues directly onto the stem, with no vowel "adhesive". Latin preserves the same phenomenon: sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt conjugates exactly like the athematic εἰμί, εἶ, ἐστί, ἐσμέν, ἐστέ, εἰσί – form for form, person for person. When you meet the "strangest" verb of all, think: it is the oldest, which is why it is the shortest and most irregular.

2. Demonstrative pronouns: pointing is their nature

A demonstrative pronoun has the character of underlining and pointing: Latin demonstrare literally means "to point out". When I point at what I mean, I make it clearer – and I can point in different directions. To visualize, borrow place adverbs: I point at this one (here) or at that one (over there) (compare English this vs. that). Greek has more demonstrative pronouns than English. Today we learn two:

  • ★ ὅδε, ἥδε, τόδε (hóde, héde, tóde) – "this one (here)" | the following
  • ★ ἐκεῖνος, ἐκείνη, ἐκεῖνο (ekeînos, ekéinē, ekeîno) – "that one (over there)"

One more important demonstrative: οὗτος, αὕτη, τοῦτο – this/that not yet (Lesson 26).

3. The morphology of ὅδε, ἥδε, τόδε ("this here")

Built from two parts:

  • The first part = the form of the article – but always accented, even in the nominative (ὅδε, ἥδε, τόδε) – and this part declines fully.
  • The second part = always -δε "here", indeclinable, unchanging.

Note also that the genitive/dative plural is identical for all three genders (τῶνδε, τοῖσδε/ταῖσδε) – just like the article itself – only the context reveals the gender.

Pattern to reuse: the "pronominal declension" of the neuter is not a private rule of ἐκεῖνος: every pronoun of this type has a neuter singular ending in a long vowel -ο/-ι instead of -ον (you will meet it again in τοῦτο, τί…). An adjective (like ἀγαθός), by contrast, has ἀγαθόν. Quick self-test: meet a "bare" neuter form like ἐκεῖνο → almost certainly a pronoun, not an adjective.

5. How they are used: alone & with a noun

5.1 Standing alone (no accompanying noun)

When no noun accompanies it, a demonstrative pronoun does the work of a noun itself: it can take on any sentence function (subject, object, after a preposition…).

GreekTransliterationEnglish
διαλέγομαι τῷδε.dialégomai tôide.I am talking with this man here.

Note: διαλέγομαι (dialégomai) is a deponent verb (middle/passive in form, active in meaning) and takes the dative (answers "with whom?").

5.2 With an accompanying noun

Rule: agree in GNC with the noun pointed at (case–number–gender) + stand in the predicative position, yet translate in the attributive style; the article is not translated.

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὅδε ὁ ἐχθρός or ὁ ἐχθρὸς ὅδεhóde ho echthrós / ho echthròs hódethis enemy (the article "ὁ" is not translated!)

5.3 Reminder: attributive position and predicative position

Attributive position – the article stands directly before the attributive:

  • Possibility 1: the attributive stands between the article and the head noun: οἱ τοῦ Ἥλίου ἵπποι (hoi toû Helíou híppoi) – "the horses of Helios".
  • Possibility 2: the article of the head noun is repeated before an attributive that follows the noun: οἱ ἵπποι οἱ τοῦ Ἥλίου – "the horses of Helios". (In a small number of cases the first article may be absent.)
  • → In every possibility: the article stands directly before the attributive.

Predicative position is the "opposite face": the article does not stand directly before the word, i.e. the word is in the predicative position…

  1. either it stands before the article of the noun pointed at…
  2. …or it stands after the noun pointed at (after both article and noun).

→ That is why ὅδε ὁ ἐχθρός (before the article) and ὁ ἐχθρὸς ὅδε (after the noun) are both predicative positions of the demonstrative pronoun – but both are translated attributively: "this enemy".

Watch out – the classic error: seeing ὅδε ὁ ἐχθρός and translating "this one is the enemy" is wrong: that would be the predicative rendering. Rule: a demonstrative pronoun that accompanies a noun is always translated attributively ("this enemy"); the predicative rendering "this one is the enemy" only occurs when the pronoun has no accompanying noun and serves as the subject/predicate of the sentence. Self-test: check whether two adjacent GNC phrases stand side by side (pronoun + noun with its article) → if so, merge them into one noun phrase in your translation.

6. Deixis: near – "just mentioned" – far

Recall the nature of the matter: a demonstrative pronoun points somewhere. Deixis = the direction of the pointing finger (from δείκνυμι (deíknymi) "I point"). The direction can be outside the text (real space) or inside the text (earlier/later in the flow of words):

PronounDeixis outside the textDeixis inside the text
ὅδε, ἥδε, τόδεpoints at something present or (spatially) near from the speaker's standpointpoints at what is about to be said, the matter still to be discussed (pointing forward)
οὗτος, αὕτη, τοῦτο not yet (Lesson 26)points at something not especially near from the speaker's standpointusually (but not always) points at what has already been mentioned
ἐκεῖνος, ἐκείνη, ἐκεῖνοpoints at something (spatially) farther away from the speaker's standpointpoints at something farther back in the text (already left behind by the flow of speech)

Reading pattern: this system of three "pointing directions" works like a ruler laid along the text: ὅδε = "the following", οὗτος = "the aforementioned", ἐκεῖνος = "that one back there". In literature, an opening phrase of the type τάδε… is precisely "the following things…". Many languages have the same three grades – English "this – that – that over there": the deictic maps of languages coincide; only the words differ.

7. εἶναι: the full paradigm & the accent trap (εἶ vs εἰ)

εἶναι (to be, to exist) is the most important athematic verb – and one of the most "broken" verbs in Greek. The paradigm (the imperfect and future below are forms of εἶναι to learn in advance; these two tenses as tenses in general are not yet covered here):

  • Movable nu: ἐστί(ν), εἰσί(ν) take the ν when the next word begins with a vowel or at a pause at the end of a sentence.
  • Two "print styles": modern editions print εἰμί, ἐσμέν, ἐστέ; older printings (and some traditional grammars) write εἶμι, ἐσμεν, ἐστε – only the accent differs; it is the same word.
  • The 2 Sg. imperative ἴσθι is completely irregular (must be memorized); the 2 Pl. imperative ἔστε is quite "regular". The 3rd person imperative is not yet.

Watch out – risk of confusion, look at the accent:

  • εἶ (with acute) = you are – a verb.
  • εἰ (no accent) = if / whether – a conjunction.

One accent mark changes the word class! In print, if the accent is "swallowed", you must judge by grammar: εἰ φίλος εἶ, … – "If you are a friend…": εἰ cannot be the verb because the clause would then lack its predicate… so εἶ is the predicate.

Historical connection: εἰμί, εἶ, ἐστί(ν) shares its root with Latin sum, es, est, German bin, bist, ist, English am, is, Sanskrit asmi, asi, asti – all from the Indo-European root *h₁es- "to exist". That is why the "strangest" paradigm is also the oldest – and that is why its forms are so short that a single accent mark has to carry the whole burden of telling the words apart.

8. Reading: The father in the hut

Source: theme based on Homer, Odyssey 16,181–206 – the passage where Odysseus, still disguised as a stranger in the hut of the swineherd Eumaeus, reveals his identity to his son Telemachus. The original (public domain) can be read on Perseus. The version below was rewritten by this project for the level of Lesson 6 – it uses only structures already learned, keeping the spirit of the original.

Simplified version (composed by this project):

ἐν τῇ καλύβῃ ὁ Τηλέμαχος μετὰ τοῦ ξένου ἐστίν. ὁ δὲ ξένος δύναται τὴν ἀλήθειαν λέγειν. εἰ σοφὸς εἶ, ὦ Τήλεμαχε, δύνασαι τὸν πατέρα γιγνώσκειν. ὁ δὲ Τηλέμαχος τόνδε τὸν ξένον γιγνώσκει· ἐκεῖνος γὰρ Ὀδυσσεὺς ἐστιν.

en têi kalýbēi ho Telémakhos metà toû xénou estín. ho dè xénos dýnatai tḕn alḗtheian légein. ei sophòs eî, ô Telémakhe, dýnasai tòn patéra gignṓskein. ho dè Telémakhos tónde tòn xénon gignṓskei· ekeînos gàr Odysseùs estin.

Translation: In the hut, Telemachus is with the stranger. And the stranger can tell the truth. If you are wise, Telemachus, you can recognize your father. And Telemachus recognizes this stranger – for that man yonder is Odysseus.

Quick analysis (check with the tools already learned):

  • ἐν τῇ καλύβῃ – the article τῇ marks the feminine singular dative; μετὰ τοῦ ξένου – μετὰ + genitive (Lesson 4). Sentences 1 and 2 open with an implicit μέν…δέ pair (δέ in sentence 2, preparing a contrast).
  • δύναται λέγειν – δύναμαι + object infinitive (not an AcI!); the accent stands on the syllable δύ- in the recessive style of an athematic verb.
  • εἰ σοφὸς εἶ – exactly this lesson's trap pair: εἰ (unaccented) = "if", εἶ (acute) = "you are"; σοφός in predicative position with an invisible subject (Lesson 3).
  • τόνδε τὸν ξένον – the demonstrative pronoun in the accusative (the article τόνδε is the giveaway) + the noun pointed at with its article τὸν: predicative position but translated attributively – "this stranger".
  • ἐκεῖνος γὰρ Ὀδυσσεὺς ἐστιν – ἐκεῖνος stands alone (no accompanying noun) → serves as subject itself; γάρ postpositive; Ὀδυσσεύς is a nominative predicate. Translate "that man yonder is Odysseus" – the spatial deixis (the disguised man) matches the "far/other-person" pointing direction.

Reading comprehension questions: (1) In which case does τόνδε τὸν ξένον stand, and what is the evidence? Why is the translation "this stranger" and not "the stranger is this one"? (2) Sentence 3 contains two words differing by one accent mark – which is the verb, which is the conjunction? (3) Read the original Homer (Odyssey 16.181–206 on Perseus): how does Odysseus reveal his identity there, and how has the simplified version "packaged" it?

Answers to the reading questions (print to check)

1. Accusative – the article τόνδε (and τὸν) gives it away; it is the object of γιγνώσκει. Because the demonstrative pronoun accompanies the noun pointed at (two adjacent GNC phrases) → always translated attributively: "this stranger"; the predicative rendering "the stranger is this one" occurs only when the pronoun stands alone. 2. εἰ (unaccented) = "if" – conjunction; εἶ (acute) = "you are" – verb (predicate of the first clause). 3. In Homer, Athena transforms Odysseus back into his younger self, and Odysseus then reveals his identity to Telemachus in tears; the simplified version packages it as "the stranger can tell the truth – if you are wise, you recognize your father" – the same recognition-of-the-father motif, built with exactly the tools of Lesson 6.

11. Exercises

Answers appear only when printing (Ctrl+P → Save as PDF) – work through them first, then print to check.

Exercise 1 – Classifying and conjugating

  1. Analyze παιδεύομεν and ἐσμέν into stem – thematic vowel – ending (if present).
  2. Fill in the missing forms of δύναμαι: 2 Sg. ___, 1 Pl. ___, 3 Pl. ___, infinitive ___.
  3. Translate: δυνάμεθα λέγειν.

Answer 1

  1. παιδεύ-ο-μεν (thematic: thematic vowel -ο-); ἐσ-μέν (athematic: the ending glues directly onto the stem ἐσ-).
  2. δύνασαι · δυνάμεθα · δύνανται · δύνασθαι
  3. "We can speak." – object infinitive, not an AcI.

Exercise 2 – Declining the demonstrative pronouns by GNC

Write the correct form of the demonstrative pronoun (with the article of the noun if needed):

  1. "this enemy (here)" – masculine singular nominative: ___
  2. "this enemy" – masculine singular accusative: ___
  3. "with these horses here" – masculine plural dative: ___
  4. "that philosopher (over there)" – masculine singular nominative: ___
  5. "with that (female) friend over there" – feminine singular dative of ἐκεῖνος: ___
  6. "that thing (neuter) over there" – neuter singular accusative of ἐκεῖνος: ___ (careful!)

Answer 2

  1. ὅδε ὁ ἐχθρός (or: ὁ ἐχθρὸς ὅδε) – "this enemy"
  2. τόνδε τὸν ἐχθρόν – "this enemy" (accusative)
  3. τοῖσδε τοῖς ἵπποις – "with these horses here" (remember: the dative plural inserts σ!)
  4. ἐκεῖνος ὁ φιλόσοφος (or: ὁ φιλόσοφος ἐκεῖνος) – "that philosopher"
  5. ἐκείνῃ (τῇ φίλῃ) – "with that (female) friend"
  6. ἐκεῖνο – pronominal declension: not ἐκεῖνον!

Exercise 3 – Position and translation

Translate (watch the position: predicative position but translated attributively; the article is not translated):

  1. ὅδε ὁ ἵππος τοῦ Ἥλίου ἐστίν.
  2. ὁ ἐχθρὸς ὅδε φίλος γίγνεται.
  3. διαλέγεσθε τῷδε;
  4. ἐκεῖνοι οἱ ἵπποι ταχείς εἰσί. (ταχείς = plural of ταχύς "fast", Lesson 3: ταχέως)

Answer 3

  1. "This horse (here) belongs to Helios." – ὅδε ὁ ἵππος = "this horse" (translated attributively although it stands before the article); τοῦ Ἥλίου = genitive attribute.
  2. "This enemy here becomes a friend." – γίγνεται: deponent, translate actively; φίλος is a nominative predicate.
  3. "Are you (pl.) talking with this man (here)?" – διαλέγομαι: deponent + dative.
  4. "Those horses (over there) are fast." – ταχείς in predicative position (the article does not stand directly before it); εἰσί = 3 Pl. of εἶναι.

Exercise 4 – Deixis in a text

In the following situations, should you use ὅδε or ἐκεῖνος? Why?

  1. The speaker points at the book he is holding in his hand.
  2. The speaker opens a lecture: "In what follows I will discuss…" (τάδε…)
  3. In a text, referring back to a character mentioned many sentences earlier.

Answer 4

  1. ὅδε – present, near from the speaker's standpoint (deixis outside the text).
  2. ὅδε (τάδε) – deixis inside the text, pointing toward "what is about to be discussed".
  3. ἐκεῖνος – deixis inside the text, pointing at something "far" back in the narrative. (Some contexts also allow οὗτος – "the just mentioned" – but that pronoun is not yet (Lesson 26).)

Exercise 5 – εἶναι and the accent trap

  1. Fill in the present paradigm of εἶναι: 1 Sg. ___, 2 Sg. ___, 3 Sg. ___, 1 Pl. ___, 2 Pl. ___, 3 Pl. ___.
  2. Translate and explain why that reading is the only possible one: εἰ φίλος εἶ, λέγε.
  3. Translate: ἔσται τὸ ἀγαθόν. (τὸ ἀγαθόν = substantivized adjective, Lesson 5)

Answer 5

  1. εἰμί · εἶ · ἐστί(ν) · ἐσμέν · ἐστέ · εἰσί(ν)
  2. "If you are a friend, speak!" – εἰ (unaccented) = "if"; εἶ (acute) = "you are". The first clause lacks a predicate → εἰ must open the conditional clause.
  3. "The good will be (there)." – ἔσται = 3 Sg. future of εἶναι; τὸ ἀγαθόν = substantivized adjective (Lesson 5).

Exercise 6 – Putting it all together (Lessons 4–6)

Translate into Greek:

  1. "We can talk with this enemy."
  2. "That man over there is a philosopher." – use ἐκεῖνος standing alone, no noun needed.
  3. "If you (pl.) are wise, make peace!" (εἰρήνη = peace; ποιεῖσθε = plural imperative of ποιέομαι "make", Lesson 5)

Answer 6

  1. δυνάμεθα διαλέγεσθαι τῷδε τῷ ἐχθρῷ. – or τῷ ἐχθρῷ τῷδε. Combination: athematic deponent δυνάμεθα + object infinitive διαλέγεσθαι + deponent διαλέγομαι taking the dative + demonstrative pronoun in the dative (predicative position, translated attributively).
  2. ἐκεῖνος φιλόσοφος ἐστίν. – demonstrative pronoun without an accompanying noun → serves as subject itself; φιλόσοφος is a nominative predicate; ἐστίν takes the movable nu before a vowel.
  3. εἰ σοφοὶ ἐστέ, εἰρήνην ποιεῖσθε! – εἰ = "if" (unaccented!); σοφοί plural predicate; ἐστέ = 2 Pl. of εἶναι; ποιεῖσθε = plural middle imperative (Lesson 5: "make for yourselves").
