What you will be able to do after this lesson: (1) recognize a deliberate gap (ellipsis) – usually a form of εἶναι or a predicate taken from the previous sentence – and "re-inflate" it in your translation; (2) identify a nominal sentence by the sign "subject + predicative noun but no verb"; (3) apply the rule "neuter plural subject → the verb stays singular" correctly in both directions of translation; (4) tell adjectives (declinable) from adverbs (indeclinable) and derive adverbs from adjectives with the ending -ος → -ως.

How to use this lesson: this lesson is shorter than Lessons 1–2 but it is a "text-reading lesson": from here on you may no longer expect every sentence to have all its parts. Vocabulary marked with ★ is compulsory memorization; the words of the reading have their own gloss table.

1. Ellipsis: a deliberate gap

1.1 Warm-up: what is missing in this sentence?

Look carefully at the following Greek sentence and answer: what is unusual?

Τὸ τῶν ἄστρων θερμὸν οὐκ αἰσθητόν, …

Tò tôn ástrōn thermòn ouk aisthētón…

→ The verb is missing! The sentence has a subject, an attribute, a predicative noun… but no verb at all.

1.2 Definition

Ellipsis = the deliberate omission of a word or phrase – but that word or phrase can be inferred from the context and "re-inflated", that is, from the same sentence or from the sentence(s) before.

The most common case: the verb is left out. When translating, you must supply:

  1. … (usually) a fitting form of εἶναι (eînai), or
  2. … or a predicate taken from the sentence before.

2. The nominal sentence: no linking verb

The linking verb (copula) – usually ἐστί(ν) or εἰσί(ν) – is frequently absent in Greek, far more often than in Latin or in English. A sentence of this type is called a nominal sentence.

How to recognize it: the sentence has no verb and has a subject + predicative noun (review Lesson 1, section 8), or just the predicative noun alone.

Greek (as in the text)TransliterationSupply when translatingEnglish
Τὸ τῶν ἄστρων θερμὸν οὐκ αἰσθητόνtò tôn ástrōn thermòn ouk aisthētón… ἐστί(ν)The warmth of the stars is not perceptible.

Anatomy of the model sentence:

  • Τὸ τῶν ἄστρων θερμόν (tò tôn ástrōn thermón) = subject: a substantivized adjective τὸ θερμόν ("the hot thing", the warmth) + genitive attribute τῶν ἄστρων ("of the stars" – review Lesson 1). Substantivized adjectives are systematized in Lesson 5 (not yet); for this lesson, learn τὸ θερμόν as a vocabulary item.
  • οὐκ αἰσθητόν (ouk aisthētón) = predicative adjective, with the negation οὐκ before a vowel (review Lesson 2, section 5).
  • ἐστί(ν) (estín) = the linking verb, left out → you must supply it yourself when translating.

Latin corner: Latin also has verbless nominal sentences, especially in sayings: ars longa, vita brevis – "art (is) long, life (is) short" – without a single est! The difference is only one of frequency: Greek drops the linking verb far more often, above all in definitions, maxims, inscriptions and verdicts.

Watch out: ellipsis is not a broken sentence – it is a deliberate gap, and it is your job as reader to "re-inflate" the missing part in the translation; the Greek text itself stays as it is. Two common mistakes: (1) translating every word correctly but leaving your English sentence "broken" without a verb (English absolutely requires the "is"); (2) the reverse – seeing a verbless Greek sentence and suspecting you skipped a word. Self-test: underline all sentence parts – subject + predicative noun but no verb → it is a nominal sentence; add "is/are" in your translation.

3. Neuter plural subject → singular predicate

3.1 The problem

Consider the following sentence:

τὰ ἄστρα ἐστιν ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ.

Tà ástra estín en tōi ouranṓi.

The stars are in the sky.

Stating the problem: τὰ ἄστρα (tà ástra) is a neuter plural, yet the verb ἐστίν stands in the singular – violating the subject–predicate agreement you would expect (agreement in both person and number). You "should" expect εἰσίν.

3.2 The rule and its reason

Rule: when the subject is a neuter plural, the predicate verb – as a general rule – stands in the singular.

Why? The neuter plural in Greek is felt as a collective concept: τὰ ἄστρα is conceived as "the starry sky" – one mass, one picture, not a count of individual stars.

Consequences for translation, in both directions:

  • Greek → English: τὰ ἄστρα ἐστίν translates as "the stars are" – English requires a plural predicate with a plural subject. Do not let yourself be "dragged along" by the number of the Greek verb!
  • English → Greek: "the stars are in the sky" must be written τὰ ἄστρα ἐστίν ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ – do not "correct" it to εἰσίν just because English uses a plural predicate.

Pattern to reuse: this rule is reserved for neuters only. Masculine and feminine nouns (when you learn them (later lessons)) behave normally: οἱ ἄνθρωποι εἰσί(ν) (hoi ánthrōpoi eisín) – "the humans are" (plural). Three-step self-test: (1) is the subject plural? (2) which gender? (3) neuter → verb in the singular; any other gender → plural verb as usual.

Historical connection: this "number mismatch" is a heritage of Proto-Indo-European: the neuter plural was originally a collective number that took a singular predicate – traces survive in Hittite (the oldest documented Indo-European language) and occasionally in archaic Latin (inscriptions, early poetry: neuter plural subjects with singular verbs). Greek kept this custom longer and more consistently than its "siblings" – a nice example of "the older, the stranger".

4. Adverbs: derived from adjectives, indeclinable

4.1 Adjective vs adverb

You already know two classes of modifying words:

  • Adjectives modify nouns → they must decline, agreeing with the noun in gender–case–number (Lesson 1, section 8): ὁ ἵππος ταχύς ἐστίν – "the horse is fast".
  • Adverbs modify verbs/adjectives/sentences → indeclinable, agreeing with nothing: ὁ φιλόσοφος ἀγαθῶς διδάσκει (ho philósophos agathôs didáskei) – "the philosopher teaches well".

4.2 The rule for deriving adverbs from adjectives

AdjectiveTransliterationAdverbTransliterationEnglish
ἀγαθόςagathósἀγαθῶςagathôsgood → well
σοφόςsophósσοφῶςsophôswise → wisely
θερμόςthermósθερμῶςthermôshot → hotly
ταχύςtachýsταχέωςtachéōsfast → quickly (exceptional ending, learn as-is)

Rule: an adjective in -ος → an adverb in -ως. Same "keyboard" – only the final syllable changes: -ός (adjective) ↔ -ῶς (adverb).

Watch out: do not use an adverb where an adjective belongs, or vice versa. ὁ ἵππος ταχύς ἐστίν is right ("the horse is fast" – ταχύς is a predicative adjective agreeing with the subject); ὁ ἵππος ταχέως ἐστίν is wrong ("the horse is quickly" – meaningless). Self-test: what does the modifier say about the verb? If it expresses how the action happens → adverb (indeclinable); if it describes or predicated something about a person/thing → adjective (declining by gender–case–number).

Latin corner: Latin derives adverbs from o-adjectives in exactly the same way: bonus → bene, malus → male, fortis → fortiter. Greek: ἀγαθός → ἀγαθῶς ↔ Latin: bonus → bene – the same idea of "using the adjective's stem to describe how an action happens".

5. Reading: the stars of Anaxagoras (simplified)

Source: theme based on Anaxagoras (DK 59A42 – the theory that "the stars are masses of fire very far away, so their heat is not perceptible") and Aristotle, Magna Moralia 1,1. The originals (public domain) can be read on Perseus / packhum. The version below was rewritten by this project for the level of Lesson 3 – it uses only structures and vocabulary already learned.

Simplified version (composed by this project):

τὰ ἄστρα λαμπρὰ ἐστίν. τὸ δὲ τῶν ἄστρων θερμὸν οὐκ αἰσθητόν· τὰ γὰρ ἄστρα μακρὰ ἐστίν. τὸ δὲ φῶς τῶν ἄστρων αἰσθητόν ἐστίν.

Tà ástra lamprá estín. Tò dè tôn ástrōn thermòn ouk aisthētón· tà gàr ástra makrà estín. Tò dè phôs tôn ástrōn aisthētón estín.

English (close translation)
The stars are bright. But the warmth of the stars is not perceptible; for the stars are far away. The light of the stars, however, is perceptible.

Quick analysis (check with the tools you have learned):

  • τὰ ἄστρα λαμπρὰ ἐστίν: neuter plural subject + singular verb (section 3); λαμπρά is a plural predicative noun – the "singular rule" applies to the verb only; the adjective still agrees with the subject in gender–case–number.
  • τὸ τῶν ἄστρων θερμόν: substantivized adjective + genitive attribute (review Lesson 1); οὐκ before a vowel (Lesson 2).
  • τὰ γὰρ ἄστρα μακρὰ ἐστίν: γάρ postpositive (second slot – Lesson 1); once again a neuter plural → singular verb.
  • τὸ δὲ φῶς: δέ postpositive; τὸ φῶς a neuter noun + the genitive attribute τῶν ἄστρων.

Reading comprehension questions: (1) Which two verbs in the reading go with the subject τὰ ἄστρα, in which number – and why? (2) What are τὸ θερμόν and τὸ αἰσθητόν – nouns or adjectives? Why do they carry an article? (3) Read the related original sources: according to the transmitted sources, what did Anaxagoras think the stars are, and how does that explain the "far away → heat not perceptible" argument in the simplified version?

Answers to the reading questions (print to check)

1. ἐστίν and ἐστίν – both singular, because the subject τὰ ἄστρα is a neuter plural (section 3). 2. They are substantivized adjectives (adjective + article → used as a noun): "the hot thing", "the perceptible thing" – systematized in Lesson 5 (not yet). 3. According to the transmitted sources, Anaxagoras held that the sun and the stars are masses of fiery stone very far away; because of the distance, their heat cannot be felt – the simplified version keeps exactly the argument frame "bright + far → light visible, heat not felt", compressed into three nominal sentences.

8. Exercises

Answers appear only when printing (Ctrl+P → Save as PDF) – work through them first, then print to check.

Exercise 1 – Recognizing ellipsis and supplying it

The following are nominal sentences. Supply the missing part (in Greek) and translate:

  1. ὁ ἵππος ταχύς.
  2. ὁ βασιλεὺς οὐ σοφός.
  3. τὸ θερμὸν αἰσθητόν.
  4. τὰ ἀγαθὰ οὐ κακά.

Answer 1

  1. Supply ἐστί(ν). – The horse is fast. (ταχύς is a predicative adjective agreeing with the masculine singular subject)
  2. Supply ἐστί(ν). – The king is not wise. (οὐ before the consonant σ)
  3. Supply ἐστί(ν). – The hot thing is perceptible.
  4. Supply ἐστί(ν) – not εἰσί! – because the subject τὰ ἀγαθά is a neuter plural (section 3). – Good things are not bad. (τὰ ἀγαθά, κακά = substantivized adjectives, agreeing with each other in the neuter plural)

Exercise 2 – Ellipsis borrowing a predicate from the previous sentence

Translate the following pair of clauses, noting what the second clause leaves out:

ὁ φιλόσοφος ἀγαθός ἐστίν· ὁ ἐχθρὸς οὐ.

Answer 2

"The philosopher is good; and the enemy is not (not good)."

  • The second clause omits the whole phrase "ἀγαθός ἐστίν" → borrow the predicate from the first clause; English requires "is not" (the enemy is not) – it cannot stay empty.
  • ὁ ἐχθρός = the enemy (see the vocabulary table); no δέ appears – here the clauses are separated by a semicolon.

Exercise 3 – Getting the number right: ἐστίν or εἰσί?

Choose the correct form of εἶναι and explain:

  1. τὰ ἄστρα ___ ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ.
  2. τὰ ἀγαθὰ ___ ἐν τοῖς ἀνθρώποις.
  3. οἱ ἵπποι ___ ταχεῖς.
  4. οἱ φίλοι ___ σοφοί.
  5. τὰ ἄστρα ___ λαμπρά.

Answer 3

  1. ἐστί(ν) – neuter plural → singular verb. – The stars are in the sky.
  2. ἐστί(ν) – as above. – Good things are among the humans.
  3. εἰσί(ν) – masculine plural → agreement as usual. – The horses are fast.
  4. εἰσί(ν) – masculine plural → normal. – The friends are wise.
  5. ἐστί(ν) – neuter plural → singular. Trap: the predicative adjective λαμπρά stays plural (agreeing with the subject) – only the verb drops to the singular!

Exercise 4 – Adjective or adverb?

Choose the correct form (adjective or adverb) and explain why:

  1. ὁ φιλόσοφος ___ διδάσκει. (ἀγαθός / ἀγαθῶς)
  2. ὁ ἵππος ___ ἐστίν. (ταχύς / ταχέως)
  3. οἱ ἵπποι ___ εἰσί(ν). (ταχεῖς / ταχέως)
  4. τὸ φῶς ___ ἐστίν. (λαμπρόν / λαμπρῶς)

Answer 4

  1. ἀγαθῶς – an indeclinable adverb describing how the "teaching" happens: the philosopher teaches well.
  2. ταχύς – a predicative adjective agreeing with the masculine singular subject: the horse is fast.
  3. ταχεῖς – a predicative adjective agreeing with the masculine plural: the horses are fast (the adverb ταχέως would be meaningless here: "the horses are quickly").
  4. λαμπρόν – a predicative adjective agreeing with the neuter singular: the light is bright.

The pattern: standing next to an action verb (διδάσκει) → usually an adverb; standing next to εἶναι as a predicate → an adjective agreeing in gender–case–number.

Exercise 5 – Translating into Greek

  1. "The warmth of the stars is not perceptible."
  2. "The stars are in the sky."
  3. "The horses are fast."
  4. "Good things are wise." – choose a fitting neuter plural subject yourself (hint: τὰ ἀγαθά)

Answer 5

  1. Τὸ τῶν ἄστρων θερμὸν οὐκ αἰσθητόν (ἐστίν). (Tò tôn ástrōn thermòn ouk aisthētón estín.) – exactly the theme sentence that opened the lesson; the linking verb may be omitted in Greek but must appear in the English translation ("is").
  2. τὰ ἄστρα ἐστιν ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ. – ἐστί(ν), do not use εἰσί even though English says "the stars are" (plural)!
  3. οἱ ἵπποι ταχεῖς εἰσί(ν). – ἵππος is masculine → plural verb as usual.
  4. Suggestion: τὰ ἀγαθὰ σοφὰ ἐστί(ν) – a neuter plural subject → a singular verb despite the plural noun. (Any other neuter plural noun works too, e.g. τὰ φῶτα "the lights": τὰ φῶτα λαμπρὰ ἐστί(ν).)

Exercise 6 – Putting it all together

Read and translate the following sentence, supplying the missing parts yourself and labeling the grammatical phenomena:

τὰ τῶν ἄστρων φῶτα λαμπρά, τὸ δὲ τῶν ἄστρων θερμὸν οὐκ αἰσθητόν.

Answer 6

"The lights of the stars (are) bright, and the warmth of the stars is not perceptible." – English absolutely requires "is/are": The lights of the stars are bright; the warmth of the stars, however, is not perceptible.

  • τὰ τῶν ἄστρων φῶτα: a neuter plural subject + a genitive attribute (Lesson 1).
  • λαμπρά: a plural predicative noun agreeing with the subject; the linking verb is omitted (sections 1–2) – when supplied it must be ἐστίν in the singular (section 3: neuter plural subject).
  • τὸ δὲ τῶν ἄστρων θερμόν: δέ postpositive; substantivized adjective + genitive attribute; οὐκ before the vowel ἀ- (Lesson 2).
  • The second clause is also a nominal sentence – a two-clause passage without a single verb, and the reader still understands everything.

(Four building blocks of Lesson 3 in one sentence: ellipsis ×2 + the neuter plural rule + genitive attribute + the negation οὐκ.)
