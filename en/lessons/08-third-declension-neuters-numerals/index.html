What you will be able to do after this lesson: (1) recognize and decline vowel-stem third-declension nouns such as βασιλεύς, ἰχθύς, ἄστυ, γένος using the genitive key; (2) read correctly every third-declension noun you have met so far, thanks to the complete ending map; (3) count, read and use in sentences every numeral from 1 to 999, knowing which of them decline and which stand still.

1. The third declension II: vowel stems – the concept

Lesson 7 gave you the golden rule of the third declension: the nominative covers the stem, the genitive opens it. Take a familiar example: ὁ ῥήτωρ "orator" – looking at the nominative, you see no stem at all; open it with the genitive τοῦ ῥήτορος and the bare stem appears: ῥήτορ-. Just join stem + ending and the whole paradigm is yours.

Lesson 7 was all consonant stems: σώματ-, ποιμέν-, ῥήτορ-, πόλι-… Today we move to the remaining group: vowel stems – stems that end in a vowel. A first look at the four representatives of this lesson:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ βασιλεύς, τοῦ βασιλέωςbasileús, basiléōsking
ὁ ἰχθύς, τοῦ ἰχθύοςichthýs, ichthýosfish
τό ἄστυ, τοῦ ἄστεοςásty, ásteostown, city
τό γένος, τοῦ γένουςgénos, génouskind, race; gender

Open the stem with the genitive and you see the vowel standing at the end of the stem: βασιλε(ϝ)- (vowel -ε-), ἰχθύ- (-υ-), ἀστυ- (-υ-), γενεσ- (-ε- + the consonant s). Why does that matter? Because the final vowel of the stem reacts with the ending: it can contract, slide away, or drop its final s. That is why the nominative of this group looks "stranger" than the consonant group: βασιλεύς is really the stem βασιλευ- + the nominative ending -ς.

The biography of a letter: digamma. The ancestor of βασιλεύς is βασιλεϝ-, with ϝ = digamma, the /w/ sound that ancient Greek possessed but later Attic writing dropped entirely. When digamma disappeared it left "scars" in the morphology: the nominative βασιλεύς (υ standing in for ϝ+ς), and Homer still "feels" the digamma when scanning his verses. You don't need to memorize the details – just know why -εύς doesn't look like an -ε- stem: a line of history flows underneath the paradigm.

A pattern to reuse forever: when you meet an unfamiliar noun, don't guess from the nominative – ask the genitive. ὁ βασιλεύς hides the stem, but τοῦ βασιλέως declares everything: stem βασιλε-, vowel-stem group, and the whole paradigm can be built in your head. That is why dictionaries always list two forms: nominative + genitive.

2. The -εύς pattern: βασιλεύς

The first representative: ὁ βασιλεύς "king" – one of the most important nouns in all of Greek literature (from Homer to the Bible: βασιλεὺς τῶν Ἰουδαίων "King of the Jews"). The full paradigm, built by exactly the Lesson 7 method: bare stem βασιλε- + endings:

SingularTransliterationPluralTransliteration
Nom.βασιλεύςbasileúsβασιλεῖςbasileîs
Gen.βασιλέωςbasiléōsβασιλέωνbasiléōn
Dat.βασιλεῖbasilêiβασιλεῦσι(ν)basileûsin
Acc.βασιλέαbasiléaβασιλέαςbasiléas
Voc.βασιλεῦbasileûβασιλεῖςbasileîs

Read the table with morphological eyes: (1) the nominative singular -εύς and the vocative -εῦ are two faces of the same stem – the nominative ending -ς is no longer visible but "was once there"; (2) the accusative singular -έα has two syllables, with the stem vowel ε fully intact; (3) the dative plural -εῦσι(ν) is the stem βασιλευ- + the ending -σι(ν) – precisely the dative plural ending of the entire third declension (Lesson 7: σώμασι(ν)).

Latin corner: "king" in Latin is rēx, rēgis – also third declension, also hiding the stem in the nominative, also needing the genitive to open the stem rēg-. Greek βασιλεύς, βασιλέως and Latin rēx, rēgis are two exercises with the same prompt: "the most important nouns are usually the ones that look most irregular from the nominative". Both languages answer with the same strategy: ask the genitive.

Watch out: don't confuse βασιλέα (accusative singular, two syllables, accent on the final syllable) with βασιλεῖς (nominative/vocative plural, one syllable with the circumflex on ει). Self-check: read aloud with the transliteration – basilé-a (2 beats) vs ba-si-leîs (long, circumflex at the end). The ear catches what the eye passes over; and in translation, one is "seeing the king" (object), the other "the kings" (subject) – one wrong letter and the whole sentence goes wrong with it.

3. The -υς pattern: ἰχθύς – a relative of πόλις

The second representative: ὁ ἰχθύς "fish" – stem ἰχθυ- ending in -υ-. It is a sibling of Lesson 7's πόλις: both belong to the "i/υ family" of the third declension, and they decline almost line for line alike:

SingularTransliterationPluralTransliteration
Nom.ἰχθύςichthýsἰχθύεςichthýes
Gen.ἰχθύοςichthýosἰχθύωνichthýōn
Dat.ἰχθύϊichthý-iἰχθύσι(ν)ichthýsin
Acc.ἰχθύνichthýnἰχθύαςichthýas
Voc.ἰχθύichthýἰχθύεςichthýes

Compare with πόλις, πόλεως (Lesson 7): the plurals of the two words nearly coincide line by line (πόλεις/ἰχθύες, πόλεων/ἰχθύων, πόλεσι(ν)/ἰχθύσι(ν)), differing only in the accusative plural: πόλεις vs ἰχθύας. The most important shared point: the stem vowel i/υ never falls out of the paradigm – it stands there in every case except one: the nominative singular, where + ς produces -ις/-ύς.

Watch out: the dative singular ἰχθύϊ has three syllables (ich-thý-i), because the dative ending -ι attaches to the stem vowel υ – two vowels standing side by side without merging (unlike Lesson 7's πόλει, where ε + ι meet and the ι slides underneath as an iota subscript: πόλει). Memory hook: υ never "swallows" an ending – the dative ending appears in full after it, and writers put a diaeresis on the ι to remind you: ἰχθύϊ is read in two beats, not as "yi".

4. Neuters: ἄστυ (-υ stem) and γένος (s stem)

The remaining two representatives are both neuter: τό ἄστυ "town" and τό γένος "race, kind" (exactly this word – it is also the word for grammatical "gender"!). Neuters in the third declension operate by the same laws you already met with σῶμα (Lesson 7) and τὰ ζῷα (Lesson 3):

Neuter ruleIn the third declensionExample
Nominative = accusative, everywhereno distinct ending for these two casesτό γένος = τὸ γένος
Plural ends in (short) -αthe same form for nominative + accusativeτὰ γένη, τὰ ἄστεα
Neuter plural subject → singular verbas in every declensionτὰ ἄστη ἐστίν

Gen.ἄστεοςásteosγένουςgénous
Dat.ἄστειásteiγένειgénei
Acc.ἄστυástyγένοςgénos
Voc.ἄστυástyγένοςgénos
ἄστυ (plural)Transliterationγένος (plural)Transliteration
Nom.ἄστεαásteaγένηgénē
Gen.ἄστεωνastéōnγένωνgénōn
Dat.ἄστεσι(ν)ástesinγένεσι(ν)génesin
Acc.ἄστεαásteaγένηgénē

Open the stems as usual: the genitive τοῦ ἄστεος gives the stem ἀστυ-; τοῦ γένους gives γενεσ- – an s stem! In γένος the final stem s drops without a trace in the cases that take no s ending (nom./acc./voc.: γένος = γενεσ- minus s), but it leaves footprints where it "met" a vowel: γέν-ους (ε+ο contracting to ου), γέν-ει (ε+ει contracting to ει). This is exactly the phenomenon you met in Lesson 7 with τὸ σῶμα, τοῦ σώματος… and you will keep meeting it: s between vowels either disappears or changes the vowel – it never stands upright.

Watch out: τό γένος looks exactly like an o-declension noun: ὁ τρόπος, τοῦ τρόπου "manner" also ends in -ος! Distinguish them with exactly one test: the genitive. γένους (the vowel ou, no o-declension -ου ending) = third declension; τρόπου (genitive -ου, the standard o-declension ending) = o-declension. In a real text the article betrays it at once: τό/τοῦ won't decide it (a neuter γένος takes the neuter article), but the genitive endings of the two groups run on different tracks.

5. The complete third-declension ending map + a reading strategy

You now have both halves of the third declension (Lesson 7: consonant stems; Lesson 8: vowel stems). Here is the complete ending map – valid for every third-declension noun, whatever its stem:

CaseSingular (m./f.)Singular (neuter)Plural (m./f.)Plural (neuter)
Nom.-ς or a special shape*(no ending)-ες-α
Gen.-ος-ος-ων-ων
Dat.-ι-ι-σι(ν)-σι(ν)
Acc.-ᾰ / -ν(no ending)-ᾰς / -ας-α
Voc.= Nom. (or bare stem)= Nom.= Nom.= Nom.

*The nominative singular is the "free slot" of the third declension: the only ending is -ς (or nothing at all in the neuter), so the stem itself decides the final shape: ῥήτωρ (bare stem + nothing), ποιμήν (stem + movable ν), βασιλεύς (vowel stem + ς). That is why dozens of different "looks" share just one ending system behind them.

The reading strategy, updated (extended from Lesson 7):

  1. The article rules first – ὁ/ἡ/τό tells you the gender + case + number of the phrase, even for a noun you have never seen before.
  2. The genitive in the dictionary entry – the little line -εως after βασιλεύς is precisely the stem key.
  3. Neuter: nominative = accusative – with τό + an endingless word, don't go hunting for an "accusative ending": it doesn't exist.
  4. Dative plural -σι(ν) – with its movable ν: βασιλεῦσι before a consonant, βασιλεῦσιν before a vowel or at the end of a clause.

6. Numerals 1–10: εἷς fully declined

The lesson's second topic: cardinal numerals – "one, two, three…". The good news: like Vietnamese, Greek numerals are built systematically, so you only need a few bricks. The news to note: some of them decline like nouns, others stand still – and the "who declines, who doesn't" table in section 9 is the key.

Watch out: masculine εἷς carries the rough breathing: pronounce an initial "h" – heîs, with /h/; while neuter ἕν carries the smooth breathing – hén, no /h/. The same word "one", two different breathings! Memory hook: the masculine "roughens" (rough breathing: with /h/, as ἱ = hi, ἡ = hē), the neuter "behaves" (smooth breathing: no /h/). Writing the wrong breathing mark is a spelling error in the very first line of a text.

Latin corner – an Indo-European museum: put the numbers 5–10 of the two languages side by side and you see 6000 years of shared history: πέντε/quīnque, ἕξ/sĕx, ἑπτά/septem, ὀκτώ/octō, ἐννέα/novem, δέκα/decem. Both sets "stand still" as indeclinable cardinals. And the number 1 is family too: ἑνός ~ ūnus. When you learn Latin vocabulary later, this number series will be your first example of the "cousinship" of the two branches.

7. Numerals 11–99

The next bricks – and all the logic is exactly like Vietnamese "mười ba" = ten + three:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ἑνδέκαendékaeleven
δώδεκαdṓdekatwelve
τρεῖς καὶ δέκαtreîs kaì dékathirteen (3 and 10)
πέντε καὶ δέκαpénte kaì dékafifteen
ἐννὲ καὶ δέκαennè kaì dékanineteen

From 13 to 19, Greek speaks the formula out loud: "units + καὶ + δέκα". (There is a fused written form τρεισκαίδεκα – found in later prose and poetry; being able to read it is enough.) Note the accent: ἐννέα keeps its acute at the end of a clause, but lowers it to a grave before καὶ: ἐννὲ καὶ δέκα – exactly the accent rule of Lesson 2.

The tens are a family with the shared face -κόντα:

And 21–99 are built with exactly the pattern of 13–19, but with the position reversed compared to Vietnamese: "units + καὶ + tens" – "five and twenty" = 25:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
εἷς καὶ εἴκοσιheîs kaì eíkositwenty-one (1 and 20)
ἑπτὰ καὶ εἴκοσιheptà kaì eíkositwenty-seven

Latin corner – two cultures, two arithmetics: for 18 Greek says ὀκτὼ καὶ δέκα "eight plus ten" (additive); Latin says duodēvīgintī "two from twenty" (subtractive!). For 19: ἐννὲ καὶ δέκα vs ūndēvīgintī. The same Mediterranean, two different ways of "counting on fingers" – Latin subtracts, Greek adds. If you study both languages, this is the classic example of "related languages does not mean identical thinking".

A dialect note: Attic uses τέτταρες "four"; Koine (the Bible) uses the form τέσσαρες – the same word, regionally separated. You will meet both in reading; the ending system and the usage do not change.

8. Numerals 100–999

The hundreds – and here is a learner's favorite: ἑκατόν "one hundred" stands still, but every other round hundred (200–900) declines like a three-termination o-adjective:

Note the 250 example: διακόσιαι stands in the feminine – because "the hundreds decline" is the same as saying "the hundreds must copy the gender of the noun they count". One rule for every declinable numeral: gender + case follow the counted noun, just as attributive adjectives always do (Lesson 1).

Looking ahead to 1000: our program stops at 999, but so that you don't get "stuck" reading Plutarch: "a thousand" is χίλιοι, -αι, -α (declining like the hundreds); "9,000" = ἐννὲ χιλιάδες. The reading below uses it – watch a new word in its natural habitat!

9. Numerals in the sentence: declinable vs indeclinable

The summary of "who declines, who stands still" – the most important table of the numeral section; learn the right-hand column by heart:

NumberDeclines?Consequence in the sentence
1 εἷς, μία, ἕνall cases, 3 gendersstands like an attributive adjective: εἷς κλῆρος, μία μερίς, ἑνὸς κλήρου
2 δύοindeclinableone form for every case; the counted noun is always plural: δύο βασιλεῖς
3, 4 τρεῖς, τέτταρεςdeclines (m./f. vs n.)gender follows the noun: τρία τέκνα (neuter)
5–10indeclinablea single form: ἑπτὰ παῖδες
11–19indeclinableδώδεκα μερίδας (accusative on the noun, the numeral unchanged)
20–90 -κόνταindeclinableπεντήκοντα ἰχθύας
100 ἑκατόνindeclinableἑκατὸν ἄστη – stands before the noun like every numeral
200–900declines like an o-adjectivegender + case follow the noun: διακόσιαι μερίδες (f.), διακόσια ἄστη (n.)

Three rules for using numerals in the sentence:

  1. Position: the numeral stands before the noun, with no article in between (unlike ordinary adjectives): τρεῖς βασιλεῖς "three kings". An article is also possible: οἱ δύο βασιλεῖς "the (two known) kings" – the two kings of Sparta in the reading!
  2. The number 1 takes the singular, every other number the plural of the counted noun – exactly the Vietnamese intuition.
  3. The "cross-check": if a declinable numeral's gender/case doesn't match its noun → wrong. If an indeclinable numeral and you "declined" it after the noun → wrong. A free one-second self-test in your head.

Ordinal numerals – "first, second, third…" (πρῶτος, δεύτερος, τρίτος…): ordinary o-adjectives, morphologically nothing special; the program will drill them when needed. Today is only about counting: the cardinals.

10. Reading: The lawgiver of Sparta (simplified from Plutarch)

Plutarch wrote the biography of Λυκοῦργος – the legendary lawgiver of Sparta, who divided the land into equal plots for the citizens. The passage below was rewritten by the project at Lesson 1–8 level (mainly present tenses + numerals + the third declension). Every sentence is dissected with exactly the toolkit you have accumulated. Unlearned words are glossed in gray parentheses on the spot.

Πλούταρχος γράφει περὶ τοῦ Λυκούργου· ὁ Λυκοῦργος ἐν Σπάρτῃ νόμους ποιεῖ καὶ τὴν γῆν τοῖς πολίταις μερίζει. (Πλούταρχος = Plutarch; Λυκοῦργος = Lycurgus; Σπάρτη = Sparta; ὁ νόμος = law; μερίζω = divide; ἡ γῆ = land)

  • Sentence 1, dissected: γράφει – present 3rd person of γράφω "write" (Lesson 1); περὶ + gen. "about" (Lesson 1); τοῦ Λυκούργου – article + genitive singular: a proper name declines like any noun; νόμους – accusative plural (o-declension) as the object of ποιεῖ; τὴν γῆν – accusative singular; τοῖς πολίταις – dative plural of ὁ πολίτης (Lesson 7, -ης pattern): "for the citizens"; μερίζει – present 3rd person. Two verbs share one subject ὁ Λυκοῦργος, joined by καὶ.
  • Translation of sentence 1: "Plutarch writes about Lycurgus: Lycurgus in Sparta makes laws and divides the land among the citizens."

τὴν γῆν εἰς ἐννὲ χιλιάδας κλήρους μερίζει· ἑκάστῳ γὰρ πολίτῃ εἷς κλῆρος ἐστίν. (εἰς + accusative = "into"; ἐννὲ χιλιάδες = 9000; ὁ κλῆρος = plot of land; ἕκαστος = each)

  • Sentence 2, dissected: εἰς is a preposition always taking the accusative (Lesson 2, direction "into"); ἐννὲ χιλιάδας κλήρους – the number 9 (indeclinable, grave before the next word!) + χιλιάδας accusative plural + κλήρους accusative plural of ὁ κλῆρος (third declension, s stem like γένος: acc pl. -ους) – two nouns stacked as the object of εἰς; ἑκάστῳ πολίτῃ – dative singular (Lesson 7: τῷ πολίτῃ); εἷς κλῆρος ἐστίν – the masculine number 1 + nominative; γάρ "for" in second position (Lesson 1). The structure "dative + ἐστίν" = dative of possession (Lesson 1): "to each citizen there is one plot".
  • Translation of sentence 2: "He divides the land into nine thousand plots; for to each citizen there is one plot."

ὁ δὲ νόμος λέγει· ὦ πολῖται, μὴ τὰ χρήματα φιλεῖτε· τὰ δὲ ὅπλα θεραπεύετε. (τὸ χρῆμα, pl. χρήματα = money, property; τὸ ὅπλον, pl. ὅπλα = weapon)

  • Sentence 3, dissected: ὁ δὲ νόμος λέγει – "and the law says" (δὲ: a connective particle in second position, Lesson 1); ὦ πολῖται – vocative plural (Lesson 2: ὦ + voc.); μὴ … φιλεῖτε – a prohibition (Lesson 7): μή + 2nd-person plural imperative of φιλέω (Lesson 2); τὰ χρήματα – neuter plural (Lesson 3), object; τὰ δὲ ὅπλα θεραπεύετε – implicit contrast with the previous clause: "but the weapons, honor", θεραπεύετε a 2nd-person plural imperative (Lesson 4).
  • Translation of sentence 3: "And the law says: O citizens, do not love possessions; but the weapons, honor."

τοὺς δὲ παῖδας ἐν τῇ αὐλῇ παιδεύουσιν· ἑπτὰ παῖδες σὺν τοῖς ὅπλοις τρέχουσι, ἐννὲ καὶ εἴκοσι δὲ παῖδες ἐν τῷ ἀγῶνι εἰσίν. (ὁ παῖς, pl. παῖδες = boy; ἡ αὐλή = farmyard; τρέχω = run; ὁ ἀγών = contest; σὺν + dative = together with)

  • Sentence 4, dissected: τοὺς παῖδας – accusative plural of ὁ παῖς (third declension, dental stem παιδ-: the stem joins the ending directly – no contraction, no loss, the "quietest" line of the third declension); παιδεύουσιν – present 3rd person plural (Lesson 2) with movable ν before a vowel; ἑπτὰ παῖδες – the indeclinable 7 + nominative plural; ἐννὲ καὶ εἴκοσι δὲ παῖδες – 29 by the "units + καὶ + tens" pattern of section 7, with δὲ (connective particle, second position) slipped right after the numeral; ἐν τῷ ἀγῶνι – dative singular, the article τῷ ruling; εἰσίν – 3rd person plural of εἶναι (Lesson 6).
  • Translation of sentence 4: "And the boys are educated in the farmyard: seven boys run with the weapons, and twenty-nine boys are present at the contest."

ἐν Σπάρτῃ δύο βασιλεῖς εἰσίν· οἱ δὲ δύο βασιλεῖς τοὺς νόμους θεραπεύουσιν. (δύο = two, indeclinable)

  • Sentence 5, dissected: δύο βασιλεῖς – the indeclinable number 2 + nominative plural of βασιλεύς (section 2 of this very lesson!); εἰσίν – "there are"; οἱ δὲ δύο βασιλεῖς – this time with the article οἱ: "the (aforesaid) two kings" – the numeral stands between article and noun like an attributive; τοὺς νόμους θεραπεύουσιν – accusative plural + plural verb: a plural object never drags the verb into the plural (only the subject decides the verb's number – and here the subject is plural, so the verb is plural).
  • Translation of sentence 5: "In Sparta there are two kings; and the two kings honor the laws."

The real background: Plutarch, Lycurgus 8: the plan of 9,000 plots (for the Spartan citizens) and 30,000 (for the surrounding population); the two kings are a Spartan constitutional specialty, as is the military education of children from an early age. The whole original is public domain – continue on Perseus or Theoi (see the footer).

13. Exercises

Answers appear only when printing (Ctrl+P → Save as PDF) – work through everything first.

Exercise 1 – Build the paradigms

Build the full paradigms of ὁ ἰχθύς and τό ἄστυ by the bare stem + ending method (no peeking at the tables in the lesson!).

Answers 1

ἰχθύς, ἰχθύος, ἰχθύϊ, ἰχθύν, ἰχθύ – ἰχθύες, ἰχθύων, ἰχθύσι(ν), ἰχθύας; ἄστυ, ἄστεος, ἄστει, ἄστυ, ἄστυ – ἄστεα, ἄστεων, ἄστεσι(ν), ἄστεα. Neuter: nominative = accusative in both numbers; plural ending -α. If you wrote ἰχθύι without the diaeresis, or ἄστυι instead of ἄστει – review sections 3 and 4.

Exercise 2 – The article rules

Determine case + number of each form, then write the correct accompanying article:

  1. βασιλεῖ
  2. γένεσι(ν)
  3. ἄστεα
  4. ἰχθύος
  5. κλήρους
  6. μερίδας

Answers 2

  1. τῷ βασιλεῖ – dative singular (the ending -ῖ is the ε+ι contraction of an -ε- stem).
  2. τοῖς γένεσι(ν) – dative plural (ending -εσι(ν) + movable ν).
  3. τὰ ἄστεα – nominative/accusative plural (neuter: two cases, one form).
  4. τοῦ ἰχθύος – genitive singular.
  5. τοὺς κλήρους – accusative plural (s stem: acc pl. -ους).
  6. τὰς μερίδας – accusative plural (a-declension; ending -ας).

Exercise 3 – Write the numbers

Write in Greek (with transliteration): 1, 3, 4, 8, 12, 13, 20, 27, 100, 125, 250, 999. For each, also note: declinable or indeclinable?

Answers 3

εἷς, μία, ἕν (declines); τρεῖς/τρία (declines); τέτταρες/τέτταρα (declines); ὀκτώ (indeclinable); δώδεκα (indeclinable); τρεῖς καὶ δέκα (indeclinable); εἴκοσι (indeclinable); ἑπτὰ καὶ εἴκοσι (indeclinable); ἑκατόν (indeclinable); ἑκατὸν εἴκοσι πέντε (indeclinable); διακόσιαι πεντήκοντα (the hundreds decline – matching the counted noun's gender; here feminine); ἐννιακόσιοι ἐνενήκοντα ἐννέα (the hundreds decline; masculine).

Exercise 4 – εἷς or εἰς?

Choose and justify with the test "counting vs going into":

  1. ἑκάστῳ πολίτῃ ___ κλῆρος ἐστίν.
  2. ὁ Λυκοῦργος τὴν γῆν ___ ἐννὲ χιλιάδας κλήρους μερίζει.
  3. ___ βασιλεύς ἐν τῇ πόλει ἐστίν.
  4. τρέχω ___ τὴν αὐλήν.

Answers 4

  1. εἷς – countable: "to each citizen there is one plot" (masculine, matching κλῆρος).
  2. εἰς – preposition + accusative: "divides the land into 9,000 plots".
  3. εἷς – "there is one king in the city" (masculine).
  4. εἰς – "I run into the farmyard" (εἰς + accusative).

Exercise 5 – Analyze + translate

Translate, stating: what the article ruled, which stem, and whether the numeral declines:

  1. ἐν τῷ ἄστει τριακόσιοι πολῖται εἰσίν.
  2. τοὺς δύο βασιλέας θεραπεύομεν.
  3. ἑπτὰ ἰχθύες ἐν τῷ ὕδατι εἰσίν. (τὸ ὕδωρ, τοῦ ὕδατος = water)

Answers 5

  1. "In the town there are three hundred citizens." – τῷ dative singular; ἄστει the ε+ι contraction; τριακόσιοι declinable (hundreds), masculine matching πολῖται; plural subject → εἰσίν.
  2. "We honor the two kings." – τοὺς accusative plural (the article rules); δύο indeclinable; βασιλέας accusative plural of βασιλεύς (section 2: -έας with two syllables – exactly the form so easily confused with the nom pl. βασιλεῖς!).
  3. "Seven fish are in the water." – ἑπτὰ indeclinable + nominative plural (the plural verb follows the subject); ἰχθύες nom pl. (section 3); τῷ ὕδατι dative singular of τὸ ὕδωρ (third declension, stem ὑδατ- – the same -ματ family as Lesson 7's σῶμα, σώματι!).

Exercise 6 – Translate the summary passage (Lessons 4–8)

Translate, naming at each step which tool from which lesson you used:

ἐν τῷ ἄστει ἑκατὸν καὶ εἴκοσι πολῖται εἰσίν· ὁ δὲ βασιλεὺς τοῖς πολίταις λέγει· ὦ φίλοι, μὴ τὰ χρήματα φιλεῖτε, τὰς δὲ μερίδας τῆς γῆς θεραπεύετε· ἑκάστῳ γὰρ ἑνὶ πολίτῃ μία μερὶς ἐστίν. (ὁ φίλος = friend; θεραπεύετε = 2nd-person plural imperative)

Answers 6

"There are one hundred and twenty citizens in the town; and the king says to the citizens: O friends, do not love money, but respect the shares of the land; for to each single citizen there is one share."

Notes on each construction: (1) ἑκατὸν καὶ εἴκοσι – 120 = hundred + καὶ + tens (section 8; ἑκατόν takes the grave before καὶ); ἑκατόν indeclinable, πολῖται plural. (2) ὁ δὲ βασιλεὺς – nominative singular, the article ruling; τοῖς πολίταις – dative plural (Lesson 7) with a verb of speaking (the hearer stands in the dative – Lesson 2). (3) μὴ … φιλεῖτε – a prohibition (Lesson 7) with a 2nd-person plural imperative (Lesson 4); τὰ χρήματα – neuter plural (Lesson 3). (4) τὰς δὲ μερίδας – accusative plural, the article τὰς ruling (feminine); τῆς γῆς – genitive singular (Lesson 1). (5) ἑκάστῳ ἑνὶ πολίτῃ μία μερὶς ἐστίν – two datives singular stacked (ἑνί declining to match πολίτῃ!) + the dative of possession (Lesson 1): "to each single citizen there is one share"; μία feminine matching μερίς (section 9: gender follows the counted noun).
