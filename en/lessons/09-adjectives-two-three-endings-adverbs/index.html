Goals for this lesson: after working through it you can (1) recognize and decline two-ending adjectives – masculine and feminine share one form, the neuter stands apart – in both the s-stem pattern (ἀληθής, ἀληθές) and the ν-stem pattern (εὐδαίμων, εὐδαίμον), and spot the two-ending branch of the o-/a-group compounds (ἄδικος, ἄδικον); (2) recognize three-ending adjectives with a separate feminine form (μέλας, μέλαινα, μέλαν); (3) build adverbs yourself from every adjective you know, by the neuter + -ως rule; (4) name "the truth", "the good", "happiness" with the substantivization trick you learned in Lesson 5.

1. Overview: third-declension adjectives and the two ending types

Since Lesson 1 you have been declining the "standard" type of adjective with confidence: σοφός, σοφή, σοφόν "wise" – masculine by the o-pattern, feminine by the a-pattern, neuter by the o-pattern. Look closely at that trio: three genders, three passports, three different endings. Grammar calls this type a three-ending adjective of the 1/2-declension family – and most adjectives you have learned so far belong here.

Today we move to the family of adjectives with third-declension stems. Good news: you already hold every tool from the last two lessons, because a third-declension adjective works exactly like a third-declension noun – the only difference is that it must "split" into three genders. Third-declension adjectives come in two types, according to the number of nominative forms listed in the dictionary:

Read closely and you can see how the counting works: a two-ending adjective lists two forms – masculine and feminine share one form in every case, the neuter is listed separately; a three-ending adjective lists three forms – the feminine alone has a look of her own (declined by the a-pattern). The dictionary always tells you which type you are holding: just count the forms at the head of the entry.

The pattern behind the form count: a two-ending third-declension adjective is as "compact" as possible – masculine and feminine already share one ending system, so no separation is needed; only the neuter is always marked separately, because the nominative = accusative rule (Lesson 1) forces it to be different. A three-ending adjective additionally "borrows" the whole a-pattern for the feminine. You already own all three pieces: o-pattern + a-pattern (Lessons 1–2), third declension (Lessons 7–8) – Lesson 9 just puts them together.

Relatives outside the family: the two-ending branch of the o-/a group (ἄδικος, ἄδικον)

The "two-ending" template is not a specialty of the third declension: the 1/2 group also has a two-ending branch, produced mainly by compounding – adding the negative prefix ἀ- (alpha-privativum; written ἀν- before a vowel), some other prefix, or the suffix -μος. The mechanism is exactly the same as in the two patterns above: masculine and feminine share one form in every case, the neuter stands apart – the only difference is that the shared form declines by the o-pattern, not by the third declension. Compare the two cousins right away: δίκαιος, δικαία, δίκαιον "just" (three-ending) ↔ ἄδικος, ἄδικον "unjust" (two-ending): the prefix ἀ- both reverses the meaning and "sucks away" the separate feminine form.

The same way of thinking runs through several languages you know: Latin in-iūstus = English un-just = German un-gerecht = ἄδικος (Greek) = "unjust". When you meet an unfamiliar compound carrying ἀ-: split off the prefix, look up the root, put them back together – the meaning opens by itself.

2. Two-ending adjectives, s-stem pattern: ἀληθής, ἀληθές

ἀληθής "true" is one of the most frequent words in all of Greek literature (Plato spent his whole life asking: τί ἐστι τὸ ἀληθές; "what is the true thing?"). Open the stem with the familiar key: genitive ἀληθοῦς → bare stem ἀληθεσ-. That's right – an s stem, a relative of γένος, γενεσ- (Lesson 8): the final s of the stem either drops out or forces the vowel to contract (contraction).

Dissecting the table line by line: (1) nominative singular ἀληθής = stem ἀληθεσ- loses its s, ε lengthens to η; (2) genitive ἀληθοῦς = ἀληθεσ-ος: s drops between two vowels, then ε+ο contracts to ου – precisely the contraction you saw in γένους in Lesson 8; (3) dative ἀληθεῖ = s drops, ε+ι contracts to ει, the iota subscript is written out; (4) accusative singular ἀληθῆ and plural ἀληθεῖς follow the πόλεις pattern from Lesson 7. The whole table holds just one stem, ἀληθε(σ)-, wearing endings you have owned since the last two lessons.

Watch out: don't confuse ἀληθεῖ (dative singular – with iota subscript) with ἀληθεῖς (nominative/accusative plural – without iota subscript). Self-check: in this table the iota subscript appears only in the dative singular; see the "dot underneath" → you are holding a dative singular. The article confirms immediately: τῷ ἀληθεῖ vs οἱ ἀληθεῖς.

Latin corner: two-ending adjectives are not a Greek specialty: Latin ingēns "huge" also gives masculine and feminine one shared set of endings (ingent-), with only the neuter differing (ingens m/f, ingens n… distinct only in the plural ingentia). Greek ἀληθής and Latin ingēns are the same strategy: "merge masculine and feminine, keep the neuter separate".

3. Two-ending adjectives, ν-stem pattern: εὐδαίμων, εὐδαίμον

εὐδαίμων "happy" (literally: "having a good divine spirit" – εὐ- "good" + δαίμων "divine spirit"; a relative of βασιλεύς in that its stem ends in vowel + ν). The genitive εὐδαίμονος opens the stem εὐδαιμον- – a vowel stem (Lesson 8!) so the paradigm runs smoothly, no contractions, no drops:

Compare with βασιλεύς (Lesson 8): the same "vowel-stem spirit" – the plural masculine/feminine endings -ες / -ων / -σι(ν) / -ας attach straight to the stem without any negotiation. The only difference: masculine and feminine of εὐδαίμων are absolutely identical in every case, so the table really holds just one masculine/feminine half plus one neuter half – that is the "two endings" of the name.

A second representative of this pattern, extremely common in ethical prose: σώφρων, σῶφρον "moderate, self-controlled" (literally: "keeping a sound mind" – σῶς "sound" + φρήν "mind"). It declines exactly like εὐδαίμων: σώφρονος, σώφρονι, σώφρονα… Wherever you meet it, recognize the family: nominative -ων / -ον, genitive -ονος is the "leg" of the whole species. Note it now for later: the comparative forms (βελτίων, βέλτιον "better"…) also belong to this pattern – reviewed in the comparison lesson.

Watch out: the pair εὐδαίμων (m./f.) vs εὐδαίμον (neuter) differs only in the final letter ω vs ο – one long/short letter. In a sentence the article always saves you: ὁ εὐδαίμων ἄνθρωπος (the happy man) vs τὸ εὐδαίμον (happiness as such). When writing by hand, draw ω long enough – lose the length and you lose the gender.

4. Three-ending adjectives: μέλας, μέλαινα, μέλαν

The second type has fewer words but appears in every text: the three-ending adjective – the feminine has a form of her own. The standard representative: μέλας, μέλαινα, μέλαν "black" (stem μελαν- – the relative of "melanin" that makes both skin and ink black). Masculine and neuter decline by the third declension; only the feminine borrows the a-pattern – the same architecture as σοφός, σοφή, σοφόν from Lesson 1, with a different ending system for the masculine/neuter.

The plural (one line per gender): masculine μέλανες, μελάνων, μέλασι(ν), μέλανᾱς; feminine μέλαιναι, μελαινῶν, μελαίναις, μελαίνᾱς; neuter μέλανα, μελάνων, μέλασι(ν), μέλανα.

Three points worth dissecting: (1) nominative masculine μέλας: stem μελαν- + s, ν drops before s and the vowel lengthens (ᾱ long, written without any mark) – the same "lengthening trade" (compensatory lengthening) you met in γένος; (2) feminine μέλαινα: underlying μελαν-ια, the iota subscript "pops up" into a full vowel inside the diphthong – the same mechanism you saw with a-pattern nouns; (3) dative plural μέλασι(ν) uses the shortened stem μελᾱ- – exactly the "stem shortening" of γένεσι(ν) in Lesson 8.

How to learn this type: you only need to memorize the nominative trio μέλας, μέλαινα, μέλαν + two principles: masculine/neuter decline by the third declension as in Lessons 7–8, the feminine by the a-pattern as in Lessons 1–2. In addition, the -ύς adjectives such as ἡδύς, ἡδεῖα, ἡδύ "sweet" also belong to the three-ending type (masculine/neuter on third-declension stems, feminine a-pattern) – you will meet them in poetry and in a later lesson.

Reading strategy for real texts, one level up:

  1. The article remains the referee: ὁ ἀληθής λόγος, τὴν ἀληθῆ ψυχήν (accusative! – the two words "look alike" but τήν rules an object), τὸ εὐδαίμον ἄστυ, τὴν μελαίνην θάλασσαν (three-ending: the feminine follows the a-pattern). Never seen the adjective before? Article + ending still assemble the whole picture.
  2. An attributive adjective "matches" its noun in all three indices – gender, case, number (Lesson 1). One index off and the phrase is wrong; in written Greek that is a built-in proofreading check.
  3. The neuter of the -ων pattern looks like an o-pattern noun (εὐδαίμον like δῶρον) – but only in nominative/accusative singular; the remaining cases (εὐδαίμονος, εὐδαίμονι) reveal the third declension. Don't jump to conclusions from a single ending.
  4. The endings -ης/-ες and -ων/-ον are just the "door"; the stem is the house. Meet an unfamiliar word: look up its genitive in the dictionary: σαφής, σαφοῦς → s stem; ἄγνωμων, ἀγνώμονος → ν stem. Whether to decline like ἀληθής or like εὐδαίμων is decided on the spot.

6. Forming adverbs: the neuter + -ως rule

Lesson 3 gave you the concept of the adverb – a word that describes how an action happens, always indeclinable. Today you learn to build adverbs from adjectives yourself, with a rule so elegant that learning it once serves you for life:

The golden rule: take the stem of the neuter form and attach -ως. "An adjective speaks about a noun; an adverb speaks about an action" – and an action has no gender, so the adverb does not decline. The Latin relatives will show you how far back this rule goes (see the box at the end of this section).

Read the "→" column closely and you see two sub-patterns: (1) o/a-pattern adjectives give a neatly contracted adverb in -ῶς (neuter σοφόν + ς, the vowel contracts: σοφῶς); (2) third-declension adjectives attach -ως directly to the stem: ἀληθε-ως → ἀληθῶς (contracting as usual), while εὐδαιμόν-ως, σωφρόν-ως attach without change. Both roads end in -ως – that is the "nameplate" by which you recognize a Greek adverb.

Watch out – the famous rebel: εὖ "well". The adverb "well" follows no rule at all – it is a law unto itself, carrying a smooth breathing and a circumflex: εὖ (transliterated eû, no /h/ – the breathing-mark convention of the terminology page again). Extremely common: εὖ λέγειν "to speak well", εὖ πράττειν "to do well / to prosper", εὖ γένοιο "may all go well for you"… and it is the prefix εὐ- inside εὐδαίμων itself. Remember: the -ως rule covers hundreds of words; the single word for "well" is εὖ.

Latin corner: Latin forms adverbs with the same philosophy: take the neuter form + an ending: cārus → cārē "dearly", vērus → vērē "truly". Greek chose -ως, Latin chose -ē – two distant relatives of one and the same Indo-European idea: "take the neuter form, turn it into an adverb". And Latin too has its own irregular pet word like εὖ: bene "well".

7. Substantivization review: "truth" is an adjective

Lesson 5 taught you the magic trick: article + adjective = noun (the substantivized adjective): τὸ ἀγαθόν "the good", ὁ ἐχθρός "the enemy". Today new adjectives move into the stall and the magic keeps running – and this is how the Greeks said their biggest philosophical concepts:

GreekTransliterationEnglish
ὁ ἀληθής / οἱ ἀληθεῖςho alēthḗsthe true one / the truthful ones
τὸ ἀληθέςtò alēthésthe true (thing), the truth
τὸ εὐδαίμονtò eudaímonhappiness (the happy thing)
τὰ σώφροναtà sṓphronathings done with moderation
τὰ μέλαναtà mélanathe black things

Three things to remember from the table: (1) the gender follows the meaning – a "person" is masculine, a "thing/matter" is neuter: ὁ ἀληθής is the truthful person, τὸ ἀληθές is the truth; (2) a neuter plural like τὰ σώφρονα "things done with moderation" expresses the concept of a "collection of actions" – exactly the neuter-plural rule from Lesson 3; (3) substantivization keeps the adjective's own paradigm: τοῦ ἀληθοῦς "of the truth" is still a perfectly ordinary third-declension genitive.

Why this trick deserves your respect: Plato builds his whole philosophical edifice on τὸ ἀγαθόν, τὸ ἀληθές, τὸ δίκαιον – "the good, the truth, the just" are all adjectives wearing an article. When you read philosophical prose, this is not an exotic structure: it is Lesson 5 and Lesson 9 shaking hands.

Adjectives also choose their case (adjective with case complement): "case government" is not the monopoly of prepositions (Lesson 1: ἐν + dative) or verbs (Lesson 4: κατηγορέω + genitive). Some adjectives also demand a particular case next to them, like a "miniature object": κοινός, κοινή, κοινόν (koinós, koinḗ, koinón) "common" + genitive or dative. Example: Ἄρα ἡ γῆ τῶν θεῶν κοινὴ ἐστιν; "So the land is common to the gods?" – τῶν θεῶν is a genitive that does not modify the noun γῆ, but the adjective κοινή standing next to the verb ἐστιν. The case of a translation need not match the original: English says common to someone, German uses the dative (jemandem gemeinsam) – so never reconstruct the original case from a translation.

Self-check by asking a question: ask the way a teacher quizzes a student: κοινὴ is common to whom? The answer carries exactly the case the adjective governs. It is the same question you already used for verbs (Lesson 4) and prepositions (Lesson 1): the "case-governing" trio – verb, preposition, adjective – shares one pattern, only the contracting party changes. Remember: each word's case must be learned or looked up individually, exactly like case-governing verbs.

8. Reading: The small king with a great soul (simplified from Xenophon)

Xenophon wrote the biography of Ἀγησίλαος – the Spartan king he served and admired, famous for being "small in body but great in soul" and for honoring the laws above royal power. The passage below was rewritten by the project at Lesson 1–9 level. Unlearned words are collected in a separate gloss block right after the reading.

1 Ξενοφῶν γράφει περὶ τοῦ Ἀγησιλάου· ὁ Ἀγησίλαος ἐν Σπάρτῃ βασιλεύς ἐστιν καὶ στρατηγός. 2 τὸ μὲν σῶμα μικρόν ἐστιν, ἡ δὲ ψυχὴ μεγάλη ἐστιν. 3 ὁ δὲ Ἀγησίλαος τοὺς νόμους θεραπεύει καὶ ἀληθῆ λέγει· οἱ γὰρ πολῖται αὐτοῦ εὐδαίμονές εἰσιν. 4 ὁ Ἀγησίλαος οὐ μόνος ἐστιν· ἀλλὰ καὶ οἱ ἄνθρωποι αὐτὸν ἀληθῶς ἀγαπῶσιν. 5 λέγουσιν οἱ πολῖται· ὦ βασιλεῦ, σὺ εὖ πράττεις· τὸ γὰρ εὐδαίμον ἐν τῇ πόλει ἐστίν.

Ksenóphōn gráphei perì toû Agesiláou· ho Agesílaos en Spártēi basileús estin kaì stratēgós. tò mèn sôma mikrón estin, hē dè psychḕ megálē estin. ho dè Agesílaos toùs nómous therapeúei kaì alēthê légei· hoi gàr polîtai autoû eudaimónes eisín. ho Agesílaos ou mónos estin· allà kaì hoi ánthrōpoi autòn alēthṓs agapôsin. légousin hoi polîtai· ô basileû, sỳ eû prátteis· tò gàr eúdaimon en têi pólei estín.

Notes:

  • Ξενοφῶν = Xenophon; Ἀγησίλαος = Agesilaus; ὁ στρατηγός = general
  • ἡ ψυχή = soul; μέγας, μεγάλη, μέγα = big – see the note below
  • θεραπεύω = to honor, revere; ὁ πολίτης = citizen
  • μόνος = alone, only; ἀγαπῶσιν = 3rd person plural of ἀγαπάω "love" – a contract verb, studied in a later lesson
  • εὖ = well, the irregular adverb; πράττω = do, accomplish

Three reading steps: work through them in order before scrolling down to the translation; the answers appear only in print, for checking. The text is numbered by sentence: C4 means sentence 4.

  1. Step 1, Formal observation: circle every adjective in the passage and note its type: two-ending (the -ης/-ες or the -ων/-ον patterns)? three-ending? or irregular? Then circle every adverb: which one ends in -ως, which one is irregular? Finally find one substantivization (neuter article + neuter adjective) and say in which case it stands.
  2. Step 2, Meaning and structure: build the "who does what" table with four rows: Xenophon, Agesilaus, the citizens, the speakers in sentence 5. Which contrast pair does sentence 2 use to set its two halves side by side? Which negation pair does sentence 4 use? In sentence 3, why does εὐδαίμονές carry an extra acute accent on its final syllable (compare the dictionary form εὐδαίμονες)?
  3. Step 3, Discussion and connection: is the portrait "small in body, great in soul" genuine praise or a political tool of Xenophon, who once served Agesilaus? Sentence 5 defines happiness as something located "in the city": in your view, how does the state in this story grant happiness to its citizens, and what would a leader today mean by "happiness lies inside society"?

English (free translation)

Dissecting each sentence (checking with the tools you have already learned):

  • Sentence 1, dissected: γράφει – present 3rd person of γράφω "write" (Lesson 1); περὶ + gen. "about" (Lesson 1); τοῦ Ἀγησιλάου – article + genitive singular; a proper name declines too (o-pattern, gen -ου); βασιλεύς – nominative singular (Lesson 8, -εύς pattern) as the predicate; ἐστιν – an enclitic "leaning" on βασιλεύς, with movable ν before a vowel; the two nouns joined by καὶ form a double predicate: "is king and is general".
  • Sentence 2, dissected: μὲν … δὲ – the contrast pair (Lesson 1): "the one… the other"; τὸ σῶμα – neuter singular subject, the article τό rules; μικρόν – neuter predicate adjective (Lesson 1: match gender-case-number); ἡ ψυχὴ μεγάλη – nominative feminine + attributive in the article-sandwich position (Lesson 1). The adjective "big" μέγας, μεγάλη, μέγα is a famous irregular: the masculine "strays" only in nominative/accusative singular (where it looks third-declension), but from the genitive on (μεγάλου, μεγάλῃ, μεγάλον) it behaves obediently by the o/a-pattern. Best approach: copy the whole trio into your notebook like a proper name. Recognition tip: nominative masculine -ας but genitive -ου = irregular, not third declension.
  • Sentence 3, dissected: τοὺς νόμους – accusative plural (already met in the Lesson 8 reading); ἀληθῆ λέγει – accusative plural of a two-ending adjective as a conceptual object, "says true things" (like English "speak the truth"); αὐτοῦ – genitive of the 3rd person pronoun "his" (Lesson 4); εὐδαίμονές εἰσιν – nominative plural masculine (the -ων pattern, Lesson 9!), with a nice detail: εἰσί(ν) is an enclitic, so it "pushes" an extra acute accent onto the final syllable of the preceding word → εὐδαίμονές (acute on the last syllable) – exactly the enclitic rule from Lessons 1–2; the article οἱ rules: nominative plural masculine.
  • Sentence 4, dissected: οὐ … ἀλλά – the "not… but" pair (Lesson 4); οὐ μόνος ἐστιν – only one negated half: "is not alone" (μόνος an o/a-pattern predicate adjective); ἀλλὰ καὶ – "but… also"; αὐτόν – 3rd person singular pronoun in the accusative (Lesson 4); ἀληθῶς – an adverb! (section 6: neuter stem + -ως): "love truly"; the indeclinable adverb sits compactly before the verb.
  • Sentence 5, dissected: λέγουσιν οἱ πολῖται – verb before subject: narrative style (already met in the Lesson 7 reading); ὦ βασιλεῦ – vocative of βασιλεύς (Lesson 8: voc βασιλεῦ – exactly the table you learned); εὖ πράττεις – irregular adverb + 2nd person verb (Lesson 4): "you do well / you prosper"; τὸ εὐδαίμον – substantivization (section 7): neuter article + neuter adjective = "the happy thing, happiness"; ἐν τῇ πόλει – dative singular (Lesson 1); ἐστίν – clause-final, with movable ν before the sentence break.

General grammar points to remember: the passage is a "demo" of the lesson itself: the two-ending adjective appears in full: εὐδαίμων (nominative plural masculine) and ἀληθῆ (accusative plural), the irregular three-ending μέγας, μεγάλη, μέγα in the feminine nominative μεγάλη, adverbs born from the neuter stem + -ως (ἀληθῶς) beside the irregular εὖ, and the substantivization τὸ εὐδαίμον for an abstract concept. One bonus gift: εὐδαίμονές εἰσιν shows the enclitic εἰσί(ν) pressing its accent onto the final syllable of the preceding word, exactly the rule learned back in Lesson 1.

The real context: Xenophon, Agesilaos – a "biographical encomium" for the Spartan king Xenophon once served; "small in body, great in soul" is the classic motif about Agesilaus, and respect for Sparta's laws is its running theme. The original text is public domain – continue reading on Perseus (see the footer). The lesson's second reference (Lukian, Deorum dialogi 24) will be used in the practice section when reading a dialogue of the gods.

Answers to the three reading steps (print to check)

Step 1, Formal observation. Adjectives in the passage: μικρόν (C2, three-ending o/a-type, neuter predicate), μεγάλη (C2, the irregular three-ending μέγας, feminine nominative), ἀληθῆ (C3, two-ending s-stem ἀληθής, accusative plural), εὐδαίμονές (C3, two-ending ν-stem εὐδαίμων, nominative plural masculine), μόνος (C4, o/a-type, masculine predicate), εὐδαίμον (C5, substantivized). Adverbs: ἀληθῶς (C4, neuter stem + -ως), εὖ (C5, irregular). Substantivization: τὸ εὐδαίμον (C5), nominative singular as the subject of ἐστίν.

Step 2, Meaning and structure. The four-row table: Xenophon writes about Agesilaus (γράφει περὶ); Agesilaus is king and general, honors the laws, speaks the truth (βασιλεύς ἐστιν, θεραπεύει, ἀληθῆ λέγει); the citizens are happy and love him (εὐδαίμονές εἰσιν, ἀγαπῶσιν); in sentence 5 the citizens themselves are the speakers, addressing the king in the vocative ὦ βασιλεῦ. Sentence 2 uses the pair μὲν … δὲ to contrast body and soul; sentence 4 uses the pair οὐ … ἀλλά to negate "alone" and assert everyone's love. In sentence 3, εὐδαίμονές carries the extra acute because εἰσί(ν) is an enclitic: it presses its accent onto the final syllable of the preceding word.

Step 3, a direction for discussion. Xenophon wrote a "biographical encomium" for the king he once served, so the story is both genuine praise and propaganda: the virtues listed (respect for the laws, truth-telling) are the virtues of a servant, not of an absolute ruler. Sentence 5 defines happiness as a joint project "in the city": the citizens' happiness comes not from wealth but from the king keeping the laws. Today, a claim that "happiness lies inside society" gets asked back: who builds that society, and who counts as its citizen.

Exercise 1 – Declension tables

Build the full paradigms of ἀληθής (m./f. + neuter, both numbers) and εὐδαίμων (m./f. + neuter, both numbers), then the singular of μέλας in all three genders, using the bare-stem + endings method.

Answers 1

ἀληθής, ἀληθοῦς, ἀληθεῖ, ἀληθῆ, ἀληθές – ἀληθεῖς, ἀληθῶν, ἀληθέσι(ν), ἀληθεῖς; neuter: ἀληθές, ἀληθοῦς, ἀληθεῖ, ἀληθές – ἀληθῆ, ἀληθῶν, ἀληθέσι(ν), ἀληθῆ. εὐδαίμων, εὐδαίμονος, εὐδαίμονι, εὐδαίμονα, εὐδαίμον – εὐδαίμονες, εὐδαιμόνων, εὐδαίμοσι(ν), εὐδαίμονας; neuter: εὐδαίμον, εὐδαίμονος, εὐδαίμονι, εὐδαίμον – εὐδαίμονα, εὐδαιμόνων, εὐδαίμοσι(ν), εὐδαίμονα. μέλας: masculine μέλας, μέλανος, μέλανι, μέλανᾱ; feminine μέλαινα, μελαίνης, μελαίνῃ, μέλαιναν; neuter μέλαν, μέλανος, μέλανι, μέλαν. If you wrote ἀληθές for the masculine accusative – look again: the m./f. accusative is ἀληθῆ (and acc pl ἀληθεῖς, no iota subscript).

Exercise 2 – The article decides

Determine the gender + case + number of each phrase, then say whether the adjective belongs to the two-ending or the three-ending type:

  1. τῷ σώφρονι ἀνθρώπῳ
  2. τὰ εὐδαίμονα ἄστη
  3. τὴν ἀληθῆ ψυχήν
  4. τοὺς στρατηγοὺς τοὺς ἀληθεῖς
  5. τὰς μελαίνᾱς ψυχάς
  6. ὦ εὐδαίμον ἄστυ

Answers 2

  1. Dative singular masculine; σώφρονι two-ending ν-stem pattern: "for the moderate man" (ἄνθρωπος, ἀνθρώπῳ dative – Lesson 7).
  2. Nominative/accusative plural neuter; εὐδαίμονα two-ending ν-stem pattern: "the happy towns" (ἄστεα – Lesson 8).
  3. Accusative singular feminine; ἀληθῆ two-ending s-stem pattern: "the truthful soul" (m./f. accusative of ἀληθής).
  4. Accusative plural masculine; ἀληθεῖς two-ending s-stem pattern, attributive with repeated article (Lesson 1): "the truthful generals".
  5. Accusative plural feminine; μελαίνᾱς three-ending type (feminine by the a-pattern): "the dark souls" (ψυχάς = acc pl of ψυχή).
  6. Vocative singular neuter; εὐδαίμον two-ending: "O happy town!"

Exercise 3 – Build the adverbs

Form the adverbs by the rule of section 6 (write the transliteration too): κακός, δίκαιος, ἀληθής, εὐδαίμων, σώφρων. Side question: which of these adverbs can never combine with an article?

Answers 3

κακῶς (kakôs), δικαίως (dikaíōs), ἀληθῶς (alēthôs), εὐδαιμόνως (eudaimónōs), σωφρόνως (sōphrónōs). Side answer: all of them – an adverb is indeclinable and has no gender/case/number, so no article can ever "attach" (the table in section 9 of Lesson 8).

Exercise 4 – Substantivization

Translate into Greek using substantivization (choose the right gender + number): "the good man", "the good", "the bad things", "the truth", "happiness", "the moderate ones".

Answers 4

ὁ ἀγαθός; τὸ ἀγαθόν; τὰ κακά; τὸ ἀληθές; τὸ εὐδαίμον; οἱ σώφρονες. Remember the rule behind it: "person" → masculine; "thing/matter" → neuter; a collection → neuter plural.

Exercise 5 – Analyze + translate

Translate, noting: what the article rules, which type the adjective belongs to, which word is the adverb:

  1. ὁ βασιλεὺς σωφρόνως τοὺς νόμους φυλάττει.
    (φυλάττω = guard)
  2. τὸ μέγα ἄστυ τοὺς πολίτας καλῶς σώζει.
  3. εὖ πράττετε, ὦ ἀληθεῖς πολῖται.

Answers 5

  1. "The king guards the laws with moderation." – ὁ βασιλεύς nominative singular (Lesson 8); σωφρόνως the adverb of a two-ending adjective (section 6); τοὺς νόμους accusative plural; φυλάττει present 3rd person.
  2. "The great town saves the citizens beautifully." – τὸ μέγα ἄστυ nominative singular neuter: the irregular adjective μέγα in attributive position (article + adjective + noun, Lesson 1) matches the neuter gender of ἄστυ (Lesson 8); neuter singular subject → the verb σώζει stays singular; καλῶς a neatly contracted adverb.
  3. "You do well, O truthful citizens." – εὖ the irregular adverb (section 6); πράττετε 2nd person plural; ὦ ἀληθεῖς πολῖται vocative plural (Lesson 2: ὦ + voc) with a two-ending attributive adjective.

Exercise 6 – Translate the cumulative passage (Lessons 4–9)

Translate, noting at each step which tool from which lesson is being used:

ἐν τῇ Σπάρτῃ δύο βασιλεῖς εἰσίν· ὁ δὲ Ἀγησίλαος οὐ μέγας τὸ σῶμα ἀλλὰ μέγας τὴν ψυχήν ἐστιν· σωφρόνως τε γὰρ τοὺς νόμους θεραπεύει καὶ ἀληθῆ λέγει. οἱ δὲ πολῖται αὐτὸν ἀγαπῶσιν καὶ λέγουσιν· εὖ πράττεις, ὦ βασιλεῦ· τὸ γὰρ εὐδαίμον ἐν τῇ πόλει ἐστίν.

Notes:

  • τε = and, standing after the first word, joining pairs "both…and"

Answers 6

"In Sparta there are two kings; and Agesilaus is not big in body but big in soul; for he both honors the laws with moderation and speaks the truth. The citizens love him and say: you do well, O king; for happiness is in the city."

Structure notes, step by step: (1) δύο βασιλεῖς – the indeclinable number 2 (Lesson 8) + nominative plural; εἰσίν 3rd person plural. (2) οὐ … ἀλλά – the negation-contrast pair (Lesson 4); μέγας τὸ σῶμα / μέγας τὴν ψυχήν – the irregular adjective with a "partitive" accusative (the double accusative already met in Lesson 2): "big in body / in soul". (3) σωφρόνως τε γὰρ – the adverb of a two-ending adjective (Lesson 9, section 6) + the paired conjunction te. (4) ἀληθῆ λέγει – accusative plural as a conceptual object, "speaks true things" (Lesson 9, reading sentence 3). (5) αὐτὸν ἀγαπῶσιν – 3rd person pronoun in the accusative (Lesson 4) + contract verb in the 3rd person plural (gloss in the reading). (6) εὖ πράττεις – irregular adverb (section 6) + 2nd person indicative (Lesson 2), not an imperative; ὦ βασιλεῦ vocative from the βασιλεύς table (Lesson 8). (7) τὸ εὐδαίμον – substantivized two-ending adjective: "happiness" (section 7); ἐν τῇ πόλει – dative singular (Lesson 1); ἐστίν – clause-final, movable ν before the sentence break.
